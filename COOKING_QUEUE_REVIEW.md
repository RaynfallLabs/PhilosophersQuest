### The chemistry-set label: what 'ultra-processed' means  (Whole vs processed, and your gut · T1-T5 · deep · q12)
**Scope:** The NOVA idea that sorts food by how much it's been PROCESSED, not by fat/sugar/salt. T1: the practical heuristic a kid can use first — the more ingredients you can't picture as a real thing, the further from whole food (an apple has one ingredient; a snack cake has thirty). T2: Brazilian researcher Carlos Monteiro's NOVA scale ranks by processing — group 1 is whole/minimally-processed food, group 4 is 'ultra-processed', industrially assembled from refined ingredients rather than cooked from recognizable food. T3: the marker ingredients that flag group 4 — high-fructose corn syrup, hydrolysed protein, modified starch, emulsifiers: substances you'd never keep in a home kitchen. T4: the honest evidence — large observational studies link ultra-processed-heavy diets to obesity and heart disease, but correlation can't yet prove the PROCESSING is the culprit versus sheer energy density or crowding out whole foods. T5: Kevin Hall's 2019 NIH controlled-feeding study — people on an ultra-processed diet ate about 500 more calories a day and gained weight versus a whole-food diet matched for sugar, fat, salt and fiber — the first controlled hint that processing itself drives overeating (still one study; mechanism debated).

**Framing:** Scene-led, never 'What is NOVA?' — open T1 on reading a real ingredient list. Food-anchored answers only: the tested thing is the food/ingredient/finding, never the researcher's name or a bare number. ACCURACY RAIL: NOVA classifies by PROCESSING, not nutrients, and the health link is CORRELATION — do NOT state ultra-processed food as a proven cause; steel-man that it may be energy density or displacement of whole foods. Dinner-test payoff = T5 (the Hall study — same nutrients, more calories eaten; the 500-calorie figure lives in the stem, the concept is the answer).
