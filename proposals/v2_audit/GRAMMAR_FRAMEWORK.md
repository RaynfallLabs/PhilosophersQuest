# Grammar Framework (v2)

The voice + structure rules for the grammar bank. This document is the
**framework** (the why + voice); see `GRAMMAR_TEMPLATES.md` for the
**templates** (the per-tier approved patterns) and
`tools/quizgen/gates/grammar.py` for the **gates** (the deterministic
checks).

Grammar is fundamentally different from the wonder subjects (history,
philosophy, cooking, animal, geography). It is one of the two snappy-rote
exceptions named in `feedback_no_rote_wonder.md` (the other is math).
The Wonder Pattern does not apply. Instead grammar has its own
controlling voice rule — §1 below.

---

## §1 The Comma-Saves-Lives Pattern (THE controlling voice rule)

**The most memorable grammar question creates a punchline through misuse.**
The wrong version is funny, absurd, or alarming — and that's how the kid
remembers the rule.

This is grammar's analog to history's Wonder Pattern. Where wonder questions
ask "what's the most retellable cool fact?", grammar questions ask "where does
the WRONG version produce the loudest laugh or the most obvious meaning-shift?"

### Hierarchy of grammar-memorability

Tier 1 of the hierarchy is the highest-priority voice — bank-wide
distribution should be top-heavy on tiers 1–2 of this hierarchy.

| Hierarchy tier | Type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. PUNCHLINE-PRODUCING ERRORS** (highest) | The wrong version creates a comically wrong reading. | "Let's eat, Grandma!" vs "Let's eat Grandma!" — comma saves lives |
| **2. REGISTER / CLARITY CONTRASTS** | Two grammatically valid sentences; one says what you mean. | "Mistakes were made" vs "I made the mistake" — active vs passive rhetoric |
| **3. INFLECTIONAL / PARADIGM** | Knowing your verb / pronoun forms. | "had ran" vs "had run"; "who" vs "whom"; lay/lie |
| **4. CATEGORY NAMING** (lowest, but valid) | Pure vocabulary recall. | "What part of speech is *quickly*?" |

All four are **valid**. None are banned (grammar IS exempt from the
anti-rote gate per `moral_vision.md`). But the BANK's center of gravity
must be tiers 1–2 of this hierarchy — that's where engagement and
retention live.

### Three-question test (mirrors the Wonder Pattern's test)

For each grammar question, ask:

1. **The Laugh Test** — does the WRONG version of this make a kid laugh?
   If yes, it's a tier-1 punchline question. If no, are you sure the
   answer earns its tier?
2. **The Articulation Test** — does this teach the kid something they
   can USE when they write or speak? Tier-1 + tier-2 pass; tier-3
   sometimes passes; tier-4 (pure category-naming) often fails this.
3. **The Retellability Test** — would the kid spontaneously tell a sibling
   "did you know if you forget the comma it changes who you're eating?"
   If yes, the question is doing its job.

### Worked examples (the canonical punchline corpus)

| Construction | Example | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| Vocative comma | "Let's eat, Grandma!" vs "Let's eat Grandma!" | Commas can change meaning utterly |
| Misplaced modifier | "I saw the boy with the telescope" | Modifier position controls meaning |
| Dangling modifier | "Walking down the street, the trees were beautiful" | Trees aren't walking |
| Garden-path | "The horse raced past the barn fell." | Syntactic ambiguity; *raced* is participial |
| Homophone confusion | "Their, there, they're going there with their stuff." | Three words, three meanings |
| Apostrophe error | "Its raining" vs "It's raining" | Possessive vs contraction |
| Oxford comma | "I invited the strippers, JFK, and Stalin" vs "I invited the strippers, JFK and Stalin" | Without the Oxford comma, JFK and Stalin become the strippers |
| Subject-verb agreement | "The boys is here" | Singular verb, plural subject = grating |
| Buffalo construction (T5) | "Buffalo buffalo Buffalo buffalo buffalo buffalo Buffalo buffalo." | Same word three ways (city / animal / verb) |
| Eats, Shoots and Leaves | The panda joke (Lynne Truss) | One comma changes a vegetarian to an outlaw |
| Tom Swiftie / pun | "'Pass me the shellfish,' she said crabbily." | Word play teaches lexical association |

### What this REPLACES (do not import from wonder subjects)

- ❌ NAMED THINGS > VIVID ACTIONS > etc. (the Wonder Pattern hierarchy)
- ❌ Drama-Available Rule
- ❌ Behind-the-scenes wonder (popular myth vs reality)
- ❌ Dinner Test in the wonder sense ("what would the kid retell?")
- ❌ Singular-cool-fact answer requirement

The Laugh Test and the Articulation Test together replace these. The
Retellability Test is the cousin of the Dinner Test, adapted.

## §2 Grade-band tier ladder

Tier IS conceptual complexity (per `feedback_wonder_pattern.md` lift — the
universal rule). For grammar, conceptual complexity maps approximately to
US grade-band ELA standards.

The kids playing this bank are described by the user as "advanced but
also failed by public schooling — they lack the vocab to easily describe
types of verbs, nouns, prepositions, etc." This means: their reasoning
runs ahead of their terminology. The bank must teach the terminology
inline (see §3 below) while letting their reasoning carry them through.

| Tier | Grade band | Conceptual scope | Voice priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T1** | Grades 1–3 | Parts of speech (noun/verb/adj/adv) introduced by example; basic plurals; common articles a/an; simple punctuation (`. ? !`); capitalization (sentence-start, proper nouns); interjections | Vocab-teaching + simple punchlines ("Let's eat Grandma!") |
| **T2** | Grades 3–5 | Subject-verb agreement; pronouns by case (subject/object/possessive); basic tenses (past/present/future); homophones (their/there/they're); commas in lists; contractions; FANBOYS; basic prepositions; basic etymology (Latin/Greek roots) | Punchline-producing errors are the workhorse here |
| **T3** | Grades 6–8 | Tense consistency; common errors (lay/lie, less/fewer, who/whom in simple cases); compound + complex sentences; semicolon/colon; apostrophe rules; dangling modifiers; figurative language (metaphor, simile, personification, hyperbole); descriptive vs prescriptive; word-meaning shifts | Punchline + clarity contrasts |
| **T4** | Grades 9–10 | Subjunctive mood; parallel structure; advanced pronoun cases; sentence variety (simple/compound/complex/compound-complex); advanced punctuation (em-dash, ellipsis); active vs passive rhetorical choice; etymology + Norman/Old-English history; style guides (Strunk/Fowler) | Articulation-strong: which sentence says what you mean |
| **T5** | Grades 11+ (AP / college prep) | Gerunds vs participles; mood (indicative/subjunctive/imperative); syntactic analysis (noun clauses, adverb clauses, restrictive vs non-restrictive); rhetorical figures (chiasmus, polysyndeton, asyndeton, anaphora, litotes); modality (epistemic vs deontic); conditional types (0/1/2/3/mixed); register; garden-path sentences; the Buffalo construction | Register / articulation / language-as-craft |

### Grade-10 ceiling (preserved from the 2026-05-19 audit)

**No Chomsky, Saussure, Sapir-Whorf, Bloomfield, Pāṇini, transformational
grammar, generative syntax, deep/surface structure, morphosyntax,
phonological theory, evidentiality typology, verb-valency taxonomy, or
critical-period hypothesis content** anywhere in the bank. These were
explicitly dropped in the 2026-05-19 audit as above-grade-10. The
`gate_no_above_grade10` gate enforces this deterministically.

What IS in scope at T5: rhetorical figures with concrete English
examples; advanced English syntax (noun clauses, restrictive vs
non-restrictive); modality with concrete English verb examples;
conditional types in English; style-guide history (Strunk/Fowler/AP/MLA);
specific English etymological events (Norman conquest, Great Vowel
Shift, KJV 1611, Webster 1828) — all concrete, grade-10-appropriate.

## §3 Vocab-teaching invariant

User-specified: "Consider my kids 'advanced' but also failed by public
schooling. They lack the vocab to easily describe types of verbs, nouns,
prepositions, etc."

**Rule**: At T1–T3, if a stem names a grammatical category ("preposition",
"subjunctive", "appositive", "gerund", etc.), the question must EITHER:

- **(a) Show the concept by example in the stem** — a quoted sentence
  the kid can parse with their reasoning, OR
- **(b) Define the term in the `context` field** — the teaching block
  shown AFTER the answer is revealed.

A kid who doesn't yet have "preposition" as vocabulary CANNOT be filtered
out by a stem that just asks "What part of speech is *quickly*?". They
need an example sentence OR a context paragraph that teaches the
category.

T4–T5 may relax this — by Grade 9, the vocab is assumed.

The gate enforcing this is `gate_vocab_teaching_at_t13`.

## §4 Articulation thread (runs through the bank)

User-specified: "Sentence-level and ARTICULATION can be here; GOOD
sentences help kids write and convey their ideas. This can be strong at
T4-5, but should be repeated through the bank."

Articulation questions teach the kid to produce clearer writing. They
sit naturally in Hierarchy tier-2 (register/clarity contrasts).

Distribution target:

- **T1–T2**: ≥ 1 in 10 — "Which sentence is clearer?" with kid-level
  scenarios. Don't ask for grammatical category names; ask which
  feels more natural / direct.
- **T3**: ≥ 2 in 10 — transitions, conjunctions, sentence-combining.
  "Which sentence combines these ideas best?"
- **T4–T5**: ≥ 3 in 10 — parallel structure, active vs passive
  rhetorical effect, sentence variety, "the right word in the right
  place" snap, register-matching.

This is bank-wide distribution; no per-question gate enforces it, but
the audit-agent prompt should track and report.

## §7 Gates (what applies)

Configured in `tools/quizgen/gates/grammar.py` and registered in
`tools/quizgen/audit/validate.py`.

### Apply
- `schema` (pipeline)
- `length_budget` (pipeline) — per the caps in §6
- `length_parity` (pipeline) — grammar is in `ANSWER_OUTLIER_SUBJECTS`
  per `length_parity.py` (the 1.6× answer-outlier flex allows the
  punchline answer to be longer than short distractors)
- `duplicate` (pipeline) — 0.85 threshold like every non-math subject
- `trailing_tokens` (pipeline)
- `choice_shape_parity` (from `gates.philosophy`, subject-agnostic)

### Skip (with reason — not "conveniently forgetting")
- `anti_rote` — grammar is EXEMPT per `moral_vision.md` (the rule that
  exempts math + grammar from the anti-rote regex)
- `answer_collision` — grammar is in `EXEMPT_SUBJECTS` per
  `answer_collision.py` (drill recurrence is by design)
- `math_correctness` — N/A
- `no_verdict_on_contested` — grammar has no contested topics
- `wonder_bias_check` — N/A
- `scenario_anchored_correct` — N/A (no fallacy-question pattern)
- `place_anchor_check`, `history_named_anchor`, `forcing_constraint`
  (history voice rules) — N/A
- `subject_named` (cooking voice rule) — N/A (the sentence under
  analysis IS the subject by convention)

### New grammar-specific gates
- `gate_example_sentence_quoted` — if a stem references "this sentence"
  or "the sentence", the sentence must be present in quotes (single
  or double) so the kid can parse it. SOFT-warn (not all references
  need a verbatim quote).
- `gate_vocab_teaching_at_t13` — T1–T3 stems naming grammatical
  categories must include either an example sentence in quotes OR a
  context field that defines the category. HARD.
- `gate_tier_concept_appropriate` — flags above-grade content at T1–T3
  (e.g., "subjunctive" at T1 is wrong). HARD.
- `gate_no_above_grade10` — flags Chomsky / Saussure / Sapir / Whorf /
  Bloomfield / Pāṇini / transformational-grammar / generative-syntax /
  deep-structure / surface-structure / morphosyntax / phonological-theory
  / evidentiality-typology / verb-valency / critical-period content
  anywhere in the bank. HARD.
- `gate_no_fake_etymology` — heuristic check against a known-bad list
  ("rule of thumb" wife-beating myth, "OK" all-correct origin, "tip"
  to-insure-promptness, "posh" port-out-starboard-home, etc.). HARD.
  Per the strategies doc anti-pattern §3.

## §8 Anti-patterns (do not generate these)

Lifted + extended from `docs/quiz/grammar_strategies.md` anti-patterns:

- **No standalone definition-recall** below T4 unless paired with example
  sentence. "What is a noun?" with no sentence to anchor it is banned
  at T1–T3 by `gate_vocab_teaching_at_t13`.
- **No fake etymologies** — every word origin must be verifiable.
  Folk-etymology myths (rule of thumb, OK, tip, posh, golf, sincerely
  "without wax", port out starboard home) are explicitly banned.
- **No prescriptivism without acknowledgment** — when teaching a rule,
  acknowledge it as such (Strunk says X, Fowler disagrees). Don't
  pronounce verdict on contested usage ("ain't is wrong" — that's a
  prescriptivist verdict; instead show "ain't" as informal-register
  and let the context teach the convention).
- **No silly for its own sake** — every joke must teach. The
  Comma-Saves-Lives Pattern requires the punchline to land on a real
  grammatical principle.
- **No above-grade-10 linguistics theory** — see §2 ceiling.
- **No content warping to fit the punchline pattern** — per
  `feedback_no_content_warping.md`, principles are MENUS not
  CHECKLISTS. Hierarchy tiers 3–4 of the Comma-Saves-Lives are valid
  voice modes (inflectional drills, category naming). Don't force a
  tier-4-hierarchy question into a tier-1 punchline if the natural
  voice for that content is paradigm recall.
