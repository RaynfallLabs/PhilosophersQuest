# Cooking Bank Framework (2026-05-22)

This is the rule set for what a good Philosopher's Quest *cooking* question looks like. The bank teaches **technique + chemistry + cultural inheritance + history as lens** — not recipe-recall, definition lookup, restaurant trivia, or fad-diet moralism.

## Core principle

A 5th-10th grader should leave this bank with:
- Working understanding of **food chemistry** — Maillard reaction, gluten development, emulsion, fermentation, gelation, denaturation, enzymatic browning, hydrolysis
- Mastery of **traditional technique** as skill heritage — knife cuts, mother sauces, braising, smoking, curing, lamination, fermentation
- Genuine appreciation for **Western culinary tradition** — Escoffier's brigade, Carême's grand cuisine, Brillat-Savarin's *Physiology of Taste*, Child, Beard, Fisher
- Wonder about **other world food cultures** — Sichuan ma-la, Persian tahdig, Mexican mole, Japanese dashi/sushi-as-preservation, Korean kimchi, Ethiopian injera, North African tagine, Indian dosa fermentation
- Insight into **food as historical lens** — what people ate at a given time tells us about the civilization (medieval feudalism reflected at the table, the Columbian Exchange's demographic consequences, sailor scurvy and empire, monasteries inventing cheese and beer, the coffeehouse and Enlightenment)
- **Recognition of analytical moves** on food politics (whole foods vs hyperprocessed, fast food vs from-scratch, farm-to-table vs global supply) — without the bank declaring verdicts
- **Religious dietary traditions as cultural fact** — kosher, halal, Christian fasting calendars, Buddhist/Hindu/Jain vegetarian traditions — descriptive knowledge, not endorsement or critique

If a question can be answered by a toddler ("what's that flat round baked food with cheese?"), it has failed. If a question reveals the chemistry, the technique, the cultural depth, or the historical significance behind something the player thought they understood, it has succeeded.

## What this framework forbids

1. **Toddler-obvious common-name lookups** — "What is a round flat baked food with toppings?" (pizza), "What's the orange root vegetable?" (carrot), "What do you call cooked beef between buns?" (hamburger). The answer being trivial common knowledge to a 5-year-old fails the bank.
2. **Definition recall** — "What does 'sauté' mean?" / "What does 'al dente' mean?" Vocabulary, not cooking.
3. **Pure recipe memorization** — "How many cups of flour in a standard pie crust?" Trivia.
4. **Brand and restaurant trivia** — "In what year did Julia Child publish *Mastering the Art of French Cooking*?" / "What restaurant did Escoffier work at?" Pure date or proper-noun recall.
5. **Pure chef name-lookup as the test** — "Who invented the brigade kitchen?" Names go in *context*, not as the test. ("A man who served as kitchen manager at the Savoy in London in the 1890s organized professional kitchens into stations under a chef de cuisine, with sauce, fish, vegetable, pastry, and grill all distinct posts. What did this system enable?")
6. **Fad diet / health moralism** — "Is gluten bad for you?" / "Is meat unhealthy?" / "Should everyone be vegan?" The bank does not moralize at the player about legitimate dietary traditions.
7. **Industrial-food blanket condemnation** — "Are processed foods poison?" with one right answer. T5 food-politics content uses the recognize-the-move pedagogy (Bastiat seen/unseen, Sowell compared-to-what, Pollan's "edible food-like substances" as an analytical move to identify) — not as verdicts.
8. **Religious dietary endorsement or critique** — kosher, halal, Hindu, Buddhist, Christian fasting all appear as cultural fact. The bank does not adjudicate the religious claims; it teaches the practices and their cultural significance.

## What the framework REQUIRES

1. **Wonder hook on every question** — chemistry revealed, technique decoded, cultural depth surfaced, or historical lens applied
2. **Real food science** — every claim about Maillard temperatures, gluten development, fermentation timing, etc. must be factually accurate
3. **Forcing constraint** — stem includes the detail that picks out one correct answer
4. **Steelman distractors** — wrong, but in a way that a thoughtful person would consider. No "magic flour" distractors.

## Where chef names, restaurant names, and Latin/French technique terms belong

**In the `context` field.** Always. Context says "Auguste Escoffier (1846-1935) at the Savoy" or "this technique is called *gastrique* in French haute cuisine." The stem itself never relies on knowing the proper noun.

## The five tiers

### T1 — 5th grade (10-11)

- **Scope**: kitchen wonder a kid can picture. Why does popcorn pop? Why does dough rise? Why does cut apple turn brown?
- **Voice**: concrete, no French technique terms in stem
- **Content**: basic chemistry (gas expansion, browning, dissolving), common ingredients with non-obvious facts (vanilla comes from an orchid; cinnamon is tree bark; saffron is dried flower stamen)
- **Form**: ≤200 char stem, ≤90 char choice, ≤200 char context

### T2 — 6th grade (11-12)

- **Scope**: technique fundamentals + cultural wonder ("Sushi was originally fish preservation. The rice was the byproduct.")
- **Voice**: introduces basic vocab (boil, simmer, sauté, braise) with inline meaning if needed
- **Content**: Maillard reaction at first-introduction level, basic knife cuts, what salt actually does to meat, why dough needs kneading
- **Form**: ≤240 char stem, ≤110 char choice, ≤240 char context

### T3 — 7th grade (12-13)

- **Scope**: specific cuisine techniques, ingredient chemistry, regional traditions
- **Voice**: can use "fermentation," "emulsion," "gluten," "denaturation" with brief inline definitions
- **Content**: French mother sauces, Japanese dashi as umami foundation, Italian sofritto, lacto-fermentation timing, why pickling and curing work
- **Form**: ≤280 char stem, ≤130 char choice, ≤300 char context

### T4 — 8th grade (13-14)

- **Scope**: deeper food science, professional kitchen organization, cultural history with specific dates/places
- **Voice**: scientist-grade with inline definitions for specialist terms
- **Content**: Maillard chemistry detail (~140°C threshold, carbonyl + amine), Escoffier's brigade system, Columbian Exchange specifics, charcuterie chemistry (curing salt, nitrites, color and safety)
- **Form**: ≤320 char stem, ≤160 char choice, ≤360 char context

### T5 — 9-10th grade (14-16)

- **Scope**: food chemistry at depth, food-as-historical-lens, recognize-the-move on food politics, world cuisine specialist content
- **Voice**: naturalist + historian + analyst; specialist terms with parenthetical first-use defs
- **Content**: enzymatic browning vs Maillard distinction, koji and Aspergillus oryzae cultivation, Roman garum factories at Pompeii reconstructed from amphora residues, Crusader-era spice trade economic effects, modernist cuisine debates (Adrià, Achatz), Pollan's "edible food-like substances" as an analytical move
- **Form**: ≤360 char stem, ≤180 char choice, ≤420 char context

## Substantive moral vision

### 1. Western culinary tradition is real human achievement
- Escoffier's brigade kitchen, Carême's grand cuisine, Brillat-Savarin's *Physiology of Taste* (1825), Beard, Child, Fisher, Elizabeth David
- French haute cuisine, Italian regional, Spanish, German charcuterie, British roasting traditions all serious — not "European cuisine is just colonialism"
- The Mediterranean diet as substantive cultural and biochemical achievement

### 2. World cuisines as wonder, not exotic othering
- Sichuan ma-la (numbing-spicy theory, hua jiao Szechuan peppercorns), Vietnamese pho as French-Chinese colonial fusion, Mexican mole's 30+ ingredient complexity, Persian tahdig (crust is prize), Korean kimchi regional variation, Japanese sushi as fish preservation method (the rice was originally discarded), Ethiopian injera spongy fermentation, North African tagine cooking, Indian dosa lactic-fermentation timing, Sicilian eggplant from Arab influence — substantive cultural depth, not "exotic food" caricature

### 3. Whole-animal-use respected
- Charcuterie, offal, bone broth, fats — nose-to-tail as the ethic, downstream from the animal bank's stewardship tradition
- Traditional respect for the animal that follows from regulated harvest

### 4. Traditional preservation is mastery
- Salt, smoke, lacto-ferment, dry, age — pre-refrigeration foodways teach things modern kitchens have forgotten
- Roman garum, Korean kimchi, Mediterranean cured meats, Scandinavian rakfisk/lutefisk, Native American pemmican

### 5. Honest about industrial food's genuine achievements
- The Green Revolution (Borlaug), refrigeration cold chain, food safety regulation, real cost reduction democratizing nutrition
- Per moral vision: not anti-civilizational. The bank does NOT claim "all industrial food is poison" — that's a verdict, not an analytical move
- Substantive recognition that American food production is a real human achievement

### 6. Recognize-the-move on food politics (T4-T5)
- When a critic argues "fast food is worse than from-scratch," the bank teaches the analytical move being made (Bastiat's seen calories vs unseen dietary diversity; Sowell's "compared to what?"; Pollan's "edible food-like substances")
- The student identifies the move; the verdict is theirs to draw
- Slow Food, terroir, AOC (Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée) appear as substantive traditional positions on quality and provenance

### 7. No fad diet / health moralism
- Keto, vegan, Paleo, intermittent fasting, gluten-free as **cultural choices**, not as right/wrong
- The bank does not declare sugar/gluten/meat/fat to be the dietary villain of the moment
- Nutrition science honestly characterized — "suggestive but not settled" where evidence is mixed (similar to the Toxoplasma framing in animal bank)

### 8. Religious dietary traditions as cultural fact
- Kosher (kashrut) rules and historical context, halal slaughter requirements, Hindu vegetarianism and the sacred cow, Buddhist and Jain dietary practices, Christian fasting calendars — all described as cultural facts the kid should know
- **Intra-Christian variation as bank flavor**: the monastic ascetic tradition (vegetarian-leaning, fish on Fridays, monasteries inventing cheese / beer / liqueurs), the Lenten and Advent fasts, the medieval feast/fast calendar; the Crusader exception (military dispensations from fasting, exposure to Levantine cuisine that returned to Europe — sugar, citrus, almonds, spices)
- The bank does not adjudicate religious claims; it teaches the practices and their cultural significance, same as Anubis in the animal bank

### 9. Food as historical lens (CROSS-CUTTING)
This is a distinguishing feature of this bank. What a people ate at a specific time tells us about everything else in that civilization:
- **Medieval feudalism at the table**: peasant black bread + pottage vs noble white bread + spiced meat. The order dishes were served signaled social ranking. Sumptuary laws.
- **Sailor scurvy + citrus + empire**: Royal Navy's 1795 lime/lemon mandate, the "limey" epithet, scurvy's effect on Age of Sail exploration
- **Columbian Exchange demographic consequences**: potato → Irish population growth → famine → emigration; tomato to Italy transforming "Italian" cuisine (which had no tomato before ~1500); chili to India and China
- **Monasteries inventing cheese, beer, distilled liqueurs**: ascetic life producing real innovations (Trappist beers, Carthusian Chartreuse, Benedictine, dozens of cheeses)
- **The coffeehouse and the Enlightenment**: rise of sober public discourse + caffeine + assembly venues
- **Roman garum factories at Pompeii**: amphora residues reveal Mediterranean trade network and the cuisine of empire
- **Crusader-era Mediterranean fusion**: sugar, citrus, almonds, spices, eggplant entering European cuisine via the Crusades; Sicilian and Spanish cuisine still bears Arab influence
- **Tudor banquet structure**: dishes served in social-hierarchy order; what got salted vs spiced revealed wealth
- **Russian tea**: 17th-century caravan trade with China taking 16 months from Beijing to Moscow; samovar culture
- **Native American foods**: pre-Columbian civilization complexity visible in maize cultivation, chocolate fermentation, chili breeding

### 10. Table etiquette and manners as cultural inheritance
Etiquette is congealed history. Why we do what we do at the table reveals the civilization that shaped the practice. The bank teaches the ORIGIN of traditions, especially American.

**American etiquette specifically:**
- The **"American zigzag"** eating style — knife in right hand to cut, then put down, switch fork to right hand to eat — is uniquely American. Origin: colonial dining when forks were scarce and a single fork was used for cutting on the plate and lifting to the mouth. The "switch" became fixed; Europe meanwhile standardized continental style (fork stays in left, tines down).
- **Napkin in lap** — medieval European origin (large tablecloths were communal napkins; eventually individualized)
- **Elbows off the table** — bench-seated medieval dining; elbows on table could topple your neighbor
- **No business at dinner** — American tradition with roots in colonial hospitality
- **Sunday dinner** as American social institution; the abundant table as expression of generosity
- **New England Thanksgiving** as American etiquette flagship
- **Southern hospitality** — open invitation, abundant table, "y'all come"
- **Bread broken by hand, not cut** — medieval table-cleanliness + Eucharistic associations
- **Grace before meals** — Christian origin, variations across American religious traditions

**The fork itself**: introduced from Byzantine empire to Venice ~1075 (Theodora Doukaina-Selvo); spread slowly across centuries; medieval Catholic clergy resisted as effeminate; took until ~17th century to reach common European tables. Americans inherited the fork from English colonial settlers but developed the zigzag style independently.

**Service-à-la-française vs service-à-la-russe**: All dishes brought at once (French) vs course-by-course (Russian). Russian ambassador Alexander Kurakin introduced the Russian style to Paris in 1810 — it became the model for modern restaurant service. Why does it matter? It transformed how we experience meals (rhythm, course-pacing, the savoring of individual dishes) and made restaurants — public dining for paying strangers — feasible.

**Place setting evolution**: Medieval table had a knife and your hands. Victorian formal table added forks of multiple sizes (fish, dinner, salad, dessert, oyster), spoons of multiple sizes, multiple glasses. The "outside-in" rule (use the outermost utensil first) is Victorian. The Victorian formal place setting tells us about industrialization (mass-produced silver), Empire (oysters from American Atlantic to English tables), and class signaling.

**Toasting traditions**: Roman safety origin (host drank first to show wine was not poisoned), medieval bread-in-wine practice (soaked bread = "toast"), modern "raise your glass" with eye contact (especially German custom — must meet eyes or seven years of bad sex, supposedly).

**Cross-cultural etiquette as cultural fact:**
- Japanese: chopsticks never stand in rice (funeral imagery), never crossed (funeral), never passed chopstick-to-chopstick (funeral bone-picking)
- Chinese: hold rice bowl up to the mouth (Western: rude; Chinese: respectful); shared-plate etiquette
- Indian: right-hand eating tradition (left hand reserved for hygiene functions)
- Middle Eastern: refuse food twice and accept on third offer (taarof in Persian culture especially); hospitality obligation
- French: bread on the tablecloth, not on the plate; cheese course before dessert in formal meals
- Italian: no cappuccino after 11 AM (digestive milk theory); cheese on seafood is sacrilege; never break spaghetti to fit a pot
- British: milk-first vs tea-first historically class-related (milk-first was working-class — cheap porcelain couldn't handle hot tea directly)
- Korean: don't start eating until the eldest does; pour drinks for others (especially elders), never yourself
- Russian: vodka always with food, never alone; toasts and refills follow strict order
