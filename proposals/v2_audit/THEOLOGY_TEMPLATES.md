# Theology Templates (v2)

Per-tier approved stem patterns + anti-patterns. Companion to `THEOLOGY_FRAMEWORK.md` (the WHY); this is the WHAT.

Authoring rule: every new theology question should match an approved template AND fit somewhere on the Wonder Pattern hierarchy (`THEOLOGY_FRAMEWORK.md` §1). **TELL THE STORY.** No doctrine quizzes. No author attribution.

---

## §1 Approved stem patterns by tier

### T1 patterns (grade 5 — crisp scene + named character/object + the wonder)

Length cap: ≤ 280 chars total.

| Pattern | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Christian story primer | "Long ago, a giant named Goliath stood nine feet tall and dared Israel's army to send one champion to fight him. A shepherd boy stepped forward with only a sling and five smooth stones. What was the boy's name?" → "David" | Named figure + vivid scene + dramatic challenge |
| Greek myth primer | "When Zeus had a terrible headache, the smith god Hephaestus split his skull open with an axe. Out leaped a fully-armed warrior goddess in a bronze helmet. Who came out of Zeus's head?" → "Athena" | Vivid action + named birth + recognition |
| Norse myth primer | "The thunder god rode a chariot pulled by two goats and carried a hammer so powerful it could level mountains. What was the hammer's name?" → "Mjölnir" | Named object + dramatic power + clean fact |
| Arthurian primer | "A young squire pulled a sword from a stone that no one else could budge, and this proved he was the rightful king of Britain. What was the boy's name?" → "Arthur" | Named action + the test + named figure |
| Robin Hood primer | "An outlaw with a longbow lived in Sherwood Forest, robbing rich travelers to give to the poor. His best friend was a giant called Little John, and his band wore green. Who was he?" → "Robin Hood" | Named setting + named companion + the legend |
| Saint legend primer | "An old bishop named Polycarp, age 86, was ordered to curse Christ or burn alive. He said, 'Eighty-six years I have served Him.' What happened next?" → "He was burned at the stake" | Named saint + named age + dramatic refusal |

### T2 patterns (grade 6 — one-paragraph story + named figure + the action)

Length cap: ≤ 480.

| Pattern | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bible narrative | "Joseph's brothers were jealous because their father Jacob gave him a coat of many colors. They threw Joseph into a pit, then sold him to traders heading to Egypt. Years later, when famine struck, those same brothers came to Egypt begging for grain — not knowing the powerful Egyptian official they faced was Joseph. What did Joseph eventually do for them?" → "Forgave them and saved his family from starvation" | Story arc + named figures + dramatic reversal |
| Hercules labor | "King Eurystheus ordered Hercules to bring back the skin of the Nemean Lion, a beast whose hide no weapon could pierce. Hercules tracked the lion to its cave, blocked one entrance, and confronted it inside. Since arrows and clubs were useless, how did he kill it?" → "He strangled it with his bare hands" | Named figures + the impossible task + vivid action |
| Thor adventure | "Thor woke up one morning and his hammer Mjölnir was gone — stolen by the giant Thrym, who demanded the goddess Freyja as ransom. Loki dressed Thor in a wedding veil and bridal gown so he could enter Thrym's hall in disguise. What did Thor do once he had Mjölnir back?" → "He killed Thrym and every giant in the hall" | Named figures + dramatic disguise + payoff |
| Arthurian episode | "A mysterious arm draped in white silk rose from a lake, holding a sword aloft. King Arthur rowed out in a boat and the Lady of the Lake gave him this enchanted blade. What was the sword's name?" → "Excalibur" | Vivid image + named giver + named object |
| Robin Hood scene | "The Sheriff of Nottingham held an archery contest, secretly hoping to trap Robin Hood. Robin came in disguise and split the previous best arrow down the middle to win the golden prize. How did the Sheriff lose his prisoner?" → "Robin's men ambushed the Sheriff on the road and freed everyone" | Disguise + named contest + payoff |
| Christian miracle | "A crowd of five thousand had followed Jesus into the wilderness all day with nothing to eat. His disciples wanted to send them home, but a boy offered the only food anyone had brought. What did the boy have?" → "Five loaves and two fish" | Named miracle + crowd scale + the cool detail |

### T3 patterns (grade 7 — scene + stakes + the dramatic moment + answer is the cool detail)

Length cap: ≤ 680.

| Pattern | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Trojan War episode | "After ten years of failed siege at Troy, the Greek army appeared to sail away one morning, leaving behind only a huge wooden horse as a 'gift to Athena.' The Trojans dragged it inside the walls and celebrated their victory all night. The Trojan priest Laocoön shouted a warning that became proverbial — and was promptly strangled by sea serpents sent from the gods. What did Laocoön shout?" → "Beware Greeks bearing gifts" | Named figure + named event + the famous quoted warning |
| Daniel in the den | "King Darius signed a decree that anyone praying to any god but the king for thirty days would be thrown to the lions. Daniel kept praying toward Jerusalem three times a day with his window open. The king tried all night to find a way out of his own decree but couldn't. When the stone was rolled away at dawn, what did the guards find?" → "Daniel alive — the lions hadn't touched him" | Named figures + dramatic stakes + the cool reveal |
| Theseus + Minotaur | "King Minos kept a half-man, half-bull monster named the Minotaur in a vast labyrinth designed by Daedalus, fed every nine years on seven Athenian youths and seven maidens. The Athenian prince Theseus volunteered to be one of the tributes, vowing to kill the beast. Princess Ariadne gave him something at the labyrinth entrance that let him kill the Minotaur AND find his way back out. What did she give him?" → "A ball of thread to mark his path back" | Named figures + impossible task + the named clever object |
| Sigurd + Fafnir | "The Volsung hero Sigurd was raised by the dwarf smith Regin, who reforged the broken sword Gram from his ancestor's fragments. Sigurd then rode out to fight the dragon Fafnir, who guarded a cursed treasure hoard. Sigurd dug a pit in the dragon's path, hid below, and stabbed upward as Fafnir slithered overhead. When Sigurd accidentally tasted Fafnir's blood, what new power did he gain?" → "He could understand the speech of birds" | Named figures + named weapon + vivid action + dramatic transformation |
| Roland at Roncevaux | "In 778 AD, the rear-guard of Charlemagne's army under the paladin Roland was ambushed by Basques at Roncevaux Pass in the Pyrenees. Roland refused to blow his ivory horn Olifant to summon the main army until it was far too late — too proud to admit defeat. When he finally blew it, the effort burst the veins in his temples. What was Roland's sword called?" → "Durendal" | Named battle + named horn + named hero + the proud-to-death detail |
| Joan at Rouen | "Joan of Arc was nineteen years old when an English ecclesiastical court at Rouen condemned her as a heretic in 1431. She wore men's armor in battle on charges from Heaven, claimed visits from Saints Catherine and Margaret, and led French forces to break the siege of Orléans. After she was burned alive on May 30, the English did something with her remains specifically to prevent French Catholics from having relics. What did they do?" → "Threw her ashes into the Seine" | Named figure + named event + named age + the specific dramatic act |

Length cap: ≤ 900.

| Pattern | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Last Supper | "On the night before his crucifixion, Jesus gathered his twelve disciples in an upper room in Jerusalem for what would become known as the Last Supper. He took bread, broke it, and said 'This is my body, given for you.' Then he took the cup of wine and said 'This is my blood of the new covenant.' During the meal he announced that one of the twelve would betray him that very night. One disciple slipped out into the darkness shortly after — and would lead the temple guards to Jesus in the Garden of Gethsemane for thirty pieces of silver. Who was the betrayer?" → "Judas Iscariot" | Named figures + dramatic specifics + named betrayer |
| Death of Balder | "Balder the beautiful, son of Odin and Frigg, dreamed his own death. His mother Frigg traveled the nine worlds extracting promises from every thing — fire, water, iron, stone, every plant, every animal — that none would ever harm Balder. The gods amused themselves throwing weapons at him, knowing nothing could hurt him. But Loki discovered Frigg had skipped one plant, thinking it too young to matter. He carved a dart from it and gave it to Balder's blind brother Hodr to throw. What plant killed Balder?" → "Mistletoe" | Named figures + cosmic stakes + named overlooked weapon |
| Grail quest | "Among the knights of King Arthur's Round Table, the seat called the Siege Perilous remained empty — reserved for the one knight pure enough to achieve the Holy Grail. When the young Sir Galahad finally arrived at Camelot, he sat down in the Siege Perilous without harm, the first ever to do so. A vision of the Grail appeared at the table that evening, veiled. Galahad, his father Lancelot, Percival, and Bors set out on the quest. Only Galahad would see the unveiled Grail. What was the Grail believed to be?" → "The cup Christ used at the Last Supper" | Named knights + named seat + named relic |
| Orpheus + Eurydice | "When the nymph Eurydice died of a snake bite on her wedding day, her grieving husband Orpheus descended into the underworld carrying only his lyre. So beautifully did he play before Hades and Persephone that they wept and granted him an impossible gift — Eurydice could follow him back to the world of the living, but on one strict condition: Orpheus must walk ahead of her all the way out and not look back even once. They were nearly out, sunlight visible ahead, when something made Orpheus turn. What was Eurydice's fate when he looked?" → "She vanished forever back into the underworld" | Named tragedy + named instrument + the rule + the failure |
| Becket murder | "On December 29, 1170, four knights of King Henry II rode into Canterbury Cathedral. They were responding to the king's rhetorical outburst about Archbishop Thomas Becket — once Henry's closest friend, now bitterly opposed over Church-vs-Crown authority. The knights demanded Becket come with them. Becket refused. They drew their swords on the cathedral's stone steps as monks watched in horror. What were the king's words that the knights had taken as an order to act?" → "Will no one rid me of this turbulent priest?" | Named date + named figures + the specific dramatic phrase |
| William Tell | "The Habsburg bailiff Hermann Gessler hung his hat on a pole in the Swiss town square and decreed that all who passed must bow to it. The hunter William Tell refused. Gessler ordered Tell shot — unless Tell could shoot an apple off his own son's head from eighty paces with a single arrow. Tell took two arrows from his quiver. He split the apple cleanly. When Gessler asked why the second arrow, Tell answered honestly — and what was that answer?" → "If I had killed my son, the second arrow was for you" | Named figures + impossible test + the deadly reply |

## §2 Choice-shape conventions

### Length parity

Theology IS in `ANSWER_OUTLIER_SUBJECTS`. The 1.6× answer-outlier flex applies — a longer dramatic-fact answer (e.g., "Threw her ashes into the Seine to deny French Catholics any relic") can legitimately exceed distractor lengths by up to 1.6×.

Distractors should still be in approximate parity with each other (1.30 max/min ratio).

### Dash-shape parity

All four choices share dash usage (all use em-dash, or none). Skim-tell failure.

### Distractor plausibility

- **Christian narratives**: distractors are other real Bible characters from adjacent stories (David, Solomon, Saul for Goliath; Daniel, Esther, Job for prayer-stories; Peter, John, James for disciples-related)
- **Greek myth**: distractors are other real Olympians or heroes for the same scene (Hera, Demeter, Artemis as wrong-goddess-from-Zeus's-head; Heracles, Perseus, Achilles as wrong-hero)
- **Norse myth**: distractors are other named objects/figures from the same body (Gungnir for Mjölnir; Sleipnir for Thor's goats; Heimdall for other gods)
- **Arthurian**: distractors are other named items from the same tradition (Galahad for Lancelot; the Holy Lance for the Grail; Caliburn-as-old-name as plausible-wrong)
- **Saint legends**: distractors are other real saints from adjacent stories (Sebastian's arrows, Lawrence's grill, Catherine's wheel)
- **Quoted phrases**: distractors are real quoted phrases from adjacent figures that match the dramatic register

Banned distractor types:
- Made-up saints, gods, heroes, swords, dates
- Modern jokey framings ("Zeus on his smartphone", "Robin Hood's Instagram")
- Cartoon-villain or smug framings ("the false gods of...", "ancient peoples ignorantly believed...")
- Implausible joke distractors that don't test anything
- Comparative-religion equivalence distractors (Islam/Buddhism/Hinduism comparisons in a Christian/Greek/Norse stem)

## §3 Per-tier register

| Tier | Register expectations |
|---|---|
| T1 | Plain English. Named figure + one vivid action OR one named object. ≤ 280 chars total. Examples in §1. |
| T2 | One-paragraph story. Named figures + the dramatic action. ≤ 480. |
| T3 | Scene + stakes + dramatic moment. ≤ 680. Multi-clause but still readable in 25 sec. |
| T4 | Multi-sentence narrative with named specifics. ≤ 900. |
| T5 | Deep story with the most memorable specific detail. ≤ 1100. Stays grade 10 — no academic theology jargon. |

NO theological-research jargon at T5 (no "supralapsarianism", "perichoresis", "filioque", "kenosis", "hypostatic union as academic problem"). Use story-specific named terminology instead (Eucharist, baptism, communion, the cross — these are story-anchored and fine).

## §4 Anti-patterns (do not generate)

See `THEOLOGY_FRAMEWORK.md` §2 + §6. Key points:

- **No doctrine quizzes** — "What is the doctrine of X?", "Define hypostatic union", "Which heresy denied Y?" — banned unless tied to a specific dramatic moment with named figures (Athanasius vs Arius at Nicaea 325 OK; "doctrine of homoousios" without the story banned)
- **No author-attribution recall** — "Who wrote the Iliad?", "Who composed the Edda?", "Who said 'Here I stand'?" — rewrite to the STORY (Luther refusing to recant at Worms 1521, the diet, the emperor's threat, the famous line — not "who said that line")
- **No comparative-religion equivalence** — "How is Christianity like Hinduism?" type questions out of scope
- **No metaphysical adjudication** — bank takes no position on truth-claims; all stories told straight
- **No smug atheist voice** — "ancient peoples ignorantly believed..." (banned)
- **No smug believer voice** — "the false gods of..." (banned)
- **No modern-cult-abuse content** — Jonestown, Heaven's Gate etc. out of scope
- **No fabricated saints, swords, or legends** — only real-named-tradition content
- **No buried-story pattern** (§14) — substance lives in stem, not context
- **No weasel closers** (§15) — pointed concrete questions only
- **No assumed-knowledge** (§16) — every major named figure has a foundational anchor at T1 or T2

## §5 The story shape — five-fact reading

A theology question should reveal these five facts in the stem-and-answer pair:

1. **WHO** — the named figure(s) — Polycarp, Daniel, Hercules, Thor, Roland, Robin Hood, Galahad
2. **WHEN/WHERE** — the named setting — Smyrna 155 AD, Babylon, the Nemean cave, Thrym's hall, Roncevaux Pass, Sherwood
3. **WHAT-IS-AT-STAKE** — the named conflict — burn or curse Christ, pray or die, kill the beast, recover the hammer
4. **THE DRAMATIC SPECIFIC** — the cool named detail in the stem — age 86, three times a day, no weapons, wedding-veil disguise, ivory horn Olifant, archery contest
5. **THE PAYOFF (the answer)** — the most memorable specific cool fact — the exact quote, the named object, the vivid action, the unforgettable image

If any of those five facts is missing or has been left to context, rewrite the stem.
