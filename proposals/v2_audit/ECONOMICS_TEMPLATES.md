## §1 Approved stem patterns by tier

### T1 patterns (grade 5 — crisp moment + named figure + the wonder)

Length cap: ≤ 280 chars total.

| Pattern | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bastiat broken-window primer | "A kid throws a rock through a bakery window. A neighbor says: 'Good — now the baker pays the glazier, the glazier pays his butcher, the butcher buys shoes. Everyone wins!' What did the neighbor miss?" → "What the baker would have spent on shoes if the window hadn't broken" | The signature seen-vs-unseen move |
| Named-figure intro | "Friedrich Hayek won the 1974 Nobel in Economics. His central insight was that no central planner has something every voluntary market produces automatically. What is it?" → "The price signal that encodes dispersed local knowledge" | Named figure + signature insight |
| Bitcoin primer | "On Halloween 2008, an anonymous person named Satoshi Nakamoto posted a nine-page paper to a cryptography mailing list. What had he invented?" → "Bitcoin — digital money that needs no central issuer" | Named event + dramatic anonymity |
| Hyperinflation primer | "In 1923 Weimar Germany, a loaf of bread cost about 200 BILLION marks. People burned money for heat because it was cheaper than firewood. What had happened to the German currency?" → "The government had printed so much money that prices doubled almost daily" | Vivid hyperinflation moment |
| Sound-money primer | "An old gold coin found in an attic still buys roughly the same amount of food today as 100 years ago. A US dollar from 100 years ago buys about 3 cents' worth. What's the difference?" → "Gold can't be printed; dollars can" | Sound-vs-fiat in one comparison |

### T2 patterns (grade 6 — one scene + named figure + action/argument)

Length cap: ≤ 480.

| Pattern | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bastiat method | "In 1850 the French economist Frédéric Bastiat published 'That Which Is Seen and That Which Is Not Seen.' He used a broken-window example to show the difference between the visible benefit (work for a glazier) and the invisible cost (everything else the shopkeeper would have bought). What is this method called?" → "The seen-and-unseen distinction — the foundational lesson of folk economics" | Bastiat as named teacher |
| Bitcoin moment | "The very first Bitcoin block, mined on January 3, 2009, contained a hidden message from Satoshi: 'The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks.' Why did Satoshi include that headline?" → "To timestamp the chain AND to comment on the bank bailouts Bitcoin was designed to make unnecessary" | Genesis block + Times headline |
| Hayek vs Keynes | "In 1931 the LSE invited the Austrian economist Friedrich Hayek to give four lectures on capital and money. The lectures were a direct intellectual challenge to John Maynard Keynes, who was at Cambridge. What was the central Austrian critique of Keynes's *Treatise on Money*?" → "Keynes ignored the structure of capital — money isn't just an aggregate but flows through specific stages of production" | Named debate + intellectual move |
| Public choice | "James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock published *The Calculus of Consent* in 1962. Their idea sounds simple but transformed political economy: politicians and bureaucrats have the SAME motivations as the rest of us — they pursue their own interests, not 'the public good.' What is this field called?" → "Public choice theory — economics applied to politics" | Named founding work + insight |
| Hyperinflation case | "Zimbabwe's central bank issued a 100-trillion-dollar note in 2008. By 2009, that note bought about a loaf of bread. Why does this pattern repeat across history?" → "Governments that can print money always eventually print too much — fiat currencies die" | Specific note denomination + cycle |

### T3 patterns (grade 7 — scene + stakes + economic mechanism or moment)

Length cap: ≤ 680.

| Pattern | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mises calculation | "In 1920 Ludwig von Mises published 'Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth.' Mises argued that socialism wasn't merely inefficient — it was IMPOSSIBLE. Without private ownership of capital goods, there are no market prices for them. Without prices, planners have no way to compare alternative uses. What's the recognition?" → "Socialism cannot allocate resources rationally because it destroys the price system economic calculation requires" | Named paper + named argument + named year |
| ABCT cycle | "The Austrian Business Cycle Theory predicts that artificially low interest rates set by a central bank cause businesses to invest in projects that look profitable at the low rate but become losers when rates normalize. Hayek's *Prices and Production* (1931) is the canonical statement. What real-world boom does ABCT cleanest explain?" → "The 2008 US housing bubble — the Fed held rates at 1% from 2003-2004, sparking a housing boom that collapsed when rates rose" | Named theory + named prediction + real event |
| Genesis block deep | "The Bitcoin Genesis block was mined January 3, 2009, with a coinbase reward of 50 BTC that is provably unspendable — the public key was generated in a way that no private key can ever spend the coins. Hal Finney received the first Bitcoin transfer on January 12, 2009 (block 170). Why does the unspendable Genesis matter?" → "The supply curve is provably accurate — no founder can spend the first coins; 'no immaculate conception' criticism is defused at block zero" | Specific block heights + named recipient + technical detail |
| Cantillon effect | "Richard Cantillon, an 18th-century banker who knew John Law personally, observed that new money entering an economy doesn't lift all prices equally at once. The first recipients buy at OLD prices; by the time the money has rippled out, prices have risen — and late recipients are net losers. What's the modern significance?" → "Cantillon effect: monetary expansion is a wealth transfer from late recipients (savers, wage earners) to early recipients (banks, government contractors, asset holders)" | Named figure + named observation + modern relevance |
| Sound money arc | "From the Lydian gold coins of ~600 BC through the 1971 Nixon shock, money was historically gold or silver. On August 15, 1971, Nixon went on TV on a Sunday night and 'closed the gold window' — dollars could no longer be exchanged for gold by foreign governments. Why did Nixon choose Sunday night for the announcement?" → "So global markets couldn't react before Monday's open — a deliberate maneuver to lock in the dollar-system change before traders could respond" | Sweep of history + dramatic detail + recognition |

### T4 patterns (grade 8 — setup + tension + named details + payoff)

Length cap: ≤ 900.

| Pattern | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Jekyll Island | "In November 1910, six men boarded a private rail car at Hoboken, New Jersey, headed to Jekyll Island, Georgia. The participants — Senator Nelson Aldrich, JP Morgan banker Henry Davison, Paul Warburg of Kuhn Loeb, Frank Vanderlip of National City Bank, A. Piatt Andrew of the Treasury — used only first names to keep the meeting secret from porters and reporters. What did they draft?" → "The Aldrich Plan, the blueprint that became the Federal Reserve Act of 1913" | Named conspiracy + named conspirators + dramatic detail |
| MMT falsified | "Stephanie Kelton's *The Deficit Myth* (2020) became Modern Monetary Theory's most influential popular statement. Its central claim: a government that issues its own currency cannot 'run out of money' the way a household can, and deficits are not inherently constrained except by inflation. The 2021-2023 US inflation reached 9.1% (June 2022 peak) after pandemic-era $4-5 trillion in deficit spending. What did this period demonstrate about MMT's central claim?" → "When the constraint MMT acknowledged (inflation) actually arrived, it arrived larger, faster, and more persistent than MMT's framework predicted — falsifying its policy implications in real time" | Named author + named book + named claim + falsified-by-event |
| ABCT 2008 | "The Austrian Business Cycle Theory predicts boom-bust cycles caused by central-bank credit expansion below the natural interest rate. The Federal Reserve held the federal funds rate at 1% from June 2003 to June 2004, then raised it gradually to 5.25% by mid-2006. US housing prices peaked in 2006, then collapsed; Bear Stearns failed March 2008; Lehman Brothers failed September 15, 2008. What does ABCT say the Fed's 2003-04 rate policy caused?" → "Artificial credit expansion drove malinvestment into housing — the 2008 GFC was the inevitable correction" | Named theory + named Fed action + named consequences |
| Bitcoin technical | "Bitcoin's difficulty adjustment recalculates every 2016 blocks (~2 weeks) to target an average block time of 10 minutes. If miners add hashrate, blocks come faster than 10 minutes, and the next adjustment makes the cryptographic puzzle harder. If hashrate drops (e.g., China's mining ban May 2021), blocks come slower, and the next adjustment makes it easier. Why is this self-regulating mechanism essential?" → "It guarantees the issuance schedule — 21M cap, halvings every 210K blocks — regardless of how much hashrate is on the network; supply policy is determined by code, not by humans" | Specific numbers + named event + technical recognition |
| Communist atrocity | "The Black Book of Communism (1997) estimated communist regimes killed approximately 65-100 million people in the 20th century: USSR ~20M (gulag + Holodomor), China ~65M (Great Leap Forward famine ~30-45M + Cultural Revolution), Cambodia ~2M (Khmer Rouge ~25% of population), North Korea, Vietnam, Eastern Europe, Cuba, Ethiopia. Mises had explained in 1920 why this would happen. What was his explanation?" → "Without private property in capital goods, prices cannot form; without prices, no rational allocation; the result is shortages, famines, and the political violence required to enforce arbitrary central plans" | Named atrocity ledger + named explanation |

### T5 patterns (grade 9-10 HARD CEILING — deep argument + history + intellectual move + consequence)

Length cap: ≤ 1100. Stays grade-10. No college-level math.

| Pattern | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hayek knowledge | "Hayek's 1945 *American Economic Review* paper 'The Use of Knowledge in Society' is one of the most-cited essays in 20th-century economics. Its argument: the economic problem is NOT how to allocate given resources — it's how to use knowledge no individual possesses. Prices encode dispersed, tacit, local information that no central planner can collect, much less compute on. The price system is, in Hayek's phrase, 'a marvel.' What broader political-economic conclusion follows?" → "Central planning fails not because planners aren't smart enough — but because the information they'd need doesn't exist in any centralizable form; the failure is epistemic, not technical" | Named paper + canonical argument + broader implication |
| Bitcoin philosophy | "Bitcoin solves a problem the cypherpunks had worked on for decades — David Chaum's DigiCash (1989) failed because it required a central issuer; Adam Back's Hashcash (1997) introduced proof-of-work; Wei Dai's b-money (1998) and Nick Szabo's bit gold (2005) proposed decentralized cash without solving the double-spend problem. Satoshi's 2008 paper combined proof-of-work + a publicly-shared ledger + difficulty adjustment to solve the Byzantine Generals Problem for money. Why is the Genesis block's January 3, 2009 timestamp + 'Chancellor on brink' headline philosophically significant?" → "It anchors Bitcoin's existence to the moment of bank bailouts that proved the case for credibly-neutral money — the unforgeable timestamp and the editorial commentary are inseparable" | Sweep + technical + philosophical |
| Fed history deep | "The Federal Reserve System was created by the Federal Reserve Act, signed by Woodrow Wilson on December 23, 1913. The Aldrich Plan, drafted at the secret 1910 Jekyll Island meeting, became (lightly modified) the Glass-Owen Act. Friedman & Schwartz's *A Monetary History of the United States 1867-1960* (1963) argued the Fed CAUSED the Great Depression by allowing the money supply to contract by ~33% between 1929 and 1933. What's the broader recognition about a central bank's track record?" → "An institution founded to prevent panics ended up causing the largest economic catastrophe in US history — and continues to be defended by the profession it employs" | Named Act + named work + named magnitude + institutional critique |
| Public choice deep | "James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock's *The Calculus of Consent* (1962) applied economic methodology — rational self-interest, marginal analysis — to political institutions. Their conclusion: politicians, bureaucrats, and voters are self-interested actors, not benevolent guardians of the public interest. Tullock's later work on rent-seeking (1967) quantified the social cost of competition for political privilege. Buchanan won the 1986 Nobel. Why is this recognition load-bearing for everything else in economics?" → "Once you see politics as self-interested, the 'market failure → government intervention' argument collapses — you have to compare imperfect markets to imperfect governments, and the government side often performs worse" | Named work + named scholars + Nobel + broader load-bearing recognition |
| Communism + Mises | "The 20th century ran a vast natural experiment on Mises's 1920 prediction. The USSR (1922-1991), Mao's China (1949-1976), Khmer Rouge Cambodia (1975-79), North Korea (1948-present), Cuba (1959-present), and Eastern Europe (1945-1991) all attempted central economic planning. The Black Book of Communism (1997) tallies the human cost: ~65-100M deaths from famine, gulag, political violence, ideological purge. Mises had said this would happen in 1920. What did he say, and what does the historical record show?" → "Mises said economic calculation under socialism is impossible — without prices, no rational allocation; the resulting shortages and famines required political violence to administer, and that violence is what the ledger counts" | Named experiment + named ledger + Mises 1920 vindicated |

## §2 Choice-shape conventions

### Length parity

Economics is in `ANSWER_OUTLIER_SUBJECTS`. The 1.6× answer-outlier flex applies — a longer mechanism-explanation answer can legitimately exceed distractor lengths by up to 1.6×.

Distractors should still be in approximate parity with each other (1.30 max/min ratio).

### Dash-shape parity

All four choices share dash usage (all use em-dash, or none). Skim-tell failure.

### Distractor plausibility

- **Austrian doctrine**: distractors are real adjacent positions from rival schools (Keynesian, Chicago/monetarist, Marxist, Walrasian general-equilibrium). For Mises 1920 calculation problem, distractors include labor theory of value, Marshallian partial equilibrium, monetarist quantity theory.
- **Bitcoin technical**: distractors are real adjacent crypto concepts that are wrong for THIS question. For proof-of-work, distractors include proof-of-stake (Ethereum post-Merge), proof-of-authority (sidechains), proof-of-history (Solana).
- **Central banking history**: real adjacent events (Aldrich Plan vs Glass-Owen, Bank of US history, etc.)
- **Hyperinflation cases**: other real hyperinflation cases (kid learns there have been many — Weimar 1923, Hungarian pengo 1946, Yugoslav dinar 1993-94, Zimbabwe 2008, Venezuela 2017+)
- **Named figures**: figures in adjacent positions (Mises / Hayek / Rothbard for Austrian; Keynes / Krugman / Stiglitz for Keynesian)

Banned distractor types:
- Made-up scientists, journals, dates
- "Both Keynesian and Austrian are valid" false-balance distractors
- Cartoon-villain framings ("the Fed wants to enslave humanity")
- Joke distractors that don't test anything
