# Grammar Templates (v2)

Per-tier approved stem patterns, the choice-shape conventions, and the
anti-patterns. The framework (`GRAMMAR_FRAMEWORK.md`) explains the WHY;
this document gives the WHAT.

Authoring rule: every new grammar question should match an approved
template AND the Comma-Saves-Lives Pattern (`GRAMMAR_FRAMEWORK.md` §1).
When a question doesn't fit a template, either:

1. Refactor it to a template, OR
2. Propose the template as a new entry here (with rationale, in a PR
   that updates this file too).

## §1 Approved stem patterns by tier

### T1 patterns (grades 1–3, vocab-teaching)

Each T1 pattern teaches a category by showing it. Length ≤ 600 total.

| Pattern | Example stem | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Identify-by-example | "In 'The cat sat on the mat,' which word is the noun(s)?" | Sentence in quotes; "the noun(s)" parenthesizes the singular/plural form |
| Definition-with-example | "A noun names a person, place, thing, or idea. Which word is a noun in 'Sarah ran to school'?" | Definition + example sentence — vocab teaching invariant satisfied |
| Choose-correct-form | "Which is correct: 'a apple' or 'an apple'?" | Choice between two forms, often punchline-producing |
| Saves-lives punchline | "Which version means we're inviting Grandma to dinner, not eating her?" | Choice text shows both versions; humor IS the hook |
| Sentence-type-by-end-mark | "Which kind of sentence is this: 'Watch out!'?" | Maps end-mark to type (declarative/interrogative/imperative/exclamatory) |

### T2 patterns (grades 3–5, agreement + homophones + basic etymology)

| Pattern | Example stem | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Subject-verb agreement | "Pick the correct verb: 'The team ___ (is / are) playing well.'" | Often punchline-producing — wrong agreement sounds awful |
| Homophone choice | "Choose the right form: 'They're / Their / There going to ___ house.'" | The classic three-way confusion |
| Apostrophe choice | "Which is correct: 'Its raining' or 'It's raining'?" | Contraction vs possessive |
| FANBOYS identification | "Which word is the coordinating conjunction in 'Sarah ran, but Tom walked'?" | Names the category implicitly through the example |
| Word-origin (basic) | "The word 'sandwich' is named after a person. What kind of person?" | Concrete etymology, kid-accessible |
| Loanword-source (basic) | "Which language gave English the word 'pajamas'?" | Concrete etymology; common loanword |
| Pun/play-on-words identification | "What kind of word play is in 'Time flies like an arrow; fruit flies like a banana'?" | Names the category by showing it |
| Idiom-meaning (basic) | "What does the idiom 'break a leg' mean?" | Common idioms, kid-accessible |

### T3 patterns (grades 6–8, clauses + advanced punctuation + figurative)

Length ≤ 750 total. Multi-sentence stems begin to appear.

| Pattern | Example stem | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Clause classification | "In 'When the rain stopped, we went outside,' which is the dependent clause?" | Sentence in quotes; standard clause-identification |
| Compound-vs-complex | "Which sentence is complex (one independent + at least one dependent clause)?" | The choices ARE four sentences |
| Punctuation rule | "Which punctuation mark joins two related independent clauses without a coordinating conjunction?" | Category recall (T3 OK) |
| Dangling-modifier funny | "'Walking down the street, the trees were beautiful.' What's wrong with this sentence?" | Punchline lives in the absurdity |
| Lay-vs-lie / who-vs-whom | "Pick the correct form: 'The book is ___ (laying / lying) on the table.'" | Inflectional paradigm |
| Figurative-language identification | "'The wind whispered through the trees' is an example of which figure?" | Names the figure by showing it |
| Meaning-shift | "'Nice' once meant something quite different. What was the original meaning?" | Etymology + semantic change |
| Active-vs-passive identification | "Which sentence is in the passive voice?" | The choices ARE four sentences |
| Common-confusable | "Which is correct: 'fewer than ten people' or 'less than ten people'?" | T2-T3 overlap; T3 versions are subtler |

### T4 patterns (grades 9–10, multi-sentence setup)

Length ≤ 950 total. Multi-sentence etymology / grammarian-history /
parallel-structure setups are the norm.

| Pattern | Example stem | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Etymology multi-sentence | "The word 'sincere' is sometimes claimed to come from Latin 'sine cera' meaning 'without wax,' from sculptors hiding cracks with wax. The truth is more boring: it comes from 'sincerus' meaning 'clean, pure.' Which sentence about the word's history is correct?" | Multi-sentence setup; busts a popular myth |
| Grammarian-attribution | "Bishop Robert Lowth in 1762 declared splitting infinitives improper, importing the rule from Latin (where infinitives are single words and can't be split). Which of these 'rules' did Lowth or his contemporaries impose, despite Latin grammar being the only justification?" | Multi-sentence; teaches the prescriptive tradition |
| Subjunctive-mood | "'If I ___ rich, I'd buy a house.' Which form is the subjunctive (counterfactual)?" | Tense + mood paradigm |
| Parallel-structure | "Which sentence has correct parallel structure?" | The choices ARE four sentences; one is parallel |
| Style-guide contrast | "The Associated Press Stylebook drops the Oxford comma; the Chicago Manual of Style keeps it. Which sentence demonstrates why the Chicago position can prevent ambiguity?" | Multi-sentence; introduces named style guides |
| Modifier-placement | "Move the modifier in the following sentence to fix the ambiguity: 'She only ate the cake at the party.'" | The fix changes meaning |
| Active-vs-passive rhetoric | "Which version is more direct (active voice)?" | Two valid sentences; one is rhetorically stronger |
| Etymology-from-Norman-conquest | "After 1066, French became the language of English nobility; English-speaking farmers kept their Old English words for animals while French-speaking nobles used French words at table. This gave English the cow/beef, pig/pork, sheep/mutton split. Which other pair shows this pattern?" | Multi-sentence; teaches the 1066 vocabulary doubling |

### T5 patterns (grades 11+, register + rhetorical figures + advanced syntax)

Length ≤ 1000 total. Often shorter than T4 because the concept is
named-figure recognition.

| Pattern | Example stem | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rhetorical-figure identification | "'I came, I saw, I conquered.' Which rhetorical figure does Caesar use?" | Named figures (asyndeton, anaphora, tricolon, polysyndeton, chiasmus, etc.) |
| Gerund-vs-participle | "In 'Running is good exercise,' what is 'running'?" | Verbals (gerund / present participle / infinitive) |
| Restrictive-vs-non-restrictive | "Which sentence uses a restrictive relative clause (no commas, essential to meaning)?" | The choices ARE four sentences |
| Modality (epistemic vs deontic) | "'You must be tired after that long flight.' What kind of modality is this?" | Epistemic (knowledge) vs deontic (obligation) vs dynamic (ability) |
| Conditional-type | "'If I had known, I would have called.' What type of conditional is this?" | Types 0 / 1 / 2 / 3 / mixed |
| Garden-path | "'The horse raced past the barn fell.' This is a grammatical sentence. Where does the parse break?" | Garden-path sentence anatomy |
| Buffalo-construction (rare) | "'Buffalo buffalo Buffalo buffalo buffalo buffalo Buffalo buffalo.' This grammatically valid sentence uses 'Buffalo' / 'buffalo' three ways. What are the three?" | One per bank at most; signature T5 |
| Register-match | "Which sentence is most appropriate for a formal business email?" | The choices ARE four sentences in different registers |
| Sentence-variety craft | "An essay opens with three short declarative sentences. Which fourth sentence would best vary the rhythm?" | Writing-craft, articulation-strong |

## §2 Choice-shape conventions

### Length parity

Grammar is in `ANSWER_OUTLIER_SUBJECTS` per `length_parity.py`. The
1.6× answer-outlier flex applies:

- If the correct answer is a punchline-bearing full sentence and the
  distractors are short alternatives, the 1.6× flex lets the answer
  legitimately be longer than the distractors.
- Distractors should still be in approximate parity with each other
  (1.30 max/min ratio among distractors).

### Dash-shape parity

All four choices share dash usage (all use em-dash, or none do). Same
rule as wonder subjects — kids spot skim-tells.

### Punctuation as content

When the question IS about punctuation, the choices may legitimately
differ in punctuation usage (that's the test). The dash-shape gate
exempts itself when the underlying question is `punct_*` (see template
naming convention).

### Distractor plausibility

Distractors must be:
- Real alternatives a learner might actually pick (not nonsense words)
- For inflectional drills (lay/lie, who/whom): the wrong forms in the
  paradigm
- For homophone drills: the other valid homophones
- For figure-identification: other named figures (not random words)
- For etymology: other plausible language sources, not "Klingon"

Banned distractor types (these were caught in 2026-05-19 audit):
- Made-up words for spelling questions (use real-but-wrong forms instead)
- Joke-only distractors that don't teach anything (no "they was speaking
  Pirate")
- Distractors that contradict an established fact in the same question
  (an etymology question whose distractors include impossible centuries)

## §3 Quotation convention for example sentences

When a stem refers to a specific sentence, the sentence must be in
single quotes OR double quotes — never bare. This is enforced by
`gate_example_sentence_quoted`.

Correct:
- "In 'The cat sat on the mat,' identify the noun(s)."
- 'In "She quickly ran to the store," what part of speech is "quickly"?'

Banned (the gate flags these):
- "In The cat sat on the mat, identify the noun(s)." (no quotes —
  ambiguous about where the example starts and ends)
- "What part of speech is the underlined word: She quickly ran."
  (no quotes — underlining doesn't reproduce in text-only display)

Exception: the question may use bold or italics in the choices to
indicate a target word, but the EXAMPLE SENTENCE itself must be
quoted.

## §4 Vocab-teaching pattern (T1–T3 invariant)

When a stem names a grammatical category, the question must teach the
category. Two acceptable patterns:

### Pattern A: Show by example (preferred)

```
stem: In "The cat sat on the mat," which word is the preposition?
answer: on
choices: on, cat, sat, mat
context: A preposition shows the relationship between a noun and the
         rest of the sentence — typically location, direction, or time.
         Common prepositions include: in, on, at, by, under, over,
         between, through, before, after.
```

### Pattern B: Define in context

```
stem: A preposition shows the relationship between a noun and the rest
      of the sentence. Which of the following words is a preposition?
answer: under
choices: under, run, happy, but
context: Prepositions in English include: in, on, at, by, under, over,
         between, through, before, after, despite, throughout. They
         often (but not always) signal location, direction, or time.
```

The gate `gate_vocab_teaching_at_t13` enforces this. T4–T5 stems may
relax the requirement.

## §5 Anti-patterns (do not generate)

- **Standalone category-recall below T4** without an example sentence
  or context definition. "What is a preposition?" with no anchor is
  banned at T1–T3.
- **Folk-etymology myths**: "rule of thumb" (wife-beating origin),
  "OK" (all-correct), "tip" (to-insure-promptness), "posh"
  (port-out-starboard-home), "sincerely" (without-wax), "golf"
  (gentlemen-only-ladies-forbidden), "SOS" (save-our-souls
  as origin not backronym), "Buick" (Bunker U.I.C.K.). These are
  popular but FALSE — never use them as correct answers.
- **Above-grade-10 linguistics theory**: Chomsky, Saussure, Sapir-Whorf,
  Bloomfield, Pāṇini, transformational grammar, generative syntax,
  deep/surface structure, morphosyntax, phonological theory,
  evidentiality typology, verb-valency taxonomy, critical-period
  hypothesis. Caught by `gate_no_above_grade10`.
- **Prescriptive verdicts on contested usage**: "ain't is wrong",
  "irregardless is not a word", "literally cannot mean figuratively".
  Show the convention (Strunk + Fowler positions) without verdict.
- **Mixed grammatical concepts per stem** below T4: a T1 stem should
  test ONE concept (e.g., parts of speech), not "parts of speech AND
  agreement AND tense". T4–T5 stems may combine concepts.
- **Choices that contradict the stem's example**: if the stem quotes
  "The cat sat on the mat", a distractor should not be "There is no
  cat in this sentence."
- **Naked sentence references** (see §3) — example sentences must be
  quoted.

## §6 Source materials

The strategies doc `docs/quiz/grammar_strategies.md` (2026-05-12)
defines ~140 strategy IDs (e.g., `pos_noun_basic`, `comma_changes_meaning`,
`gerund_vs_infinitive`) across the 5 pillars. Templates §1 above are
the STEM PATTERNS those strategies should follow — multiple strategies
can share a template.

When authoring a new question, the workflow is:

1. Pick the strategy (from the strategies doc) — what content is being
   taught?
2. Pick the tier (T1–T5 per the grade-band ladder, framework §2)
3. Pick the template (from §1 above) — what stem pattern fits?
4. Author the question following the template
5. Self-validate against the gate suite (`tools/quizgen/gates/grammar.py`)
6. Score the question against the Comma-Saves-Lives hierarchy
   (`GRAMMAR_FRAMEWORK.md` §1) — is it tier-1 / tier-2 / tier-3 / tier-4
   of the hierarchy?
