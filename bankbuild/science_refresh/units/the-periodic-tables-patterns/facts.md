## Groups: why a column shares its chemistry

F1. Elements in the same group (column) behave alike because they share comparable outermost (valence) electron configurations. The table has seven rows (periods) and 18 columns (groups); the alkali metals are group 1, the halogens group 17 and the noble gases group 18.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Periodic_table ; https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/2-5-the-periodic-table

F2. The alkali metals (lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, francium) each have a single valence electron and readily lose it to form a +1 ion. They are soft enough to cut with a knife and are stored under mineral oil; they are never found free in nature. Their densities are low: lithium 0.534, sodium 0.968, potassium 0.89 grams per cubic centimetre, all less than water's.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alkali_metal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metal

F3. All the alkali metals react with water, giving hydrogen gas and a hydroxide, and the heavier ones react more vigorously: lithium "often appearing as a slow fizzing", sodium "more rapidly with noticeable bubbling and heat" (Grokipedia); "potassium reacts violently with water at room temperature" and "caesium reacts explosively with water even at low temperatures" (Wikipedia). Reactivity rises down the group, lithium least and caesium most.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alkali_metal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium

F4. Why reactivity rises down the alkali metals: atomic radius increases down the group as electron shells are added, and "for larger atoms, the most loosely bound electron is located farther from the nucleus and so is easier to remove" (OpenStax); "the inner electrons partially shield the outer electrons from the pull of the nucleus." Grokipedia gives the cause as "decreasing first ionization energies and increasing atomic radii." Metallic radii: lithium 157, sodium 191, potassium 235, caesium 272 picometres.
    https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/6-5-periodic-variations-in-element-properties ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alkali_metal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atomic_radius

F5. The halogens (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine) have seven valence electrons and readily gain one electron to form a -1 (halide) ion, reaching a stable octet. The name is from Greek for "salt-former": they form salts with metals (2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl). At room temperature fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine a red-brown liquid and iodine a black solid.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Halogen

F6. Reactivity falls down the halogens: "fluorine is the most reactive element in the periodic table" (Grokipedia Halogen; Grokipedia Fluorine calls it "the most reactive element in the periodic table"), and the trend is put down mainly to increasing atomic size and weaker nuclear attraction for an incoming electron. A more reactive halogen takes the electron from the ion of a less reactive one (order F > Cl > Br > I). Fluorine has 9 protons and electrons in shells of 2 and 7.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Halogen ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fluorine ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Periodic_table

F7. The noble gases have "completely filled valence electron shells that confer high stability and minimal tendency to form chemical bonds" (eight outer electrons; helium two), and they have the highest first ionization energy in each period. They exist as single atoms (monatomic). Neon (atomic number 10, shells of 2 and 8, "a completely filled outer shell") forms no stable neutral compounds under ordinary conditions and does not even form Ne2 molecules; Wikipedia: neutral compounds of helium and neon "have not been formed."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Noble_gas ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Neon ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas ; https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/6-5-periodic-variations-in-element-properties

F8. "Almost never": reactivity rises down the noble gases with falling ionization energy. Neil Bartlett made the first noble-gas compound, xenon hexafluoroplatinate, in 1962, overturning the belief that they could form none; fluorine also forms XeF2, XeF4, XeF6 and the unstable KrF2.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Noble_gas ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fluorine

F9. Sodium has atomic number 11, configuration [Ne] 3s1 (shells 2, 8, 1); water "triggers a violent reaction." Group 16, the chalcogens (oxygen, sulfur, selenium, tellurium, polonium), have six valence electrons; tellurium is a brittle metalloid.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chalcogen

## Periods: how properties change across a row

F10. Across a period, atomic radius decreases from left to right because of increasing nuclear charge: each element adds a proton, and the extra electron goes into the same shell, which gives "minimal shielding from this increased attraction" (Grokipedia). Calculated radii fall from 190 pm for sodium to 99 pm for chlorine (about half), and from 167 pm for lithium to 38 pm for neon. Down a group radii increase as shells are added. OpenStax: the effective nuclear charge increases left to right across a period.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atomic_radius ; https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/6-5-periodic-variations-in-element-properties ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table

F11. Other row trends: first ionization energy generally increases left to right across a period and decreases down a group; metallic character decreases left to right and increases down a group.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Periodic_table ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table ; https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/6-5-periodic-variations-in-element-properties

## Mendeleev's table (1869-1871)

F12. Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the 63 known elements by increasing atomic weight, with similar elements in columns; the paper was presented on 6 March 1869 (22 February in Russia's Julian calendar) and read for him by his colleague Nikolai Menshutkin because he was ill. MYTH: that the table came to him in a dream; Grokipedia calls it "a persistent myth" and says the table came from years of arranging data.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dmitri_Mendeleev ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_periodic_table

F13. Mendeleev "deliberately left gaps in the table for undiscovered elements" and predicted them; his 1869 paper says "One can predict the discovery of many new elements, for example analogues of Si and Al with atomic weights of 65-75." In 1871 he named the gaps eka-boron, eka-aluminium and eka-silicon, eka (Sanskrit "one") meaning one place below the named element in its group.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/History_of_the_periodic_table ; https://web.lemoyne.edu/GIUNTA/EA/MENDELEEVann.HTML ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendeleev's_predicted_elements ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendeleev%27s_predicted_elements

F14. How he made the forecasts: "The core prediction method involved extrapolating properties from known analogs in the same group," with properties interpolated "between known elements" and drawn from "periodic trends observed in atomic weights and valencies of known elements in the group" (Grokipedia); eka-silicon was expected to have "properties intermediate between silicon and tin" (Grokipedia Periodic table).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendeleev's_predicted_elements ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Periodic_table

F15. Eka-aluminium (1871): atomic weight 68, density about 6.0 (Grokipedia's introduction says 5.9), a low melting point, oxide E2O3. Gallium, found by Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran in 1875 in zinc blende (sphalerite): atomic weight 69.72, density 5.91, melting point 29.8 degrees Celsius, oxide Ga2O3. Lecoq de Boisbaudran first reported a density of 4.7 and revised it to 5.9 after Mendeleev's critique; Wikipedia: Mendeleev "wrote to him and suggested that he should remeasure the density," and the second measurement matched the prediction. Named from Gallia (France).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendeleev's_predicted_elements ; https://grokipedia.com/page/History_of_the_periodic_table ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallium ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendeleev%27s_predicted_elements

F16. Eka-silicon (1871): atomic weight about 72, density about 5.5, a dioxide of specific gravity 4.7, a volatile chloride boiling below 100 degrees Celsius. Clemens Winkler found germanium in 1886 in the mineral argyrodite from Freiberg, Saxony (the mineral itself was found in 1885); he measured an atomic weight of 72.32; density 5.323; GeCl4 boils at 86.5 degrees (Wikipedia; Grokipedia gives 84). Winkler first took it for "eka-antimony" and "was soon convinced that it was instead eka-silicon"; Wikipedia's predicted-elements article says germanium "provided the best confirmation of the theory up to that time." Named for Germany.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Germanium ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendeleev's_predicted_elements ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendeleev%27s_predicted_elements ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanium

F17. Eka-boron: scandium, isolated by Lars Fredrik Nilson at Uppsala in 1879 (atomic weight 44.96 against 44 predicted; density 2.99 against about 3.0); Per Teodor Cleve recognised the match.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendeleev's_predicted_elements ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendeleev%27s_predicted_elements

F18. Effect: "These validations in the 1870s and 1880s dispelled skepticism among chemists who had questioned the table's predictive power" (Grokipedia); "Success of Mendeleev's predictions helped spread the word about his periodic table," and "By 1890, Mendeleev's periodic table had been universally recognized as a piece of basic chemical knowledge" (Wikipedia). Honest caveat: not every forecast came true; "Some other predictions were unsuccessful because he failed to recognise the presence of the lanthanides in the sixth row" (Wikipedia), and his tellurium weight was wrong (F19).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendeleev's_predicted_elements ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_periodic_table ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendeleev%27s_predicted_elements

F19. Tellurium and iodine: Mendeleev placed tellurium before iodine "despite tellurium's higher atomic weight (approximately 127.6 versus iodine's 126.9)" (Grokipedia). Giunta's notes on the 1869 paper: "Iodine is placed correctly after tellurium (i.e., with the halogens) despite having a lower atomic weight," and "Mendeleev was right to put tellurium in the same group with sulfur and oxygen." The 1869 paper itself says "Te cannot have the atomic weight 128, but rather 123-126" (a correction Giunta calls erroneous); Grokipedia says he proposed about 125. Creative Chemistry: "He had no experimental evidence for changing the atomic weight of tellurium from 128 to 125." Iodine behaves "akin to chlorine" (Gay-Lussac) and forms compounds like chlorine's and fluorine's (Davy).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/History_of_the_periodic_table ; https://web.lemoyne.edu/GIUNTA/EA/MENDELEEVann.HTML ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dmitri_Mendeleev ; https://www.creative-chemistry.org.uk/gcse/keyideas/periodic-table/periodic-table-development ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Iodine

F20. The weights hold: tellurium's standard atomic weight is 127.60; its commonest isotopes are tellurium-130 (34.08 percent), tellurium-128 (31.74) and tellurium-126 (18.84), with 78, 76 and 74 neutrons for its 52 protons. Iodine (atomic number 53, atomic mass 126.904) occurs as one stable isotope, iodine-127 (74 neutrons). Both sit in the fifth row (period).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_tellurium ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Iodine ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Periodic_table

## Henry Moseley (1913-1915)

F21. Henry Moseley, at Manchester under Rutherford from 1910 and at Oxford from 1913, fired cathode rays (electrons) at pure metal targets and measured their characteristic X-ray lines with a crystal spectrometer; at Oxford "he measured the Kα and Kβ lines for the elements from aluminum to silver (Z = 13 to 47)" (NIST). The square root of the X-ray frequency rises in nearly a straight line with atomic number (Moseley's law). He postulated that "each successive element has a nuclear charge exactly one unit greater than its predecessor" (Wikipedia). Before him, atomic numbers "had been thought of as a semi-arbitrary sequential number."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Henry_Moseley ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moseley ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moseley%27s_law ; https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/02/henry-moseley-and-periodic-table-elements

F22. Moseley's work made atomic number (the nuclear charge, later counted as protons; Rutherford named the proton in 1920) the basis of the order, not atomic weight. It resolved the three places where weight order was inverted: argon-potassium (18 and 19), cobalt-nickel (27 and 28) and tellurium-iodine (52 and 53). Antonius van den Broek had proposed in 1911 that an element's place matches its nuclear charge.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atomic_number ; https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/02/henry-moseley-and-periodic-table-elements ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_periodic_table ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Periodic_table

F23. The rare earths and the gaps: how many lanthanides there were was "a question that was very far from being settled by the chemists of the early 20th Century"; chemists "could not yet produce pure samples of all the rare-earth elements," could not always "distinguish between mixtures of two very similar (adjacent) rare-earth elements," and "didymium" proved a mixture of neodymium and praseodymium; ion exchange, which later separated them, did not yet exist (Wikipedia). Moseley showed the lanthanides, lanthanum through lutetium, "must have exactly 15 members – no more and no less" (Wikipedia; NIST). He showed gaps at atomic numbers 43, 61, 72 and 75 (later technetium, promethium, hafnium, rhenium), and with X-rays found element 61 absent from Georges Urbain's ore samples (NIST). IUPAC: he "concluded correctly that there were only 92 elements up to and including uranium."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moseley ; https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/02/henry-moseley-and-periodic-table-elements ; https://iupac.org/100/chemist/henry-moseley-hf/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Henry_Moseley

F24. Moseley enlisted in the Royal Engineers in 1914, against his family's wishes, served as a signals officer at Gallipoli from April 1915, and was killed by a sniper on 10 August 1915, aged 27. Rutherford believed his work would have earned a Nobel Prize; Svante Arrhenius had nominated him for the 1915 chemistry prize.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moseley ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Henry_Moseley ; https://iupac.org/100/chemist/henry-moseley-hf/

F25. The neutron was found in 1932 (inside-the-atom, T4); X-ray frequencies follow the nuclear charge, not the neutrons.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moseley%27s_law ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atomic_number

## Lord Rayleigh, William Ramsay and the noble gases (1892-1902)

F26. Lord Rayleigh found that nitrogen made from chemical compounds was "0.5% lighter than nitrogen from the atmosphere" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: about 0.5 percent, suggesting "an unknown heavier gas contaminating the atmosphere"). In 1892 he asked readers of Nature to explain the difference (Encyclopedia.com). Henry Cavendish in 1785 had been left with about 1 percent of air as an unreactive residue and did not pursue it.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Argon ; https://www.encyclopedia.com/science/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/ramsay-william

F27. William Ramsay passed nitrogen from the air over hot magnesium, which forms solid magnesium nitride, and a small amount of unreactive gas remained (Encyclopedia.com; search summary of the UCL history page: repeated passes until the residue would no longer react with magnesium). Rayleigh and Ramsay announced the gas on 13 August 1894 and named it argon, from the Greek argos, "lazy" or "inactive". It is about 0.934 percent of the air.
    https://www.encyclopedia.com/science/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/ramsay-william ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Argon ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon ; https://www.chem.ucl.ac.uk/resources/history/chemhistucl/hist14.html

F28. Argon had nowhere to go: its atomic weight of 39.9 sat "awkwardly" between chlorine (35.5) and potassium (39.1) (Grokipedia); the table "lacked a position for an inert gas of that mass" (Grokipedia Argon); its weight is greater than potassium's, yet "the inertness of argon suggested a placement before the reactive alkali metal" (Wikipedia). Mendeleev at first argued it was a form of nitrogen (Wikipedia History) and doubted "argon's status as a simple element" (Grokipedia Noble gas). Ramsay wrote that "it followed chlorine in the atomic table, and not fluorine."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/History_of_the_periodic_table ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Argon ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_periodic_table ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Noble_gas ; https://web.lemoyne.edu/GIUNTA/EA/RAMSAYann.HTML

F29. The column fills: Ramsay isolated helium from the mineral cleveite in 1895; Ramsay and Morris Travers found neon, krypton and xenon in 1898 by fractional distillation of liquid air; radon was found by Friedrich Ernst Dorn in 1900. "They realized an entire class of gases was missing from the periodic table" (Wikipedia). "Ramsay proposed that these elements be put in a new group between halogens and alkali metals," and Mendeleev added them as group 0 in 1902.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Noble_gas ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_periodic_table ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dmitri_Mendeleev ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Neon

F30. Ramsay used the table to forecast, as Mendeleev had: in his 1897 address he wrote, "There should, therefore, be an undiscovered element between helium and argon, with an atomic weight 16 units higher than that of helium, and 20 units lower than that of argon, namely 20," and that it should be "as indifferent to union with other elements as the two allied elements." Neon was found in 1898.
    https://web.lemoyne.edu/GIUNTA/EA/RAMSAYann.HTML ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Neon

F31. Rayleigh won the 1904 Nobel Prize in Physics for his gas-density work and the discovery of argon; Ramsay the 1904 Nobel Prize in Chemistry "in recognition of his services in the discovery of the inert gaseous elements in air."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Argon ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ramsay ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Strutt,_3rd_Baron_Rayleigh

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- Atoms get bigger across a row because they hold more electrons (F10: more protons pull the same shell in; a revision site names the electron-repulsion guess as the usual error, https://www.revisiondojo.com/student-faq/why-atomic-radius-decreases-across-a-period-832e6b14).
- More protons always means a stronger pull, so the bigger atom is the more reactive halogen (F6: fluorine, with the fewest protons, pulls hardest).
- Reactivity rises down every column (F3 versus F6: the alkali metals rise, the halogens fall).
- Heavier alkali metals sink in water (F2: lithium, sodium and potassium are all less dense than water).
- Noble gases are unreactive because their nucleus has no charge, or because they are paired molecules like nitrogen (F7: neon has 10 protons and stays single atoms).
- Noble gases can never react (F8: xenon compounds since 1962).
- Mendeleev ordered the table by atomic number, or dreamed it (F12, F21).
- A confirmed prediction proves a theory has no errors left (F18).
- Moseley's X-rays weigh atoms or count neutrons (F21, F25).
- Argon filled one of Mendeleev's gaps (F28: it fitted none).
