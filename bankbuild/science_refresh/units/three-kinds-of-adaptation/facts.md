# Three Kinds of Adaptation (T4, 8th grade, life strand)

Sources were read on 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; a second source is given
for anything a child could be taught wrong. No old-bank topic was quarried.
Map line G8.L3: inherited variation within a population leads, over
generations, to structural, behavioural and physiological adaptations.

## What an adaptation is, and the three kinds

F1. An adaptation is "a heritable trait that has been molded by natural selection", becoming "more prevalent in populations over generations" (Grokipedia). Adjustments made within one individual's lifetime are "non-heritable adjustments" and are not adaptations; National Geographic calls an individual's response to its conditions "acclimation".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Adaptation ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/adaptation/

F2. "Adaptations arise through mutations, or inheritable changes in an organism's genetic material" (EBSCO); individuals with helpful traits leave more offspring, so the traits grow more common over long periods. Natural selection involves no effort or intent, "Populations, not individual organisms, evolve", and selection "acts only on variation already present" (UC Berkeley, Understanding Evolution).
    https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/animal-adaptations ; https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/

F3. The 8th-grade standard TEKS 8.13C: "Describe how variations of traits within a population lead to structural, behavioral, and physiological adaptations" that raise the chance of survival and reproduction over generations. A teaching guide for it sorts them: structural = "physical body features like thick fur on an Arctic fox"; behavioural = "patterns of action like migration, hibernation, nocturnal hunting, or cooperative hunting in wolves"; physiological = "internal or chemical processes like a snake's venom".
    https://tea.texas.gov/sites/default/files/STAAR-Alt%20Curriculum%20Framework%20Biology.pdf ; https://keslerscience.com/teks-8.13c-adaptations-for-survival

F4. A functional (physiological) adaptation is "an internal body process or physiological mechanism that increases an organism's chances of survival" (Save My Exams glossary). Its examples: snakes "produce venom through specialised glands", an internal chemical process, "not a physical structure"; "Many desert animals have kidneys adapted to produce very concentrated urine", so less water is lost. EBSCO also counts structural adaptations as "parts of organisms", physiological ones as changes in metabolism or internal processes, and behavioural ones as actions.
    https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/biology/functional-adaptation/ ; https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/animal-adaptations

F5. Kangaroo rats (desert rodents): the kidneys make urine of "up to 6,000 mOsm/L, compared to a maximum of about 1,200 mOsm/L in humans" (about five times as concentrated); most of their water is metabolic water from the dry seeds they eat, and they can live on dry seeds with no drinking water. They are strictly nocturnal, and the daytime burrow "provides a cooler, more humid microclimate" (a behavioural adaptation). EBSCO: "They produce highly concentrated urine".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kangaroo_rat ; https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/animal-adaptations

F6. Rattlesnakes "primarily inhabit arid and semi-arid regions"; their colouring varies "for crypsis", tan, grey or brown grounds "overlaid with darker dorsal blotches"; the fangs are "elongated, hollow, tubular teeth" fed by "paired venom glands"; they hunt by "ambush predation ... prolonged immobility in cryptic positions", choosing sites "using chemosensory cues from prey feces or trails".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rattlesnake

F7. Pack hunters hunt cooperatively and can take prey that "would be difficult or impossible for a solitary individual to subdue" (wolves, lions, African wild dogs, orcas). Cooperative hunting is listed as a behavioural adaptation in F3.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pack_hunter ; https://keslerscience.com/teks-8.13c-adaptations-for-survival

## Mimicry: Henry Walter Bates and the Amazon butterflies

F8. Henry Walter Bates sailed with Alfred Russel Wallace to the Amazon in 1848 (reaching Pará on 28 May 1848); Wallace left in 1852 and Bates stayed until 1859, "nearly eleven years on the Amazon". He sent home over 14,700 species, mostly insects (about 8,000 new to science, by his own count). His mimicry paper, "Contributions to an Insect Fauna of the Amazon Valley", was read to the Linnean Society in 1861 and printed in its Transactions in 1862. Darwin called it "one of the most remarkable & admirable papers I ever read".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Henry_Walter_Bates ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Walter_Bates ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batesian_mimicry

F9. Bates's models were abundant, foul-tasting (noxious) heliconid butterflies with bold warning patterns; Bates "never saw the flocks of slow-flying Heliconidae in the woods persecuted by birds". His mimics were scarce, edible pierids (Leptalis, now Dismorphia), "not even closely related" to the models. Predators "have learned to avoid" the model's warning signals, and the avoidance carries over to mimics that look like it (Grokipedia: predators learn "after a bad experience"). This is Batesian mimicry: a harmless species resembling a harmful one.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Batesian_mimicry ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Henry_Walter_Bates ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Walter_Bates ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batesian_mimicry

F10. Mimicry works only while mimics stay rarer than their models: "if mimics outnumber models, predators may learn to target the imposters" (negative frequency-dependent selection). In Müllerian mimicry (Fritz Müller, 1878) two harmful species look alike and share the benefit.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Batesian_mimicry ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batesian_mimicry

F11. Bright warning colouring is the opposite of camouflage: Bates's unpalatable butterflies had "very striking coloration" and flew "almost as if taunting predators to eat them". Camouflage instead lets an animal "hide from predators or prey" (EBSCO).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batesian_mimicry ; https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/animal-adaptations

## Mimicry tested: the kingsnake models

F12. Scarlet kingsnakes are harmless; coral snakes are venomous. Both wear red, yellow and black bands; on a coral snake "the red and yellow rings touch", on a kingsnake a black band separates them.
    https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2001/03/010315075129.htm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Batesian_mimicry

F13. David Pfennig, William Harcombe and Karin Pfennig (Nature, 2001) made 1,200 life-size snake models of plasticine ("a mixture of wax and modeling clay"), ringed like kingsnakes, or striped, or plain brown, and set them out inside the coral snake's range (southern North Carolina and South Carolina) and north of it in central North Carolina, where coral snakes are absent. Bite and scratch marks recorded the attacks. The share of attacks that fell on ringed models was about 50 percent in central North Carolina against about 6 percent farther south; Arizona gave the same pattern. Predators readily attacked the ringed models only where no coral snakes lived. The result supported Bates's idea of 1862.
    https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2001/03/010315075129.htm ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/snake-study-supports-surv/ ; https://www.nature.com/articles/35066628 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Batesian_mimicry (replica experiments: up to 80 percent lower predation where coral snakes occur)

## Antifreeze in polar fish: Arthur DeVries

F14. Arthur DeVries went to McMurdo Station in Antarctica in 1961, fresh from Stanford, for 13 months studying the respiration of the local fish; the antifreeze proteins were discovered in fish he collected there while a Stanford graduate student (NSF; Scientia).
    https://scientia.global/?p=2586 ; https://nsf.gov/pubs/1996/nstc96rp/sb3.htm

F15. Polar seawater sits at about -1.9 degrees Celsius (the 1969 paper: about -1.87). The Antarctic fishes' serum froze at about -2 degrees Celsius, while temperate fishes' serum freezes at about -0.8 degrees Celsius, so ordinary fish blood would freeze in that sea.
    https://scientia.global/?p=2586 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antifreeze_protein ; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:5764871%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json

F16. DeVries and Donald Wohlschlag, "Freezing resistance in some Antarctic fishes", Science 163 (1969): salts, urea and free amino acids explained only about half of the serum's freezing-point depression; a protein containing carbohydrate (a glycoprotein) was isolated that accounted for about 30 percent. Scientia: "Additional salt content in the serum of the cold-water fish could only explain a portion of this difference."
    https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:5764871%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json ; https://scientia.global/?p=2586 ; https://doi.org/10.1126/science.163.3871.1073

F17. Antifreeze proteins "bind to ice crystals and inhibit their growth" by adsorbing to ice surfaces (adsorption-inhibition); the freezing point drops while "the melting point remains largely unchanged". Car antifreeze works differently, by the amount dissolved (a colligative effect). Ice crystals get into the fish with food and through the gills and have been found in the blood and spleen. The Arctic cods' antifreeze glycoproteins are near-identical to the Antarctic fishes' but evolved separately (convergent evolution).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antifreeze_protein ; https://scientia.global/?p=2586 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antifreeze_protein

F18. Freezing solid and thawing is a different, real strategy (freeze tolerance): a wood frog can have "65-70% of total body water frozen" as ice outside its cells, with heartbeat and breathing halted, and revives on thawing, glucose acting as a cryoprotectant.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wood_frog

## Temperature control by behaviour: Cowles and Bogert's lizards

F19. Raymond Cowles and Charles Bogert, "A Preliminary Study of the Thermal Requirements of Desert Reptiles", Bulletin of the American Museum of Natural History 83 (1944), covered the first four years of work from Cowles's field station near Indian Wells in the Coachella Valley, California, "a desert of extreme heat and aridity". They showed that desert lizards, usually called "cold-blooded", are not simply at the temperature of their surroundings: "by day they use behavioural mechanisms to achieve and maintain a preferred body temperature". When cool they move into and turn toward the sun; "When too hot, they move into shade and/or into burrows." They kept body temperatures "within a fairly wide range" by behaviour. They measured, for example, chuckwallas and sidewinders while recording their positions, and threw out 80 percent of their readings where the animal's prior activity was uncertain. Cowles proposed the terms ectothermic and endothermic.
    https://zoologyweblog.blogspot.com/2022/09/a-reminder-of-how-raymond-cowles-and.html ; https://journals.ku.edu/iguana/article/view/17574 ; https://people.wku.edu/charles.smith/chronob/COWL1896.htm ; https://biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/89358

F20. Reptiles are ectotherms: body temperature "primarily determined by environmental conditions rather than internal metabolic heat production"; they "regulate body temperature primarily through behavioral means, such as basking in sunlight or seeking shade", or burrowing; their metabolic rates are about 10 percent of endotherms' of similar size. Lizards "shuttle between sunlit basking sites and shaded retreats". Reptile skin is dry and scaly with no sweat glands.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Reptile ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ectotherm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermoregulation ; https://www.scmp.com/article/265058/dry-and-scaly-skin-means-living-hot-climates-no-sweat-reptiles

F21. Endotherms (birds and mammals) control temperature physiologically: they make heat from food (metabolic heat; ectotherms need "10 to 20 times less" food); shivering, "an involuntary physiological response" found in mammals and birds, makes heat; sweating and panting cool by evaporation; blood vessels in the skin widen or narrow. Human core temperature is about 37 degrees Celsius.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermoregulation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Shivering ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ectotherm ; https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/animal-adaptations

## Inherited variation becomes an adaptation: the rock pocket mice

F22. Rock pocket mice of the American Southwest mostly have light, tawny fur matching desert rock, but "darker coloured rock pocket mice are found living amid black, basaltic rock formations"; on the lava flows the mice are nearly all black, with white bellies. Owls are the main predators; most of their predators hunt by sight, so mice that stand out on their background are more likely to be eaten.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_pocket_mouse ; https://media.hhmi.org/biointeractive/interactivevideo/pocketmousequiz/videos/natural_selection.vtt

F23. Michael Nachman, Hopi Hoekstra and Susan D'Agostino (PNAS, 2003) found "four mutations in the melanocortin-1-receptor gene, Mc1r" behind the dark coats in one lava-dwelling population; a dark population on "a different lava flow" showed no link to Mc1r, so "adaptive dark color has evolved independently in this species through changes at different genes" (convergent evolution). HHMI: the flows are "hundreds of miles apart"; mutations are "random and rare events", but natural selection is not random ("mutation is random, natural selection is not", Sean Carroll).
    https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1073/pnas.0431157100&resultType=core&format=json ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_pocket_mouse ; https://media.hhmi.org/biointeractive/interactivevideo/pocketmousequiz/videos/natural_selection.vtt

## An adaptation mismatched to a changed world: the snowshoe hares

F24. The snowshoe hare moults from a brown summer coat to a mostly white winter coat in autumn, and back in spring. "Hares initiate winter molting based on photoperiod cues" (Grokipedia); Zimova and colleagues (2014): moult timing is "presumably initiated by photoperiod" (day length), while temperature and possibly snow "may affect the rate of the change".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Snowshoe_hare ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3973274

F25. Mills, Zimova and colleagues (PNAS, 2013) watched hares through 3 years of widely varying snowpack: the start dates of the colour change did not vary (only the spring moult's rate did). Snow seasons are shortening, and they forecast the days of white hares on snowless ground will rise "four- to eightfold by the end of the century". Zimova et al. (2014), with nearly 200 hares at two Montana sites: "moult initiation dates are fixed across years of different snowpacks"; mismatched hares "did not modify their hiding behaviour"; adapting "will require natural selection on moult phenology or behaviour".
    https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22Camouflage%20mismatch%20in%20seasonal%20coat%20color%20due%20to%20decreased%20snow%20duration%22&resultType=core&format=json ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3973274

F26. Marketa Zimova, L. Scott Mills and Joshua Nowak (Ecology Letters, 2016) followed 186 radio-collared snowshoe hares in western Montana: mismatched hares had "weekly survival decreases of up to 7%", and individual hares vary a great deal in when they moult, variation that natural selection could act on. White hares on bare ground "stand out like lightbulbs against a dark background".
    https://usanpn.org/news/article/climate-change-means-snowshoe-hares-stand-out-lightbulbs-against-snowless-background ; https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/high-fitness-costs-of-climate-change-induced-camouflage-mismatch/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Snowshoe_hare

## A new behavioural adaptation: the blackcaps' route to Britain

F27. Blackcaps (small European songbirds): west of a migratory divide, central European birds "head southwest towards Iberia or West Africa" (Wikipedia); "Since the 1960s" increasing numbers of central European birds winter in Britain and Ireland, "driven by reliable garden feeders and milder winters" (Grokipedia). Discover: blackcaps rarely crossed to Britain before the 1950s, and no European blackcap had been recorded migrating northwest before 1960; one group "flies southwest to Spain", a smaller one "heads northwest towards Britain"; food on bird tables made Britain a good wintering place. The British winter population grew to several thousand birds.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Eurasian_blackcap ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_blackcap ; https://www.discovermagazine.com/british-birdfeeders-split-blackcaps-into-two-genetically-distinct-groups-3803 ; https://doi.org/10.1038/360668a0

F28. Peter Berthold, Andreas Helbig, Gerhard Mohr and Ulrich Querner, "Rapid microevolution of migratory behaviour in a wild bird species", Nature 360 (1992): blackcaps caught wintering in England were taken to Germany and bred in captivity; chicks from both groups "flew in the same direction as their parents even when raised in identical environments" (Discover), and both the English-caught parents and their young oriented toward England (American Birds). Crossed birds' young headed "about halfway between the routes of their parents". The direction of migration is "genetically determined" (Wikipedia). The paper concluded that the new route had evolved within the past 30 years, with selection favouring birds that winter about 1,500 km farther north.
    https://www.discovermagazine.com/british-birdfeeders-split-blackcaps-into-two-genetically-distinct-groups-3803 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_blackcap ; https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/nab/v046n05/p01082-p01083.pdf ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Eurasian_blackcap ; https://doi.org/10.1038/360668a0

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Organisms adapt because they try to, or because they need to ("Natural selection involves organisms trying to adapt"; "Natural selection gives organisms what they need"). (Berkeley)
M2. An individual evolves within its lifetime, and changes caused by its surroundings are passed on (Lamarck's inheritance of acquired traits). (Berkeley: lifetime changes "are not caused by changes in its genes")
M3. Structural and physiological are mixed up: an organ is a body part, so its working is called structural; Save My Exams stresses that venom is an internal process, "not a physical structure".
M4. "Cold-blooded" animals simply take the temperature of their surroundings (the view Cowles and Bogert overturned).
M5. Bright colours are camouflage; in fact warning colours make an animal easy to see (F11).
    https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/ ; https://www.savemyexams.com/glossary/gcse/biology/functional-adaptation/ ; https://zoologyweblog.blogspot.com/2022/09/a-reminder-of-how-raymond-cowles-and.html

## Left out, or not sure

- Hibernation: sources sort it differently (Kesler: behavioural; EBSCO: physiological), so it is neither a key nor a wrong choice.
- The lava flows' ages: HHMI says "about a thousand years old" for one flow; other material says thousands of years. Not used.
- Bates's exact model genera: Grokipedia names Heliconius, Wikipedia the heliconids and ithomiines. Questions say only "foul-tasting butterflies of one group".
- The 7 percent figure (F26) is "up to" 7 percent a week; it is given that way.
- Whether temperature nudges the hares' moult timing: a camera-trap study found some plasticity, especially in spring; the start is said to be set by day length "presumably". Temperature is not a wrong choice for that reason.
- Snowshoe hares appear in the T2 unit predators-partners-and-parasites for the lynx cycle, a different fact; their coat colour is not asked anywhere else.
