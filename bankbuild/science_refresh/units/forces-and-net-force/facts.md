# Forces and Net Force: fact sheet (T2, 6th grade)

Researched 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; Texas TEKS, NGSS, OpenStax, Oak National Academy (KS3 Year 7 and Year 8), the Science Learning Hub, the University of Illinois physics Q&A, Horizon Research (the ATLAST middle-grades force and motion assessment, built on AAAS Project 2061) and PBS Design Squad to corroborate and to fix the grade.

## Facts

F1 A force has both a size (its strength) and a direction; scientists measure its size in newtons, a unit named after Isaac Newton. https://grokipedia.com/page/Force ("A force is a vector quantity, characterized by both magnitude and direction"; "The newton was named in honor of Sir Isaac Newton for his foundational work on mechanics")

F2 Contact forces act only where two things touch: friction, the normal force, tension, an applied push or pull, and drag (air or water resistance). Gravity and magnetism (and electric forces) are non-contact forces: they act across a gap, without the objects touching. https://grokipedia.com/page/Contact_force ("A contact force is a force that arises from the direct physical interaction between two objects touching each other"; "Applied force refers to any direct push or pull exerted on an object through physical contact by an external agent"; "non-contact forces, such as gravitational or magnetic forces that act across distances through fields"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Force (contact forces "require physical touch between objects (such as friction, tension, normal force, and applied force)"; non-contact forces "act at a distance without direct interaction (such as gravitational, electric, and magnetic forces)"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-secondary-ks3/units/forces/lessons/the-different-kinds-of-force (Year 7: "Contact forces only act when objects are touching"; "Drag is a contact force that pushes against objects moving through liquids or gases"; exit quiz: "Gravitational forces act whether objects are touching Earth's surface or not"; magnetic force and weight marked as non-contact)

F3 The normal force is the push a surface gives an object pressing on it, at right angles to the surface ("normal" means perpendicular). For a thing resting on a level surface, the normal force pushes straight up exactly as hard as gravity pulls it down. https://grokipedia.com/page/Normal_force ("the component of the contact force between two objects that acts perpendicular to their interface"; "always directed away from the surface and normal"; "On a horizontal surface at rest, the normal force equals the object's weight"); https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-5-normal-tension-and-other-examples-of-forces ("The word normal means perpendicular to a surface"; a table "must supply an upward force equal to the weight of the load")

F4 Even a hard, solid surface pushes back: it is squeezed a tiny amount, too little to see, and pushes back like a squeezed spring. A soft surface (a mattress, a trampoline) only sinks more before its push matches the load. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-5-normal-tension-and-other-examples-of-forces ("the table actually sags slightly under the load"; "it will exert a restoring force much like a deformed spring"; "The table sags quickly, and the sag is slight so we do not notice it"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Normal_force ("effectively behaving like a restorative force from slight deformations in the materials"; "adjusts instantaneously to maintain contact")

F5 MISCONCEPTION: still, non-living things such as tables cannot push. David Brown and John Clement interviewed 21 high school students with no physics; 14 at first held that a table does not push up on a book resting on it. Their lessons bridged from a hand pressing a spring to a book on a bendy board, then to a book on a solid table. https://www.compadre.org/portal/items/detail.cfm?ID=2356 (Brown and Clement, AERA 1987: "the common misconception that static objects are unable to exert forces"; "Of the 21 high school students with no prior physics instruction who were individually interviewed, 14 initially maintained that a table does not exert a force upward on a book resting on it")

F6 A force diagram (free-body diagram) draws each force on one object as an arrow: the arrow starts at the object, its head points the way the force acts, and its length shows how strong the force is (a longer arrow, a stronger force). Gravity is drawn as an arrow pointing down from the object's centre. https://grokipedia.com/page/Free_body_diagram ("illustrating all external forces and moments acting on it as directed vectors or arrows"; "the arrowhead points in the direction of the force's action"; "The length of the force arrow is typically drawn proportional to the force's magnitude"; "Draw the weight vector as a downward arrow from the center")

F7 The net force is all the forces on an object added up, minding their directions: forces the same way add, forces opposite ways take away from each other, and with two opposite forces the net force points the way of the bigger one. An object's motion changes in the direction of the net force. https://grokipedia.com/page/Net_force ("the vector sum of all external forces acting upon it"; "forces in the same direction add positively, while opposite directions subtract"; "The direction of the acceleration matches that of the net force"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/balanced-and-unbalanced-forces (Year 7: "The resultant force is the sum of the forces acting on the object, taking into account their direction"; "When there are two forces acting in opposite directions, the resultant force acts in the direction of the bigger force")

F8 Balanced forces (net force zero) leave motion unchanged: a still object stays still, and a moving object keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line. Balanced forces do not mean the object is still. https://grokipedia.com/page/Force ("Balanced forces occur when the resultant force is zero, resulting in no acceleration; the object remains at rest or continues with constant velocity"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Net_force ("maintains constant velocity (either at rest or in uniform motion)"); https://horizon-research.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/FM-SA-User-Manual.pdf (sub-idea I: "If an object has constant speed in a straight line (or zero speed), then there is no net force acting on the object. This can occur either when the forces on the object are balanced or there are no forces exerted on the object"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/balanced-and-unbalanced-forces ("When forces are balanced there is no resultant force and no change in speed"; exit quiz, balanced forces: the car "could be stationary and remaining stationary" or "could be moving forwards at steady speed")

F9 Unbalanced forces change motion: a net force the same way as the motion speeds an object up, a net force against the motion slows it down (it keeps going the same way while it slows), and a net force from the side changes its direction. https://horizon-research.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/FM-SA-User-Manual.pdf (the assessment's idea: "an unbalanced force acting on an object changes the object's speed, or direction of motion, or both"; "If an object is moving faster and faster, then there is a net force acting on the object in the same direction as the motion"; "If an object is moving slower and slower, then there is a net force acting on the object in the direction opposite to the object's motion"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps2-2-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions (PS2.A: "if the total force on the object is not zero, its motion will change"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/balanced-and-unbalanced-forces (exit quiz: a car moving forwards with a bigger backward force "keeps moving forwards but slows down")

F10 A vehicle moving at a steady speed in a straight line has balanced forces: the total force forward equals the total force pushing back (friction and the air). https://horizon-research.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/FM-SA-User-Manual.pdf (sample item: a roller coaster moving forward in a straight line; the total force in the direction of its motion equals the total force in the opposite direction when "The roller coaster moves at a constant speed"); https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/1343-forces-and-speed ("Once the forces become balanced, your speed stays the same"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/balanced-and-unbalanced-forces (balanced forces: the car "could be moving forwards at steady speed")

F11 MISCONCEPTIONS about motion that middle-grades students hold (from the research list behind the ATLAST assessment): "If a body is moving, there is a force acting on it in the direction of the motion"; "Constant force produces constant velocity"; "Sustained motion requires sustained force"; "Moving objects have a force in them that keeps them going"; "Forces always act in the direction of motion". Oak's Year 7 lessons list "The direction of the (resultant) force shows the direction of motion" and "An object 'has' force". https://horizon-research.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/FM-SA-User-Manual.pdf (Figure 3); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/balanced-and-unbalanced-forces; https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-secondary-ks3/units/forces/lessons/the-different-kinds-of-force

F12 Friction acts along the surfaces, always against the motion (or the motion a push is trying to start). https://grokipedia.com/page/Friction (friction acts "always in the direction opposite to the impending or actual motion"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-secondary-ks3/units/forces/lessons/the-different-kinds-of-force ("Friction is a contact force that slows or prevents movement")

F13 Air resistance (drag) is the push of the air against a thing moving through it, and it grows as the thing goes faster. https://grokipedia.com/page/Drag_(physics) ("Drag is the resistive force exerted by a fluid on an object moving through it"; drag "is particularly prominent at higher speeds where it scales with the square of the velocity"); https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/1359-cycling-aerodynamics-introduction ("As speed increases, so does the opposing force of drag"; "Speed will always change until forces become balanced again"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/changing-the-speed-of-a-parachute (Year 8: "The drag force on a falling obejct depends on its speed and shape" [sic])

F14 A cyclist pedalling hard speeds up while her forward push is bigger than the forces against her; as she goes faster the air pushes back harder, until the forces balance and her speed stays the same. https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/1343-forces-and-speed ("When the forward forces are bigger than the opposing forces, you speed up (accelerate)"; "As you go faster, the force of air resistance pushing back on you increases"; "Eventually, the forces become balanced (the forward forces are the same size as the opposing forces)"; "Once the forces become balanced, your speed stays the same")

F15 A falling skydiver speeds up until the air's upward push matches gravity's pull; then the forces balance and she falls at a steady speed, her terminal velocity (about 53 metres a second spread-eagle). An open parachute lowers it to about 5 to 8 metres a second. https://grokipedia.com/page/Terminal_velocity ("the net force acting on the object becomes zero and it no longer accelerates"; "the downward gravitational force is exactly balanced by the upward forces of drag and buoyancy (if significant)"; "a human in a spread-eagle position reaches a terminal velocity of approximately 53 m/s (120 mph)"; "the canopy's large surface area and high drag coefficient dramatically lower terminal velocity to 5-8 m/s (11-18 mph)"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/changing-the-speed-of-a-parachute ("Objects falling through the air will reach a terminal velocity when the drag and gravitational forces are equal")

F16 When the parachute opens, the air's upward push suddenly becomes bigger than gravity's pull, so the net force points up; this slows her fall, but she keeps moving down the whole time and never goes up. As she slows, the air's push weakens until it matches gravity again. https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/125 ("The resultant (that is, the total) force on the parachutist's body right after the parachute opens is upwards"; "This does not mean he will move in the upwards direction"; "As the parachute slows down, the upward air friction force drops"; "So although the acceleration is upwards, it never leads to an upward velocity"); https://physicslens.com/misconception-skydiver-goes-up-when-parachute-opens/ ("The parachutist is always moving downward, but the parachute causes a very sharp deceleration")

F17 MISCONCEPTION: a skydiver shoots upward when her parachute opens. Films give this impression because the camera, carried by another skydiver, is still falling fast. https://physicslens.com/misconception-skydiver-goes-up-when-parachute-opens/ ("When a parachute opens, many people think the parachutist suddenly shoots upward"; "The camera-holder is still dropping rapidly, but the parachutist has slowed down"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/changing-the-speed-of-a-parachute ("Video of skydiving may lead pupils to think opening a parachute makes a skydiver 'shoot upwards'")

F18 A magnet pulls iron and steel toward itself across a gap, without touching them; its pull drops off quickly with distance. https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnet ("ferromagnetic materials such as iron, nickel, cobalt, and gadolinium"; electromagnets "lift and transport ferrous metals like steel scrap or billets"; "The interaction between magnetic poles follows an inverse-square law"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Contact_force (magnetic forces "act across distances through fields"); https://dcmp.org/guides/TID9868_2.pdf (Design Squad leader notes: "a magnet's strength drops off quickly with distance")

F19 A magnet set beside the path of a rolling steel ball pulls it sideways and bends its path toward the magnet, so the ball changes direction without anything touching it; PBS Design Squad's "Invisible Force" challenge has children steer a ball this way into a target off to one side, and the ball must pass close enough for the pull to bend its path. https://dcmp.org/guides/TID9868_2.pdf ("CHALLENGE: Design a setup so that when a steel ball rolls past a magnet, it changes direction and hits a target that's off to the side"; "instead of using gravity to steer a ball, they'll use a magnetic force"; "To change the path, the ball has to pass close enough to the magnet so the magnetic force affects the path of the ball"); https://www.pbs.org/video/design-squad-nation-invisible-force/ (the ball "tends to get attracted towards the middle of the magnet"; a rolling ball "always goes in a straight line unless a different force acts against it")

F20 MISCONCEPTIONS about gravity: that it exists because of the air, and that it is not a force because it does not touch what it pulls. https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/294-alternative-conceptions-about-gravity ("Students may also think that gravity exists due to the presence of air or the Earth's atmosphere"; "Gravity is not a force because it does not physically touch the objects it acts upon"; "there is no gravity on the Moon or in space because there is no air")

F21 The grade: Texas puts the five forces (gravity, friction, magnetism, applied and normal), net force from diagrams, and balanced or unbalanced forces in grade 6 (TEKS 6.7A, 6.7B); NGSS MS-PS2-2 (grades 6-8) asks for balanced and unbalanced forces and qualitative comparisons of forces; England teaches contact and non-contact forces, resultant force and balanced forces in Year 7 (ages 11-12) and parachutes and terminal velocity in Year 8. https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-26 (6.7A: "identify and explain how forces act on objects, including gravity, friction, magnetism, applied forces, and normal forces"; 6.7B: "calculate the net force on an object in a horizontal or vertical direction using diagrams and determine if the forces are balanced or unbalanced"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps2-2-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions ("Emphasis is on balanced (Newton's First Law) and unbalanced forces in a system, qualitative comparisons of forces"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/balanced-and-unbalanced-forces (KS3, Y7); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/changing-the-speed-of-a-parachute (KS3, Y8)

F22 Gravity is a pull between any two things that have mass; Earth's gravity pulls everything toward the planet's centre, which is straight down wherever a thing is, and it pulls on all matter, not only on metals. https://grokipedia.com/page/Gravity ("It acts as an attractive force between any two objects that possess mass or energy"; Earth's pull on an object, "pulling it downward toward the planet's center"); https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/what-is-gravity/en/ ("Gravity is the force by which a planet or other body draws objects toward its center"; "Earth's gravity is what keeps you on the ground and what makes things fall")

## Misconceptions a 6th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Still, non-living things cannot push: a table or a floor only "gets in the way" (F5, Brown and Clement). A related belief, the author's reading of the same research and not a quoted survey result: a harder surface pushes harder, or a surface that does not visibly sink does not push at all.

M2 A moving object needs a forward force bigger than the forces against it just to keep going at a steady speed; a steady push gives a steady speed (F11, Horizon Research list: "If a body is moving, there is a force acting on it in the direction of the motion"; "Constant force produces constant velocity").

M3 The net force points the way the object is moving, so an upward net force on a falling skydiver would send her up (F11 Oak: "The direction of the (resultant) force shows the direction of motion"; F17).

M4 An object holds or carries force that can run out (F11: "Moving objects have a force in them that keeps them going"; Oak: "An object 'has' force").

M5 Forces need touching, so a magnet or gravity cannot act across a gap; gravity is not a real force, or needs air (F20).

M6 Arrows in a force diagram show the motion (speed or distance) rather than the forces (F11 Oak: force direction confused with motion direction). The specific belief that an arrow's length shows speed is the author's reading of that misconception, not a quoted survey result.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F23. The floating paper clip: a steel paper clip on a thread taped down below a strong magnet hangs in mid-air, pulled toward the magnet but not touching it. Vanderbilt's kit: "Tape the string to the base of the ring stand or the table at a point where the paperclip is attracted to, but not touching, the magnet"; "the paper clip floats in mid-air because the force of magnetism travels through the air between the paper clip and the magnet". The University of Regina's notes tell the teacher to "Show the students that the thread is not holding the clip up", the belief a student brings to it. A thread's tension is "the pulling force that acts along the length of a flexible medium" and it "pulls outward on the objects it connects, never pushing" (Grokipedia), so the tight thread pulls the clip down toward the desk. The clip is still, so the forces balance (F7, F8): the magnet's upward pull equals gravity's pull plus the thread's pull, and so is stronger than gravity's alone.
    https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-URL/wp-content/uploads/sites/336/2023/07/28135345/Floating-_Paperclip_Kit_2023.pdf ; https://www.cmste.uregina.ca/Quickstarts/floatingpaperclip.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tension_(physics)

F24. Friction and the normal force act on still things as well as moving ones. Static friction is "the frictional force that acts between two surfaces in contact when there is no relative motion between them", "maintaining equilibrium in systems like a parked vehicle on an incline" (Grokipedia); the normal force holds up a book at rest (F3). So "acts only on moving things" and "always slows things down" do not sort contact forces from the others.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Friction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Normal_force

F25. The fact-check's correction to the skydiver's context: an unbalanced force against an object's motion does not always just slow it. A rock thrown straight up slows, its velocity is zero at its highest point, and then it moves downward (OpenStax), all under gravity. The parachutist is never turned round because the air's upward push drops as she slows, until it matches gravity again while she is still moving down (F16, Illinois: "As the parachute slows down, the upward air friction force drops").
