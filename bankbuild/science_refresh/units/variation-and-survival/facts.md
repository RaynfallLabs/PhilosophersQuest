# Variation and Survival (T2, 6th grade, life strand)

Curriculum line G6.L3 (TEKS 6.13C; NGSS MS-LS4-4). Sources read on 2026-11-26.
Grokipedia first; a second source for anything a child could be taught wrong.

## The standard

F1. TEKS 6.13C asks 6th graders to "describe how variations within a population can be an advantage or disadvantage to the survival of a population as environments change." Its examples of variation include fur colour, beak size, root length and running speed. NGSS MS-LS4-4 asks for an explanation of how "genetic variations of traits in a population increase some individuals' probability of surviving and reproducing in a specific environment."
    https://keslerscience.com/teks-6.13c-variations-survival ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ls4-4-biological-evolution-unity-and-diversity (clarification: "Emphasis is on using simple probability statements and proportional reasoning")

## Variation within one species

F2. Members of one species are not identical: they differ in size, colour, shape, speed, resistance to disease, and tolerance of heat, cold or drought. These differences among members of one species are called variations. Genetic variation is "the naturally occurring differences in the DNA sequences among individuals of the same species".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_variation ; https://keslerscience.com/teks-6.13c-variations-survival ("Some rabbits have darker fur, some lighter"; "Some plants have deeper roots, some shallower")

F3. A species is a group of living things that "actually or potentially interbreed in nature to produce fertile offspring" (the biological species concept): its members can mate and have young that can themselves have young.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Species

F4. A horse and a donkey can mate, but their young, the mule, is "almost universally sterile": the horse has 64 chromosomes, the donkey 62, and the mule an odd 63. So the horse and the donkey are two species.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mule ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Species ("their hybrids, mules, exhibit post-zygotic sterility due to chromosomal mismatches")

F5. Every dog breed belongs to one species, the domestic dog (Canis familiaris, now usually classed as Canis lupus familiaris); a breed is "a particular strain or variety of the domestic dog". Dogs of different breeds mate and have puppies (crossbred and mixed-breed dogs). Dogs range in size "from Chihuahuas under 3 kg to Great Danes over 70 kg", more body-size variation than in any other land mammal.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dog ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dog_breed

F6. Rabbits and hares look alike but belong to different genera, and "hares and rabbits do not successfully interbreed". Alligators (family Alligatoridae) and true crocodiles (family Crocodylidae) belong to different families.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hare ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alligator

## Most variation is inherited; some comes from the surroundings

F7. Much of the variation among members of a species is inherited: it is carried in the genes passed from parents to young. Some is not: the surroundings also shape how a living thing turns out ("environmental factors and gene regulation modulate how genetic differences manifest"), and many traits depend on both.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_variation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Phenotypic_plasticity ("the capacity of a single genotype to produce multiple distinct phenotypes" under "different environmental conditions")

F8. Hidden inherited variation: people differ in whether they taste the chemical phenylthiocarbamide (PTC) as bitter. The difference is genetic ("The genetic basis of PTC sensitivity resides in the TAS2R38 gene"); about 25% of people are nontasters, who "exhibit no perception of bitterness", so about three in four taste it. Paper strips soaked in PTC are a standard classroom tool for showing inheritance.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Phenylthiocarbamide

F9. The "tongue map" (separate zones for sweet, sour, salty and bitter) is a "scientifically discredited" myth: "all tastes can be detected across the tongue's surface". MISCONCEPTION used as a wrong choice.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tongue_map

F10. Identical (monozygotic) twins "originate from a single fertilized egg that divides into two separate embryos" and "share nearly 100% of their nuclear DNA". Fraternal twins come from two eggs and are no more alike in their genes than other brothers and sisters. Even identical twins differ a little: their fingerprints are "highly similar but not identical ... due to environmental influences".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Twin

F11. A plant grown from a cutting is "a genetic clone of the parent"; "cuttings produce genetically identical clones".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cutting_(plant)

F12. The flower colour of the bigleaf hydrangea (Hydrangea macrophylla) is set by the soil. In acidic soil (pH below about 5.5) aluminium dissolves and "is taken up by the plant roots", and the flowers are blue; in alkaline soil (pH above 6.5) the aluminium is locked up and the flowers are pink or red. Colour is fixed as the flower forms.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrangea_macrophylla

F13. A difference caused only by the surroundings is not passed on to the young. Wilhelm Johannsen, who coined the word "gene", grew pure lines of beans whose plants shared the same inheritance: within a line, bean size varied because of the surroundings ("non-heritable and likely environmental in origin"), and sowing only the biggest or only the smallest beans "produced no lasting heritable shifts"; the next generation's beans kept the line's usual average size.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wilhelm_Johannsen ; https://keslerscience.com/teks-6.13c-variations-survival (misconception: "Traits gained during life can be passed to your children")

## A change in the environment

F14. When the environment changes, a variation can become an advantage or a disadvantage. Kesler's example: "A lighter-colored rabbit might be hard to see in a snowy winter", an advantage, but the same rabbit "stands out to predators" on a dark summer forest floor.
    https://keslerscience.com/teks-6.13c-variations-survival

F15. Individuals with a helpful variation survive and have young more often than others, and their young inherit the variation, so over generations it becomes more common in the population. Berkeley's model: "some beetles are green and some are brown"; "green beetles tend to get eaten by birds and survive to reproduce less often than brown beetles do"; "The surviving beetles (more of which are brown) have offspring of the same color because this trait has a genetic basis"; "eventually, all individuals in the population will be brown."
    https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolution-101/mechanisms-the-processes-of-evolution/natural-selection/

F16. MISCONCEPTIONS (honest wrong choices). Living things do not grow a helpful trait because they need it: the change "does not involve effort, trying, or wanting", and it "cannot sense what a species or an individual 'needs'". "Populations, not individual organisms, evolve." The change works only on "the genetic variation in a population": if the helpful variation is not already there, the population may die out. Kesler lists four student misconceptions: "An animal adapts by changing itself when it needs to"; "Survival of the fittest means the strongest animal wins"; "Traits gained during life can be passed to your children"; "Helpful traits always appear when they're needed."
    https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/ ; https://keslerscience.com/teks-6.13c-variations-survival

## Real cases

F17. Hurricane lizards. Biologist Colin Donihue had just finished measuring the small anole lizards on two islands of the Turks and Caicos when Hurricane Irma struck in September 2017 (Hurricane Maria followed). Returning weeks later, the team found that the survivors "had larger toe pads on both their forelimbs and hindlimbs", proportionately longer front legs, shorter back legs, and "The survivor population had smaller bodies, too." In strong wind the lizards "lost hold with their hindlimbs first". A year later, "Lizards in the next generation had toepads as large as the survivors".
    https://source.washu.edu/2018/07/leggy-lizards-dont-survive-the-storm/ ; https://source.washu.edu/2020/04/hurricanes-twist-evolution-in-island-lizards/ ; https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-018-05821-7

F18. Beak size flips with the seeds. A thick beak does well when seeds are "large and tough" but struggles when only "small and soft" seeds remain (Kesler). On a Galápagos island in 1977, a drought "left only large, hard seeds", "finches with deeper beaks experienced significantly higher survival rates", and the offspring's average beak depth rose by 4-5%. After the 1983 El Niño rains "triggered a frenzy of small-seed plant growth", small-beaked finches ate the new seeds more efficiently and the average beak shrank "by 2.5% within a few years". After the 2002-2003 El Niño, "with abundant small seeds", selection again ran against large beaks.
    https://keslerscience.com/teks-6.13c-variations-survival ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Medium_ground_finch ; https://carlzimmer.com/darwins-avian-muses-continue-to-evolve/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Darwin%27s_finches

F19. Ash dieback is a disease of ash trees caused by the fungus Hymenoscyphus fraxineus; it has killed millions of trees, and European surveys found deaths as high as 85% in plantations and 69% in woodlands (scientists had once expected more than 95%). Genetic variation makes "some ash trees resistant to the disease", and "resistance to ash is heritable". In a Kew and Queen Mary study (Science, 2025) a new generation of ash in a wood "exhibits greater resistance to the disease compared to older trees". Caveat: "The existing genetic variation in the ash population may be too low" for full resistance from nature alone.
    https://www.qmul.ac.uk/sbbs/research/featured-research/genetic-resistance-brings-hope-for-ash-trees/index.html ; https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1088772

F20. Ash dieback kills young ash trees fastest, and old trees die of it too: "Young trees can be killed in one season and older trees tend to succumb after several seasons of infection."
    https://www.rhs.org.uk/disease/ash-dieback

F21. The Gros Michel banana "was, until the 1950s, the main variety grown" and "the dominant export banana to Europe and North America". In the 1950s Panama disease, "a wilt caused by the fungus Fusarium oxysporum f.sp. cubense, wiped out vast tracts of Gros Michel plantations in Central America", and exporters switched to the Cavendish, which resists that race of the fungus.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gros_Michel_banana ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Panama_disease ("Cavendish bananas (also AAA) demonstrate strong resistance to TR1")

F22. Farm bananas are clones. The Cavendish is "seedless and reproductively sterile" and is grown from suckers or tissue culture, which "produces genetically identical offspring". The Gros Michel's "clonal uniformity enabled Foc race 1 to collapse export plantations in the Americas", and today "all commercial clones share identical susceptibility" to a new strain, Tropical Race 4.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cavendish_banana

F23. Panama disease is "a soil-borne infection that invades the roots and spreads upward"; a plant collapses slowly, "within six to twelve months after initial symptoms", and the spores stay alive in soil "for up to 30 years". It attacks bananas; it is not a blight of every plant.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Panama_disease

F24. Why variation matters to a population: "Diverse gene pools buffer populations against stressors, reducing the likelihood of widespread decline." Crops grown as one uniform variety (monocultures) are "uniform populations susceptible to synchronized failure against pests, pathogens, or environmental stresses"; the Irish grew mainly the "Lumper" potato, "propagated vegetatively, which lacked diversity to withstand" potato blight.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_variation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_diversity

F25. Swallows and traffic. Cliff swallows "often nest under bridges and overpasses, which makes them susceptible to being killed by passing cars". Charles Brown and Mary Bomberger Brown, studying them in southwest Nebraska for 30 years (Current Biology, 2013), found that the birds killed on the road "disproportionately possessed longer wings"; Science News: swallows that died on the road had wings a few millimetres longer than the rest of the population. Over those 30 years road kills "plunged" and "the birds' average wing length has shrunk". "Shorter wings make for easier mobility", letting a bird "fly upward and pivot away from oncoming traffic". The team ruled out some other causes (the kinds of vehicles, scavengers), but Charles Brown has said wing length may not be the whole story.
    https://ianrnews.unl.edu/swallows-evolving-shorter-wing-lengths-thanks-messy-encounters-vehicles ; https://www.sciencenews.org/article/shorter-winged-swallows-evolve-around-highways ; https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013CBio...23.R233B

## Misconceptions a 6th grader brings (see also F9, F16)

M1. "An animal changes itself when it needs to" (the helpful trait appears on demand). Wrong: the variation must already be in the population (F16).
M2. "The strongest or biggest always survive." Wrong: the members that survive are the ones whose variations suit the new conditions; the hurricane survivors were smaller (F16, F17).
M3. "A trait gained in life is passed on." Wrong: only inherited variation is passed on (F13, F16).
M4. "Members of a species that look very different must be different species, and look-alikes must be one." Wrong: the test is breeding (F3-F6).
M5. "Different parts of the tongue taste different things." Wrong: a myth (F9).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F26. Hurricane lizards, the later survey and the authors' caution. Donihue and others, PNAS 117:10429 (2020): "We repeated those surveys 18 mo (April 1 to 8, 2019) after our initial posthurricane survey" (the first was 16 to 20 October 2017), so about a year and a half after the storms; young lizards that hatched after the storms had toepads "indistinguishable from that of the hurricane survivors", still larger than before the storms. Jonathan Losos (WashU, 2018): "hurricane-induced natural selection seems like the best explanation for these findings", but "We can't rule these possibilities out", for example "Perhaps the hurricane blew in lizards with bigger toepads and shorter hindlegs from another island". The grip finding comes from a wind test on survivors after the storms (the hindlimbs let go first), not from watching the storm.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7229758 ; https://source.washu.edu/2018/07/leggy-lizards-dont-survive-the-storm/

F27. Panama disease is the disease, not the fungus: "Panama disease, also known as Fusarium wilt, is a destructive soil-borne fungal disease", "caused by the pathogen Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense". Infection starts in the roots; a plant collapses "within six to twelve months after initial symptoms"; spread is mainly by soil, water, tools and planting material, while wind and animals "play only minor roles in long-distance spread". It was not new in the 1950s: "First documented in northern Australia in 1874" (the page also gives 1876), and "By 1890, TR1 had reached Panama"; the Central American epidemic "peaked between the 1940s and 1960s".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Panama_disease

F28. The finches after the rains of 1982-83: "At the end of 1982, heavy rains came to the islands"; "the plants with small seeds were particularly abundant"; "Now small-beaked birds had the advantage"; "They could eat small seeds more efficiently than the big-beaked ones", which let them grow faster and put more into raising young; "The average size of beaks decreased by 2.5%" (Carl Zimmer, National Geographic). Zimmer's blog: "Ground finches with small beaks were more efficient at eating the new seeds and had more offspring."
    https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/a-career-among-the-finches ; https://carlzimmer.com/darwins-avian-muses-continue-to-evolve/
