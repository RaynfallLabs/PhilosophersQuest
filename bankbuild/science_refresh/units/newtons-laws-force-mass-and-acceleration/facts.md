## The second law

F1. Newton's second law: the acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass; F = ma. (Grokipedia: "the acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass"; OpenStax: "directly proportional to and in the same direction as the net external force", and inversely proportional to mass.) So with the mass fixed, twice the net force gives twice the acceleration; with the force fixed, twice the mass gives half the acceleration; and to give three times the mass the same acceleration takes three times the net force.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-3-newtons-second-law-of-motion-concept-of-a-system

F2. The acceleration is in the same direction as the net force (OpenStax, F1). Newton's own wording: the change of motion "is made in the direction of the right line in which that force is impressed" (Grokipedia). A slowing object therefore accelerates against its motion, the way the net force on it points.
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-3-newtons-second-law-of-motion-concept-of-a-system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion

F3. The newton: "1 N is defined as the force required to accelerate a 1 kg mass by 1 m/s²" (Grokipedia, Newton's laws); OpenStax: "the force needed to accelerate a 1-kg system at the rate of 1 m/s2". Named after Isaac Newton; adopted in 1948 by the 9th CGPM. A small apple of about 100 g weighs about 1 N; 1 kg weighs about 9.8 N on Earth.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton_(unit) ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-3-newtons-second-law-of-motion-concept-of-a-system

F4. The same force gives a big mass a small acceleration: "the same net external force applied to a car produces a much smaller acceleration than when applied to a basketball" (OpenStax); "accelerating a lightweight baseball requires far less effort than accelerating a heavy truck at the same rate" (Grokipedia).
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-3-newtons-second-law-of-motion-concept-of-a-system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion

F5. The school trolley experiment: a trolley on a track, pulled by a measured force; to test force, the total mass is kept the same. Result: "The line of best fit passes straight through the origin, showing that force and acceleration are directly proportional". With a constant force, "as the trolley's mass increases, its acceleration decreases. Mass and acceleration are inversely proportional." Friction opposes the trolley; "using an air track will reduce the friction to almost zero". (A spring scale reads a pull in newtons: the T1 unit measuring-and-the-fair-test, and F3.)
    https://myedspace.co.uk/myresources/gcse/physics/aqa/revision-notes/force-and-acceleration-rp ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-3-newtons-second-law-of-motion-concept-of-a-system

## Falling together

F6. Weight is the gravitational force on an object, w = mg, so the pull on an object grows in step with its mass (OpenStax). "In the absence of air resistance, all objects fall with the same acceleration" (OpenStax); "the acceleration is the same for all bodies, independently of their mass", because "the body's mass m cancels from both sides of the equation" (Wikipedia).
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-3-newtons-second-law-of-motion-concept-of-a-system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion

F7. Apollo 15: at the end of the last moonwalk, on 2 August 1971, Commander David Scott dropped a geologic hammer and a feather together for the TV cameras. Joe Allen's report (NASA SP-289): "a 1.32-kg aluminum geological hammer" and "a 0.03-kg falcon feather" were "released simultaneously" from about 1.6 m; "the objects were observed to undergo the same acceleration and strike the lunar surface simultaneously". (The curator notes that 0.03 kg is too high for a feather; typical feathers are 0.0003-0.003 kg. Either way the hammer has many times the feather's mass.) NASA calls it a test of Galileo's idea that in a vacuum all objects fall at the same rate regardless of mass; the feather was named for the lunar module, Falcon.
    https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/lunar/apollo_15_feather_drop.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/resource/the-apollo-15-hammer-feather-drop/ ; https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/objects-of-intrigue-the-falcon-feather-on-the-moon

F8. Brian Cox, for the BBC's Human Universe (2014), dropped a bowling ball and feathers inside NASA's Space Power Facility near Sandusky, Ohio, the world's largest vacuum chamber; with the air pumped out they fell together under Earth's full gravity. So falling together does not need weak gravity, only no air.
    https://physicsworld.com/a/blockbuster-physics-bowling-balls-and-feathers-in-a-vacuum-and-more/ ; https://www.discovermagazine.com/watch-a-feather-and-bowling-ball-fall-at-the-same-speed-1199

## The rocket that gets lighter

F9. A rocket's mass falls as it burns fuel, so the same thrust gives more and more acceleration: "acceleration increases continuously, reaching a maximum just before the fuel is exhausted"; "The acceleration does increase steadily as the rocket burns fuel" (OpenStax). Wikipedia: rockets "expel mass during operation", so the mass being pushed "is constantly changing".
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-ap-courses-2e/pages/8-7-introduction-to-rocket-propulsion ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion

F10. Saturn V: the first stage had a fuelled mass of 2,214,000 kg, 33,000 kN of thrust at sea level and a burn of about 150 seconds. The hold-down arms let go once acceleration reached about 1.2 g; "As the Saturn V consumed fuel, its weight decreased, increasing its acceleration", so the crew felt 4 g at T+2 minutes 15 seconds; "To limit acceleration to about 4 g for crew safety as fuel mass decreased, the center F-1 engine underwent cutoff" (Grokipedia), lowering it to 3 g (Wikipedia). (Thrust also rises a little as the air thins; the question sets the craft in space with one steady push, so only the mass changes.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Saturn_V ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V

## Finding mass in orbit

F11. On the International Space Station a crew member's mass is measured by a device that "applies a known force to an attached astronaut and measures the resulting acceleration to acquire the crew member's mass. The result is based on a form of Newton's Second Law of Motion" (NASA image caption, 11 February 2025). NASA's Space Linear Acceleration Mass Measurement Device "takes advantage of Isaac Newton's second law of motion": two springs push a guide arm against the astronaut, "The force exerted by the springs is already known", an optical sensor tracks the motion, and a laptop works out mass from F = ma "to within an accuracy of half a pound (0.2 kilograms)" (Space.com).
    https://www.nasa.gov/image-detail/amf-iss072e616386 ; https://www.space.com/space-exploration/human-spaceflight/how-do-astronauts-weigh-themselves-in-space

F12. In orbit astronauts are "effectively weightless", while mass stays the same; weight is mass times the gravitational acceleration around the body (Space.com). ESA astronaut Matthias Maurer: "Measurement of mass in zero gravity requires other techniques than on the ground – a normal scale wouldn't work here." A balance works by comparing an object with reference masses as gravity pulls on both sides (Wikipedia, Weighing scale); with weight all but gone, neither pan presses down, so a pan balance cannot settle (reasoned from these two sourced facts). The space a body fills is its volume, not its mass (T2 unit density-float-or-sink: density is mass per unit of volume).
    https://www.space.com/space-exploration/human-spaceflight/how-do-astronauts-weigh-themselves-in-space ; https://esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2021/12/Matthias_Maurer_on_the_space_scale ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighing_scale

## The laws together: kick, catch, tackle

F13. The third law: "To every action there is always opposed an equal reaction" (Newton, quoted by Grokipedia); two objects push on each other equally hard, and the two forces act on different objects. Put with the second law, the same-sized force on half the mass gives twice the acceleration, so in a collision the lighter body's motion changes more, in inverse proportion to the masses (F1).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-3-newtons-second-law-of-motion-concept-of-a-system

F14. A kick: the foot touches the ball only briefly (it moves with the ball for "approximately the 2/3 of the diameter of the ball"); maximum instep kicks send the ball off at 18 to 35 m/s (Kellis and Katis). The ball is accelerated only during contact: the "effectual duration to accelerate the ball corresponds to approximately three fourths of visually determined ball contact time" (Nunome and others, ISBS). Once the ball leaves the foot, rolling resistance, "the resistive force that opposes the motion of a rolling object" (Grokipedia), and the air act against its motion, so a ball rolling on flat grass slows from then on (F2). The ball is still gaining speed midway through the boot's push, since the push still points forward then; it is fastest as contact ends.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3786235 ; https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/5153 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rolling_resistance

F15. A catch: the mitt pushes back on the ball, against its motion, so the net force on the slowing ball points back toward the thrower, and so does its acceleration (F2). The ball pushes forward on the mitt equally hard, but that force acts on the mitt (F13).
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-3-newtons-second-law-of-motion-concept-of-a-system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion

## Newton, the plague years and the Principia

F16. "When the plague closed the University in the summer of 1665", Newton "had to return to Lincolnshire", and there, "in a period of less than two years, while Newton was still under 25 years old, he began revolutionary advances in mathematics, optics, physics, and astronomy" (MacTutor). He went home to Woolsthorpe Manor, where he was born, and stayed until spring 1667; there he developed the method of fluxions (an early calculus), experimented with prisms and began on universal gravitation (Grokipedia, Woolsthorpe Manor). In 1665-1666 he "developed the foundational concepts for his laws of motion and universal gravitation" (Grokipedia, Isaac Newton). Wikipedia: the university closed "as a precaution against the Great Plague of London", and the next two years at Woolsthorpe saw his theories on calculus, optics and gravitation.
    https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Newton/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Woolsthorpe_Manor ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Isaac_Newton ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton

F17. The three laws were first stated in Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica, "first published in 1687" (Grokipedia); "published in Latin on July 5, 1687" (Grokipedia, Principia). The book begins with "Definitions" and "Axioms or Laws of Motion" (Wikipedia); "The three laws of motion, termed axioms, follow as the foundational rules" (Grokipedia). Book III is "The System of the World". Newton's second law in his words: "The alteration of motion is ever proportional to the motive force impressed".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica

F18. In January 1684 Hooke "claimed to not only have derived the inverse-square law but also all the laws of planetary motion"; Wren was unconvinced and Hooke never produced the derivation (Wikipedia, Principia). In August 1684 Halley visited Newton at Cambridge and "posed the question of what form planetary orbits would take under an inverse-square law" (Grokipedia, Principia); "Newton replied immediately that it would be an ellipse" (MacTutor, quoting Nauenberg). Newton "surprised Halley by saying that he had already made the derivations some time ago; but that he could not find the papers" (Wikipedia, Principia); he "promised to redo them and send them on later" (Wikipedia, Halley).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica ; https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Newton/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmond_Halley

F19. "In November 1684, Newton drafted De motu corporum in gyrum" (Grokipedia), "a short treatise titled On the motion of bodies in an orbit" (Wikipedia, Halley). "Halley persuaded Newton to write a full treatment of his new physics and its application to astronomy" (MacTutor); Grokipedia (Halley) speaks of "persuading the reluctant author".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmond_Halley ; https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Newton/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Edmond_Halley

F20. Kepler found his laws by "rigorous empirical fitting" to Tycho Brahe's observations (Astronomia Nova, 1609; Harmonices Mundi, 1619), and "lacked a full mechanical framework to derive it rigorously"; Newton "provided a theoretical foundation for Kepler's empirical laws" in the Principia (Grokipedia). Newton built his first reflecting telescope in 1668 (Grokipedia, Newtonian telescope); he found the ellipse by calculation (F18), not by watching planets.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kepler's_laws_of_planetary_motion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Newtonian_telescope

F21. Printing: the Royal Society "withdrew its funding commitment after expending resources" on "Francis Willughby's De historia piscium (1686)", "a lavishly illustrated but commercially unsuccessful volume on fish"; "Halley personally financed the publication at his own expense"; "He meticulously proofread the galleys" (Grokipedia, Principia). Wikipedia: "The Society had just spent its book budget on De Historia piscium", "the cost of publication was borne by Edmund Halley", and "Halley was paid with leftover copies of De Historia piscium"; Halley "was given 75 unsold copies of the society's unsuccessful book The History of Fish" (Wikipedia, Halley). Halley served the Society as clerk from 1686 (Grokipedia, Halley).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmond_Halley ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Edmond_Halley

F22. When Hooke's priority claim surfaced in 1686, "Newton threatened to withdraw and suppress Book 3 altogether"; Halley persuaded him to let publication go ahead (Wikipedia, Principia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. A moving thing needs a continuing push, so a steady force gives a steady speed: Aristotle's violent motion is "necessitating an ongoing external agent to sustain it" (Grokipedia). Students who hold it expect zero acceleration under a steady pull.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Aristotelian_physics

M2. Heavier things fall faster: Aristotle had "heavier bodies moving faster than lighter ones through the same medium" (Grokipedia). Apollo 15 (F7) and the vacuum chamber (F8) show otherwise.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Aristotelian_physics

M3. Impetus: a thrown or kicked thing carries a force with it that keeps it going and wears away. Philoponus and Buridan held it ("a motive force impressed upon a body by its mover that persists in the absence of resistance", Grokipedia); students today still speak of "the force of the throw" (Robertson, Goodhew and Scherr, The Physics Teacher, 2021).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Theory_of_impetus ; https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10295285

M4. In a collision the bigger body pushes harder: the "dominance principle", or "greater mass implies a greater force", found in students on the Force Concept Inventory and the Force and Motion Conceptual Evaluation (truck and car questions). Action and reaction are also wrongly thought to cancel (Grokipedia).
    https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.06176 ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00967 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion

M5. With no friction, no force is needed to speed up a heavier load (mass mixed up with friction or weight); and things fall together on the Moon only because its gravity is weak (answered by F8).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26; Grokipedia first)

F23. Gravity at the space station is still strong: there "Earth's gravity is about 90 percent of what it is on the planet's surface", and "The spacecraft, its crew and any objects aboard are all falling toward but around Earth", so the crew and objects float compared with the spacecraft (NASA, What Is Microgravity?, grades 5-8). An astronaut in orbit keeps most of his weight; what vanishes is the scale's reading, because he and the station fall together. (T3 gravity-and-orbits keys both; here they are context only.) This replaces F12's "effectively weightless" wording in the card.
    https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-5-8/

F24. Saturn V at liftoff: "the fueled mass reached approximately 2,820,000 kilograms" and the first stage had "five F-1 engines delivering 34.5 MN of thrust" (Grokipedia); "Fully fueled, the Saturn V had a mass of 2,822,171 to 2,965,241 kg" and the first stage "provided 33,000 kN ... of thrust at sea level" (Wikipedia). With 1 kg weighing about 9.8 N (F3), the full rocket weighed about 27.7 to 29.1 million newtons: about 28 million newtons against about 34 million newtons of push. That leaves about 6 million newtons (4 to 7 million across the sources' figures) to speed up nearly 3 million kilograms, about 2 metres a second each second (reasoned with F1).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Saturn_V ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V

F25. Drag "requires relative velocity for its generation" (Grokipedia, Drag), so a rocket just leaving its pad meets almost no drag from the air. (The T2 unit forces-and-net-force keys that air resistance grows with speed.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Drag_(physics)

F26. The kick (F14): the ball is accelerated only while the boot touches it, so it has barely begun to move at the first touch and gains speed all through the contact (reasoned from F2 and F14). The picture of a kick giving all its speed at the first blow is the wrong choice "Just as his boot first strikes it"; the stem no longer says the ball sits still, so its words do not rule that choice out.
    https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/5153 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3786235

F27. Wrong choices checked in the revision: the sled's "A little harder, for the extra friction" is wrong because the extra mass, not friction, needs the extra force, and ice friction only grows in step with the load (F1, M5; the fact-check's note on the sled question). The astronaut's "height and the size of his waist" give only his size; NASA measures his mass in orbit with a known force and the acceleration it gives (F11).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion ; https://www.nasa.gov/image-detail/amf-iss072e616386
