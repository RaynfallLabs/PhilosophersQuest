## Chemical change, physical change, and the signs

F1. A chemical change (a chemical reaction) rearranges atoms into one or more new substances; a physical change alters a substance's form or physical properties while its composition stays the same. Physical changes include melting ice, boiling water, dissolving sugar in water, tearing a sheet of paper, cutting, and crushing (which "applies compressive stress to reduce particle size").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change

F2. Chemical reactions are "typically identified by observable changes": "a color change, evolution of a gas, formation of a precipitate, change in temperature, or emission of light."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction

F3. The 6th-grade standard TEKS 6.6E asks students to identify the formation of a new substance "by using the evidence of a possible chemical change", such as production of a gas, a change in thermal energy, production of a precipitate, and a colour change. NGSS MS-PS1-2 has students compare the properties of substances before and after they interact to decide whether a reaction happened. A new smell is commonly taught alongside these clues.
    https://teksguide.org/teks/s66e/alignments ; https://tea.texas.gov/academics/subject-areas/science/tif-physical-and-chemical-changes-may-24-0.pdf ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps1-2-matter-and-its-interactions ; https://lessonplanet.com/teachers/serc-investigate-chemical-changes-what-are-some-signs-of-chemical-change

F4. The signs are clues, not proof: physical changes can show them too. Boiling water is a physical change; an ice cube melting in a drink cools it; diluting paint with water visibly lightens its colour. Bubbles in boiling water are steam, water changed to the gas state, not hydrogen and oxygen.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change ; https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-changes-of-state-beyond-appearances/4017768.article

## A gas given off

F5. An effervescent antacid tablet such as the original Alka-Seltzer contains aspirin, sodium bicarbonate (the antacid) and citric acid. Its fizz "arises from an acid-base reaction between citric acid and sodium bicarbonate", which produces sodium citrate, water and carbon dioxide gas, the bubbles. The reaction starts once water reaches the ingredients; even trace moisture can set it off early, so the tablets are made and kept dry.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alka-Seltzer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Effervescent_tablet

## A change in temperature on its own

F6. An air-activated hand warmer holds iron powder, water, salt, activated charcoal and vermiculite (which holds the water) in an air-permeable cloth pouch, sealed inside an airtight wrapper. Once the wrapper is opened, oxygen in the air, together with the water, reacts with the iron to form rust, and this oxidation gives off heat (it is exothermic). Salt is added to speed the reaction.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hand_warmer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_warmer

F7. A hand warmer gives steady heat at about 50-55 degrees Celsius for 8-12 hours (Grokipedia); Wikipedia notes the package can reach 74 degrees Celsius. Ordinary rusting goes so slowly that its energy spreads away and is never felt as warmth; powdering the iron puts much more of it in contact with the air, and salt and water speed the rusting, so a hand warmer gets hot.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hand_warmer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_warmer ; https://www.customs.go.jp/ccl/e_kids/2.htm

F8. Dissolving table salt (sodium chloride) in water is very slightly endothermic (about +3.9 kJ per mole): it gives off no heat.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium_chloride

F9. Exothermic reactions release heat to the surroundings (burning methane is the example); endothermic reactions absorb heat from the surroundings, whose temperature falls, so they "often feel cold to the touch" (photosynthesis takes in light energy). Mixing barium hydroxide octahydrate with ammonium chloride "drops the temperature significantly".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Endothermic_process

F10. The classroom demonstration: solid barium hydroxide octahydrate and solid ammonium chloride (both white) are stirred together in a flask standing on a wooden block wetted with a little water. The temperature drops rapidly to about -20 degrees Celsius, and "the water on the base freezes causing the watch glass or wooden block to freeze to the flask." The products are ammonia gas (it turns damp red litmus blue), water and barium chloride.
    https://edu.rsc.org/exhibition-chemistry/chilling-effects/2000044.article ; https://edu.rsc.org/experiments/endothermic-solid-solid-reactions/739.article ; https://www.colorado.edu/lab/lecture-demo-manual/t515-endothermic-reaction-baoh2-nh4no3

F11. Cold is not a substance that moves: thermal energy moves only from a warmer object to a cooler one. Something feels cold because heat is flowing out of the hand or object touching it.
    https://www.open.edu/openlearncreate/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=64798&section=5 ; https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2919

## A solid forming in a liquid (a precipitate)

F12. Limewater is a saturated solution of calcium hydroxide in water and is clear and colourless. Carbon dioxide passed through it makes it milky, through "precipitation of insoluble calcium carbonate" (Ca(OH)2 + CO2 gives CaCO3 + H2O). Much more carbon dioxide turns the carbonate into soluble calcium bicarbonate, and the milkiness clears again.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Calcium_hydroxide ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limewater ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Calcium_carbonate

F13. Breathing out through a straw into limewater for about 20-30 seconds turns it cloudy: the carbon dioxide in exhaled breath makes the insoluble calcium carbonate. The test is specific to carbon dioxide; oxygen and nitrogen cause no change.
    https://www.chem.indiana.edu/faculty-research/faculty-resources/chemistry-demos/demo/limewater-as-indicator-of-carbon-dioxide-gas ; https://chem.libretexts.org/Ancillary_Materials/Demos_Techniques_and_Experiments/Lecture_Demonstrations/Magic_Breath

F14. Calcium carbonate is a white solid, practically insoluble in water (about 0.013 g per litre); its mineral calcite "forms the bulk of limestone, chalk, marble, and various shells".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Calcium_carbonate

## A colour change

F15. Copper is reddish-orange with a bright metallic lustre, and copper sheet is used for roofing; copper roofs can last more than 100 years. Copper first reacts with oxygen (a reddish copper oxide), then carbon dioxide and moisture turn this into a "protective blue-green layer primarily of basic copper carbonate" that "adheres tightly and prevents deeper corrosion". In sulfur-polluted city air the patina is mostly sulfates; near the sea, chlorides. A copper roof develops it over about 5-10 years in continental climates (Grokipedia); Wikipedia says "many years", faster in damp coastal places.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Patina ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Copper ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patina

## Light given off

F16. A glow stick is a flexible plastic tube holding a brittle glass vial. Bending the tube breaks the vial, and shaking mixes the two chemical solutions (hydrogen peroxide, and a phenyl oxalate ester with a fluorescent dye). They react and give off light (chemiluminescence) "with very little heat", with no battery or other energy input; a standard stick glows for about 4-12 hours, cannot be switched off and works only once. Below 0 degrees Celsius it glows dimmer but longer.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Glow_stick ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glow_stick

F17. Glow-in-the-dark toys work differently: their phosphorescent materials (strontium aluminate, zinc sulfide) absorb light and re-emit it slowly after the light is gone. Chemiluminescence, as in a glow stick, needs no light first.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Phosphorescence

## A new smell

F18. Fresh sweat is mostly odourless (eccrine sweat is "a watery, mostly odorless sweat"; apocrine secretions are an odourless fluid until skin microbes act on them). Body odour forms when bacteria on the skin (chiefly Corynebacterium species and Staphylococcus hominis) break down sweat into smelly compounds such as thioalcohols and short-chain fatty acids (butyric acid, for one). The armpits are the main site; feet get a cheesy smell from brevibacteria. Sweat itself carries water, salt and fat. Salt (sodium chloride) has at most a faint smell: Wikipedia's infobox gives "Faint, salty (when powdered), or odorless (solid rock)".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Body_odor ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_odor ; https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/17865-body-odor ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride

## A colour change with no new substance: the streak

F19. A mineral's streak is "the color of the fine powder produced when the mineral is scratched or rubbed against an unglazed porcelain streak plate". The streak can differ from the lump's own colour and is more consistent, so geologists use it to identify minerals. A streak can only be made by minerals softer than the plate, about 7 on the Mohs scale.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Streak_(mineralogy) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streak_(mineralogy)

F20. Hematite is the mineral form of iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3. Massive and crystalline specimens are typically steel-grey to black with a metallic lustre, yet all forms leave a reddish-brown (rust-red) streak. Its Mohs hardness is 5 to 6.5 for metallic varieties, below the streak plate's.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hematite ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streak_(mineralogy)

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Students treat any visible change, a colour change above all, as a sign of a new substance; one student reasoned that a change "in colour, mass and state" made a chemical change "obvious". In one study 80 percent of students took a colour difference between reactant and product as evidence of chemical change.
    https://edu.rsc.org/resources/introducing-chemical-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017789.article

M2. "If a teacher gives a general explanation, students may think that all reactions produce heat."
    https://edu.rsc.org/resources/introducing-chemical-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017789.article

M3. Students believe "cold" is a substance that moves from one object to another (keeping the fridge shut "to let the cold out").
    https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2919 ; https://www.open.edu/openlearncreate/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=64798&section=5

M4. Asked what the bubbles in boiling water are made of, students aged 8-17 answered heat, air, oxygen or hydrogen and steam; many 17-year-olds thought heating splits water into its elements.
    https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-changes-of-state-beyond-appearances/4017768.article

M5. Students define a physical change as one that "can be easily reversed" and a chemical change as one that "is not easily reversed", a rule of thumb that fails (tearing paper cannot be undone, yet it is physical).
    https://edu.rsc.org/resources/introducing-chemical-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017789.article ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change

M6. Author's judgment, from the facts above, for the lures: that a copper roof's green is moss or algae, or its shine washed or faded away (F15 shows it is a new substance made by reaction); that a glow stick stores daylight like a glow-in-the-dark toy (F17); that a hand warmer's heat comes from its grains rubbing as it is shaken or from its salt dissolving (F6-F8); that the milkiness of breathed-on limewater is trapped bubbles or mist (F12-F13); that a smell grows from sweat drying (F18); that a mineral's coloured powder is a new substance made by rubbing, comes from the tile, or is some other red rock inside the lump (F19-F20: the powder is the mineral itself, softer than the plate).
