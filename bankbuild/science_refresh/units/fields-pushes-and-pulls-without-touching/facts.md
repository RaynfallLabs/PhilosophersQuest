## Electric charge: like and unlike, and what rubbing moves

F1. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract (OpenStax: "Like charges repel, whereas unlike charges attract"; Grokipedia: "like charges repelling each other and opposite charges attracting").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Static_electricity ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/18-1-static-electricity-and-charge-conservation-of-charge ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge

F2. Charging by rubbing or contact (the triboelectric effect) moves electrons from one material to the other; static build-up results "from the transfer of electrons between materials". No charge is made: "No charge is actually created or destroyed when charges are separated" (OpenStax). Contact alone can charge; friction is "not strictly necessary" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Triboelectric_effect ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Static_electricity ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/18-1-static-electricity-and-charge-conservation-of-charge

F3. Protons do not move in ordinary charging: the positive charge "is carried by the proton, which is stuck firmly in the nucleus of atoms", and electrons are far easier to remove (OpenStax Physics). The Physics Classroom: charging "always occurs by means of a movement of electrons"; an object turns positive by losing electrons, not by gaining protons.
    https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge ; https://www.physicsclassroom.com/class/estatics/u8l2a

F4. A balloon rubbed on hair: "electrons are moved from the hair to the surface of the balloon"; the balloon becomes negatively charged and the hair positively, having fewer electrons than before (Science Learning Hub). Hair sits near the positive end of the triboelectric series and a rubber balloon well toward the negative end; the Physics Classroom's hair-and-balloon example has electrons moving from hair to balloon.
    https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/2746-static-electricity-and-electrical-charge ; https://www.physicsclassroom.com/class/estatics/u8l2a ; https://knowledge.carolina.com/uncategorized/the-triboelectric-series-an-introduction-for-static-electricity-labs/

F5. Rubbed hair stands up because its strands carry like charge and repel one another (Grokipedia: friction "causes the hair to repel and stand upright due to like-charge interactions").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Static_electricity

F6. A charged object attracts a neutral one by polarization, "the separation of charges in an object that remains neutral": in an insulator "the distribution of charge in atoms and molecules is shifted slightly", the opposite charge pulled nearer and the like charge pushed away; because the force weakens with distance, the nearer attraction outweighs the farther repulsion (OpenStax). A rubbed balloon sticks to a neutral wall this way: the charges within the wall rearrange so a positive area meets the negative balloon (Science Buddies). A balloon rubbed on metal does not stick, because its extra electrons quickly leave it (Science Buddies). Grokipedia: "The induced charges create an attractive force between the original charged object and the induced opposite charge."
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/18-2-conductors-and-insulators ; https://www.sciencebuddies.org/science-fair-projects/project_ideas/Elec_p017.shtml ; https://www.sciencebuddies.org/stem-activities/rubbing-up-against-static-electricity (Science Buddies read through search excerpts; the pages refuse direct fetch) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electrostatic_induction

F7. Thales of Miletus noted (around 600 BC) that rubbed amber attracts light things such as feathers; amber is elektron in Greek. William Gilbert coined the Latin electricus ("like amber") in 1600, the root of "electricity".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Static_electricity ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triboelectric_effect ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gilbert_(physicist)

## How strong: more charge or a stronger magnet, and distance

F8. NGSS core idea PS2.B: "Electric and magnetic (electromagnetic) forces can be attractive or repulsive, and their sizes depend on the magnitudes of the charges, currents, or magnetic strengths involved and on the distances between the interacting objects." Forces at a distance "can be explained by fields that extend through space".
    https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps2-3-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps2-5-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions

F9. The electric force between two charged bodies grows with the charge on each (Grokipedia: "directly proportional to the product of their magnitudes") and "decreases with distance" (OpenStax). Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured it in 1785 with a torsion balance: a light rod hung on a thin thread, a charged ball at one end, the twist showing the force. (The inverse-square form is T5's and is not used here.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Coulomb's_law ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/18-1-static-electricity-and-charge-conservation-of-charge

F10. A magnet's field weakens quickly with distance: "the strength of the field falls off inversely with the cube of the distance from the magnet's center" (Wikipedia, Magnet); a dipole's field "decreases as the inverse cube of the distance" (Wikipedia, Magnetic moment). Grokipedia gives the same far-field formula, B = (mu0/4pi)(2m/r^3).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_moment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnet

F11. Earth's field at the surface is weak: "about 25 to 65 microtesla" (Grokipedia); 31 microtesla at the equator and 58 at 50 degrees latitude (Wikipedia). Refrigerator magnets are 1 to 10 millitesla (10 to 100 gauss) and a penny-sized neodymium magnet about 150 millitesla (Wikipedia), so close to a small magnet its field is many times Earth's.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_magnetic_field ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude_(magnetic_field)

F12. As whole magnets, Earth is vastly the stronger: its magnetic moment is about 8 x 10^22 A m^2 (Grokipedia), while "A good bar magnet may have a magnetic moment of magnitude 0.1 A m^2" (Wikipedia). Earth's moment is nearly a million billion billion (about 8 x 10^23) times the bar magnet's. With F10, a good bar magnet's field on its axis is about 2.5 millitesla at 2 cm and 0.16 millitesla at 5 cm (several times Earth's) but only about 0.02 microtesla at 1 m (far below Earth's). (Worked from the sourced formula and moments.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_magnetic_field ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet

F13. Fields from several sources add: the total at a point is "the vector sum of the fields produced by individual sources", and a compass needle shows the field by its "alignment with the local magnetic vector" (Grokipedia). A nearby magnet does not block Earth's field; the two add, and the needle follows the sum.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnetic_field

## Magnets, Gilbert and Earth

F14. Like magnetic poles repel and unlike poles attract (OpenStax: "like poles repel and unlike poles attract"; Grokipedia: "Like poles repel, while opposite poles attract"). Poles always come in pairs; a magnet cut in two makes two magnets.
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-1-magnets ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnet

F15. Plain iron is drawn to either pole of a magnet: "ordinary iron is always attracted to a magnet", for near a magnet it becomes "a temporary magnet, of a polarity suitable for attraction", as Gilbert guessed (NASA, D. P. Stern). OpenStax: a magnet near unmagnetized iron "causes local magnetization", its domains lining up.
    https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/earthmag/inducemg.htm ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets

F16. William Gilbert (1544?-1603), an English physician, later physician to Queen Elizabeth I (from 1601), published De Magnete, Magneticisque Corporibus, et de Magno Magnete Tellure in London in 1600. His terrella was a lodestone shaped into a sphere, a "little Earth". A pivoted needle held over it lined up along meridians converging at the ball's poles and "tilted downward toward the terrella's surface"; the dip ran from level at its equator to upright at its poles, as Robert Norman had found a compass dipping on Earth (1581). Gilbert concluded that Earth itself is a great magnet, which is why a compass points north; he was also the first to argue that Earth's centre is iron. He relied on experiment, "experience rather than the arguments of philosophy".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/De_Magnete ; https://grokipedia.com/page/William_Gilbert_(physicist) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Terrella ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gilbert_(physicist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Magnete ; https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/earthmag/demagint.htm

F17. Before Gilbert, some held that compasses pointed to the Pole Star (Polaris), or that "a large magnetic island on the north pole" attracted them (Wikipedia); Gilbert dismissed tales of magnetic mountains and the idea of stars ruling the compass (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gilbert_(physicist) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/De_Magnete ; https://grokipedia.com/page/William_Gilbert_(physicist)

F18. A compass needle's north (north-seeking) end is drawn toward the far north, and "the magnetic pole that is near the geographic North Pole is actually a south magnetic pole" (OpenStax); the North Magnetic Pole "functions as a magnetic south pole" and is named for the compass end that points there (Grokipedia); "the North Magnetic Pole is actually the south pole of the Earth's magnetic field" (Wikipedia). It is where the field points straight down; it lies in the Arctic (85.76 N, 139.30 E in the World Magnetic Model 2025), was first located in 1831 on the Boothia Peninsula in northern Canada, and is drifting toward Siberia.
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-1-magnets ; https://grokipedia.com/page/North_magnetic_pole ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet

F19. Earth's field resembles that of "a giant bar magnet", tilted about 11 degrees from the spin axis, and comes from dynamo action in the liquid outer core, where moving molten iron carries electric currents.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_magnetic_field

## Oersted's needle

F20. Hans Christian Oersted, preparing a lecture demonstration at the University of Copenhagen in April 1820 (21 April, by tradition; Grokipedia: "during a lecture demonstration, Ørsted observed..."), using a battery of about 20 copper and zinc plates, passed a current "through a thin wire positioned above a compass needle covered by glass"; the needle swung (Wikipedia: "deflected from magnetic north by a nearby electric current"). "No deflection occurred when the circuit was open or the current absent" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hans_Christian_%C3%98rsted ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Christian_%C3%98rsted ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/hans-christian-oersted/

F21. It was the first evidence of a direct link between electricity and magnetism: an electric current makes a magnetic field. Later work that year showed the field runs in circles around the wire (Linda Hall: "an electric current generates a circular magnetic field around a wire"). The effect passed through wood and glass. He announced it in a four-page Latin pamphlet dated 21 July 1820, and Arago in Paris and Ampere followed within months. OpenStax: Oersted's wire deflected a compass needle, showing that moving charges are connected to magnetism.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hans_Christian_%C3%98rsted ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Christian_%C3%98rsted ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/hans-christian-oersted/ ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets

F22. The story that he found the effect by chance mid-lecture is called a myth (Wikipedia: he had looked for a link since 1818); Grokipedia calls the find "serendipitous". The two disagree, so no question rests on it.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Christian_%C3%98rsted ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hans_Christian_%C3%98rsted

## The electromagnet

F23. An electromagnet is a magnet whose field "is produced by an electric current", the wire usually wound in a coil, often round an iron core; "The magnetic field disappears when the current is turned off" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: the field "ceases entirely when the current is interrupted"). "The main advantage of an electromagnet over a permanent magnet is that the magnetic field can be quickly changed" (Wikipedia). OpenStax: "Electromagnetism is the use of electric current to make magnets."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electromagnet ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets

F24. Its strength grows with both the number of turns and the current: the field is "proportional to both the number of turns of wire N and the current I" (Wikipedia), "quantified as ampere-turns" (Grokipedia). NGSS MS-PS2-3's own example of data: "the effect of the number of turns of wire on the strength of an electromagnet".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electromagnet ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps2-3-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions

F25. The iron core is what makes it strong: "A core can increase the magnetic field to thousands of times the strength of the field of the coil alone" (Wikipedia); the core "aligns magnetic domains in response to the current" (Grokipedia). The magnetic (ferromagnetic) elements at room temperature are iron, cobalt, nickel and gadolinium; copper "exhibit[s] no net moment" (Grokipedia, Ferromagnetism). So neither copper nor zinc, the metals of Oersted's battery plates (F20), is magnetic.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electromagnet ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ferromagnetism

F26. Turning the battery round reverses the current and swaps the electromagnet's poles: "To change the poles, just change which way the battery is hooked up. This changes the direction the current flows and reverses the field" (University of Illinois, Ask the Van). The field's direction follows the current by the right-hand rule (Grokipedia, Magnetic field; Wikipedia, Electromagnet); its strength depends on the size of the current (F24).
    https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/2340 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnetic_field ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet

F27. William Sturgeon built the first electromagnet in the mid-1820s (Wikipedia 1824; Grokipedia demonstrated 1825): a varnished horseshoe of soft iron wound with bare copper wire, weighing seven ounces and lifting nine pounds. Joseph Henry, from 1830, insulated the wire with silk thread so he could wind it in many layers, making magnets with thousands of turns.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electromagnet ; https://grokipedia.com/page/William_Sturgeon

F28. Scrapyard cranes use large circular electromagnets to lift "ferrous loads weighing several tons", "with the field de-energized to release the material at the destination" (Grokipedia); OpenStax gives "a wrecking yard crane that lifts scrapped cars". Electromagnets pick up "heavy iron objects such as scrap iron and steel" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Electromagnet ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet

F29. An electromagnetic lock (maglock) is an electromagnet on the door frame and a steel armature plate on the door; "the continuous application of power maintains the magnetic field", and "the lock releases instantly when power is interrupted", so it is "inherently fail-safe", "ensuring free egress in emergencies such as fire alarms or power outages". Holding force is typically 600 to over 2,000 pounds, standard locks 1,200 to 1,500 pounds-force, with "no moving parts to wear out" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia lists "Magnetic locks" among electromagnets' uses.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Electromagnetic_lock ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Electric charge and magnetism are run together: many students take a magnet's poles to be charges (the north pole as positive), and the idea that magnetism comes from static charge "is very common (Driver et al., 1994), even amongst undergraduate science students (Maloney, 1985)" (IOP diagnostic, as reported in the search; ComPADRE's listing of IOPSpark's "Some students conflate magnetic and electrostatic effects").
    https://www.compadre.org/precollege/items/detail.cfm?ID=15994 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0503132
M2. "Rubbing makes charge": charge is moved, not created (F2).
M3. "A positive object has gained protons": it has lost electrons (F3).
M4. "The North Magnetic Pole is a north pole": by its kind it is a south pole (F18).
M5. "A small magnet that turns a compass is stronger than Earth": its field is stronger close to it, but Earth is the far stronger magnet (F11, F12).
M6. "Magnets attract all metals": 39% of 56 trainee science teachers thought so (Hickey and Schibeci, 1999, as reported by the IOP diagnostic). Copper is not magnetic (F25).
