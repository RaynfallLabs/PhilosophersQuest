# Temperature and Moving Particles (T3, 7th grade, physical strand, G7.P7)

Sources were read on 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; a second source is given
for anything a child could be taught wrong. No old-bank topic was quarried.

Left to other units and not re-asked here: particles in each state, heating
speeding particles up so solids melt and liquids boil, particles keeping their
size through a change of state (T2 particles-in-motion); the states and water's
0 and 100 degree marks (T1 solids-liquids-and-gases); heat flowing from warmer
to cooler, conduction, convection, radiation, insulators and the vacuum flask
(T3 heat-on-the-move); kinetic energy as the energy of motion (T2
kinetic-and-potential-energy). Arithmetic and unit conversions are the math
bank's: no question asks for one.

## Temperature and thermal energy

F1. Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy (energy of motion)
of a substance's particles. Grokipedia: temperature is "a measure of the
average kinetic energy of the microscopic particles". LibreTexts (K12): "The
temperature of an object is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of all
the molecules of the object." Penn State: "temperature measures the average
kinetic energy of individual particles".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Temperature ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Physics/09%3A_Thermal_Physics/9.01%3A_Heat_Temperature_and_Thermal_Energy ; https://courses.ems.psu.edu/egee102/node/2376

F2. A rise in temperature means the particles have more kinetic energy, so
they move faster; cooling lowers it. OpenStax: "an increase in temperature
implies an increase in the kinetic energy of the individual atoms."
Grokipedia: particles' "kinetic energy rises with temperature".
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/13-2-thermal-expansion-of-solids-and-liquids ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_expansion

F3. Thermal energy is the total: the kinetic energy of all of a thing's
particles added together. LibreTexts (K12): "Adding up the kinetic energy of
all the molecules in an object yields the thermal energy of the object."
Penn State: thermal energy is "the total internal energy contained within a
substance due to the random motion and interactions of its atoms and
molecules". So thermal energy depends on how much of the substance there is,
while temperature (an average) does not.
    https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Physics/09%3A_Thermal_Physics/9.01%3A_Heat_Temperature_and_Thermal_Energy ; https://courses.ems.psu.edu/egee102/node/2376

F4. A bathtub of cooler water holds more thermal energy than a little
near-boiling water. LibreTexts (K12) compares a millilitre of near-boiling
water with a bathtub at room temperature: "The bathtub contains a billion
times as many water molecules, and therefore has a higher total kinetic
energy and more heat", yet it is colder, because its average kinetic energy
is lower. Penn State: "Even though the cup of boiling water has a higher
temperature, the bathtub's massive volume contains so many more water
molecules that their collective movement stores far more total heat". So the
cup's particles move faster on average; the bathtub wins on numbers.
    https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Physics/09%3A_Thermal_Physics/9.01%3A_Heat_Temperature_and_Thermal_Energy ; https://courses.ems.psu.edu/egee102/node/2376

## Not every particle moves at the average speed: evaporation cools

F5. The particles of a liquid move at many different speeds. Grokipedia: "the
molecules in a liquid exhibit a distribution of kinetic energies", and only
"those in the energetic tail of the distribution" escape. Evaporation happens
below the boiling point. As the most energetic molecules leave, "the average
kinetic energy of the remaining liquid decreases, leading to a cooling
effect" (sweat cools skin this way). LibreTexts: "a given liquid sample will
have molecules with a wide range of kinetic energies"; "As evaporation
occurs, the temperature of the remaining liquid decreases."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Evaporation ; https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Introductory_Chemistry/Introductory_Chemistry_(CK-12)/13%3A_States_of_Matter/13.08%3A_Evaporation

F6. A wet-bulb thermometer has its bulb covered with a wet wick in moving air.
It reads lower than a dry-bulb thermometer beside it, because water
evaporating from the wick draws heat from the bulb; at 100 percent relative
humidity evaporation stops and the two read the same. The pair together is a
psychrometer, which weather observers use to find humidity. The low reading
is a real temperature (the wet-bulb temperature), not an error.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wet-bulb_temperature

## Energy that changes the state, not the temperature

F7. While ice melts, its temperature stays at 0 degrees Celsius even as heat
is added, and climbs again only when all the ice has melted. OpenStax: "No
temperature change occurs from heat transfer if ice melts and becomes liquid
water"; "The temperature of a cup of soda initially at 0ºC stays at 0ºC until
all the ice has melted"; the energy goes into breaking "the cohesive bonds
between the molecules in the solid ... thus, there is no rise in
temperature." Grokipedia (Latent heat): the energy is "used to reorganize the
molecular structure of the substance rather than increase its kinetic
energy", absorbed "to overcome the rigid lattice structure of the solid".
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Latent_heat

## Thermal expansion

F8. Why heated things expand: heating raises the particles' kinetic energy,
and their harder jostling "leads to greater average separation and thus
expansion" (Grokipedia). OpenStax: "This neighbor-to-neighbor pushing results
in a slightly greater distance, on average, between neighbors." The particles
themselves do not grow: MOSART (Harvard-Smithsonian) lists "The expansion or
contraction of matter is due to corresponding changes in the constituent
particles" as a student misconception.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_expansion ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/13-2-thermal-expansion-of-solids-and-liquids ; https://waps.cfa.harvard.edu/mosart/misconceptions.php

F9. The ball-and-ring demonstration: a metal ball passes through a metal ring
when both are at the same temperature; heated in a flame, the ball expands and
no longer goes through (University of Toronto demo: "Heat up the ball so that
it no longer goes through the ring"). Heating the ring instead makes the hole
larger, though "A surprising number of students" expect the ring to expand
inward (Kenyon College). It is known as 's Gravesande's ring, after Willem
's Gravesande (1688-1742), professor at Leiden, who probably devised it
(Kenyon calls this a reasonable assumption, not a documented fact; the
question does not name him).
    https://ugdemos.physics.utoronto.ca/db/demos/thermal-expansion-ball-and-ring ; https://physics.kenyon.edu/EarlyApparatus/Thermodynamics/sGravesandes_Apparatus/sGravesandes_Apparatus.html

F10. The caloric theory, a respectable wrong theory of the past, explained
expansion with a fluid of heat: when heat is added, "additional caloric enters
and occupies the spaces between particles", pushing them apart. Count
Rumford's cannon-boring experiments (1798) and James Joule's work undermined
it, attempts "failed to separate it as a distinct entity", and by the 1850s
energy-based thermodynamics left it obsolete.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Caloric_theory

F11. Bridges have expansion joints so they can change length with the
temperature: OpenStax says the joints "allow bridges to change length without
buckling", and railroad tracks and bridges "have expansion joints to allow
them to freely expand and contract with temperature changes". Grokipedia:
joints permit "longitudinal movement from seasonal thermal expansion";
finger joints of interlocking steel plates handle the largest movements. In
cold weather the concrete shrinks and the gaps grow (SlashGear: "bridge gaps
feel larger and bumpier in the winter when the concrete in the bridges
shrinks"); in hot weather the deck lengthens and the gap narrows. OpenStax's
worked example: the Golden Gate Bridge's 1275 m steel main span changes by
about 0.84 m between -15 and 40 degrees Celsius.
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/13-2-thermal-expansion-of-solids-and-liquids ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Expansion_joint ; https://www.slashgear.com/2150984/highway-bridge-gaps-serve-purpose/

F12. Railway track laid in separate lengths has "intentional gaps between
segments to accommodate thermal expansion of steel rails" (Grokipedia, Thermal
expansion); rail-end gaps of about 1/4 to 1/2 inch help avoid buckling, and a
50 degree rise can lengthen a 100 m rail by up to 60 mm (Grokipedia,
Expansion joint).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_expansion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Expansion_joint

F13. Different materials expand by different amounts for the same warming.
Grokipedia's table: aluminium about 23, steel about 12 and soda-lime glass
about 9 millionths of its length per degree Celsius. A bimetallic strip bends
because one metal "elongates more". HyperPhysics (Georgia State) on loosening
a jar lid: steel expands about 1.44 times as much as glass, and "Directing the
stream of hot water at the center of the lid heats the metal more than the
glass", so the lid widens a little more than the jar's rim. Glass also
expands when heated; it does not shrink.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_expansion ; https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/thermo/jarlid.html

F14. Metal lids stay solid and hard far above hot-water temperatures:
aluminium melts at about 660 degrees Celsius (Grokipedia), and hot water is
below water's 100 degree boiling point. So hot water does not soften a lid.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Aluminium

F15. In a liquid-in-glass thermometer, as the temperature rises "the liquid
expands more than the glass bulb" and climbs the calibrated tube (Grokipedia).
OpenStax: "the expansion of alcohol is much greater than the expansion of the
glass containing it." Gases expand the most, solids the least: "solids
typically exhibiting the smallest changes and gases the largest" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_expansion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Temperature ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/13-2-thermal-expansion-of-solids-and-liquids

F16. Galileo's thermoscope: a glass flask "roughly the size of a hen's egg"
with "a long, narrow neck inverted into a water reservoir" (Grokipedia).
Heating the flask makes the air inside expand and push the water down the
neck; cooling lets the water rise. It had no graduated scale. Grokipedia
credits Galileo, earliest construction around 1592 in Padua (date uncertain);
Museo Galileo: designed during his stay in Padua (Viviani gives 1597).
Benedetto Castelli, in a letter of 20 September 1638, recalled Galileo
warming the flask in his hands and turning it mouth-down into water, and the
water rising up the neck as it cooled.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermoscope ; https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/object/Thermoscope.html ; https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/indepth/LetterByCastelliOnInventionThermoscope.html

## Absolute zero and the Kelvin scale

F17. Absolute zero is "the lowest possible temperature", 0 kelvins, equal to
-273.15 degrees Celsius. There particles have their minimum kinetic energy;
quantum zero-point energy "prevents complete cessation of motion", so they
keep a tiny jiggle (the least energy they can have). By the third law it
cannot be reached in a finite number of steps; laboratories have come within
about 38 trillionths of a kelvin (2021), less than a billionth of a degree.
Grokipedia (Temperature): at absolute zero a system's "internal energy reaches
its minimum".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Absolute_zero ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Temperature

F18. Guillaume Amontons, using an air thermometer in 1703, found that a gas's
pressure falls as it cools and reasoned that it would reach zero at roughly
-240 degrees Celsius: an early estimate of absolute zero. (Context only.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Absolute_zero

F19. William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, "first proposed an absolute
temperature scale in 1848" (Grokipedia, Kelvin; Grokipedia, Absolute zero).
The kelvin scale starts at absolute zero and "prohibits negative
temperatures"; a change of 1 kelvin equals a change of 1 degree Celsius;
0 degrees Celsius, where water freezes, is 273.15 kelvins.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kelvin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Absolute_zero

F20. Not everything freezes near absolute zero: helium "has no melting point
at standard pressure and does not solidify upon cooling to absolute zero";
it needs about 25 atmospheres of pressure to solidify.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Helium

F21. Empty outer space is not at absolute zero: the cosmic microwave
background has a temperature of 2.7255 kelvins (Grokipedia), and an object
left in the void eventually comes to about 2.7 kelvins (NASA Goddard,
Cosmicopia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_microwave_background ; https://science.gsfc.nasa.gov/attic/cosmicopia.gsfc.nasa.gov/qa_sp_ht.html

F22. The lowest air temperature ever recorded on Earth is -89.2 degrees
Celsius, at Vostok Station in Antarctica on 21 July 1983; the lowest surface
temperature measured from a satellite is -93.2 degrees Celsius (2010). Both
are far above absolute zero's -273.15.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lowest_temperature_recorded_on_Earth

## Misconceptions a 7th grader holds (honest wrong choices)

M1. Heat (thermal energy) and temperature are the same thing (MOSART, grades
9-12 list); "Students do not make a clear distinction between heat and
temperature" (NSTA probe, grades 6-12, citing AAAS 2009). So a hotter thing
must hold more thermal energy.
    https://waps.cfa.harvard.edu/mosart/misconceptions.php ; https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/boiling-time-and-temperature

M2. Heat and cold are substances (MOSART, grades 5-8), so "cold" can flow or
be given off.
    https://waps.cfa.harvard.edu/mosart/misconceptions.php

M3. Things expand because their particles expand (MOSART 9-12, see F8).
    https://waps.cfa.harvard.edu/mosart/misconceptions.php

M4. The longer heat is applied, the higher the temperature goes, even while
the substance changes state: middle school students "may still intuitively
believe that the temperature rises the longer heat is applied to a boiling
liquid" (NSTA, citing Stavy and Tirosh 2000).
    https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/boiling-time-and-temperature

M5. Only warm or hot objects have thermal energy (MOSART 9-12).
    https://waps.cfa.harvard.edu/mosart/misconceptions.php

M6. A heated ring's hole shrinks, as if the metal expanded inward (Kenyon,
F9): the everyday idea that a gap shares in the heating rather than being
closed by the solid around it.
    https://physics.kenyon.edu/EarlyApparatus/Thermodynamics/sGravesandes_Apparatus/sGravesandes_Apparatus.html
