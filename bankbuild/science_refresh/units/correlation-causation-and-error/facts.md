## Correlation and the hidden third factor

F1. A correlation (two things rising and falling together) does not by itself show that one causes the other. Grokipedia: "a statistical association between two variables does not necessarily indicate that one causes the other."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Correlation_does_not_imply_causation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_does_not_imply_causation

F2. Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together, because both are driven by hot weather: Grokipedia, "both are influenced by a third variable, such as warmer summer temperatures"; "seasonal temperature serves as the confounder". Wikipedia: "As ice cream sales increase, the rate of drowning deaths increases sharply", both put down to hot weather, when more people swim.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Correlation_does_not_imply_causation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spurious_relationship ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_does_not_imply_causation

F3. A confounder, or "lurking variable", is "an extraneous third variable (Z) that influences both the independent variable (X) and the dependent variable (Y)" (Grokipedia). A link made by one is called spurious.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spurious_relationship ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Correlation_does_not_imply_causation

F4. Eating before a swim does not raise the risk of drowning: "Eating less than an hour before swimming does not significantly increase the risk of experiencing muscle cramps", "and does not increase the risk of drowning" (Wikipedia, common misconceptions).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_common_misconceptions_about_science,_technology,_and_mathematics

## Night lights and nearsightedness: a third factor found

F5. Richard Stone and Graham Quinn (University of Pennsylvania and the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia) reported in Nature on 13 May 1999, from parents' questionnaires, an association between light at night during sleep in the first two years of life and later nearsightedness (myopia), "the strongest association with use of ambient room lighting and a somewhat weaker association with use of a night light" (NEI); roughly half of the children who had slept in fully lit rooms became myopic (Ohio State).
    https://www.nei.nih.gov/about/news-and-events/news/myopia-development-and-nighttime-light-exposure-infancy ; https://news.osu.edu/night-lights-dont-lead-to-nearsightedness-study-suggests/

F6. Karla Zadnik's team at Ohio State University (Nature, March 2000) asked about the night-time lighting of 1,220 children before age 2 and found no link: 20 percent of 417 who slept in the dark were myopic, 17 percent of 758 with a night light, 22 percent of 45 in fully lit rooms. Nearsighted parents were more likely to use night lights (Zadnik: "We think this may be due to the parents' own poor eyesight"), and "genetics plays a significant role in causing myopia". Her study took parental myopia into account; the 1999 study had not. Jane Gwiazda's team, working independently, found the same (NEI). Wikipedia gives parental myopia as the common cause.
    https://news.osu.edu/night-lights-dont-lead-to-nearsightedness-study-suggests/ ; https://www.nei.nih.gov/about/news-and-events/news/myopia-development-and-nighttime-light-exposure-infancy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_does_not_imply_causation

## Which way the arrow points: smoking and lung cancer

F7. Reverse causation is the mistake "where cause and effect are reversed. The cause is said to be the effect and vice versa" (Wikipedia; its example: the faster windmills turn, the more wind is seen, yet the wind turns the windmills).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_does_not_imply_causation

F8. Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill studied lung-cancer patients in twenty London hospitals (BMJ, 30 September 1950), matching each with a patient of the same sex and five-year age group who did not have cancer. Doll began "under the belief that it was due to the new material tarmac, or motor car fumes"; tobacco smoking turned out to be the factor the patients had in common, and the risk rose with the amount smoked. Doll himself stopped smoking because of the findings.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Doll ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2038856/ ; https://csts.ua.edu/files/2019/01/1950-09-30-BMJ-Smoking-Carcinoma-of-the-Lung-Doll-Hill.pdf (title, design and controls, from the search snippet)

F9. The British Doctors Study: Doll and Hill sent questionnaires in October 1951 to the 59,600 doctors on the British Medical Register and followed 40,701 who replied (34,440 men) for 50 years, to 2001. Lung-cancer death rates rose with the number of cigarettes smoked a day: about 9 times the never-smokers' rate for 1-14 a day, about 24 times for 25 or more (Grokipedia, British Doctors Study).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/British_Doctors_Study ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Doll

F10. Austin Bradford Hill's "viewpoints" of 1965 include the biological gradient (dose-response): "a causal association is strengthened when the incidence or risk of the disease or outcome exhibits a monotonic increase" with exposure; heavier smokers' higher lung-cancer risk is the key example. "Causation cannot be proven definitively" by them; they are viewpoints, not a checklist (Grokipedia, Bradford Hill criteria).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Bradford_Hill_criteria

F11. Giving up smoking lowers the risk: in the doctors study the excess lung-cancer risk halved after 10 years without smoking (Grokipedia). Peto, Doll and others (BMJ 2000): at 1990 rates the cumulative risk of death from lung cancer by age 75 was 16 percent for British men who kept smoking cigarettes, and 10, 6, 3 and 2 percent for men who stopped at 60, 50, 40 and 30. So most smokers do not die of lung cancer, though smoking multiplies the risk many times.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/British_Doctors_Study ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC27446/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10926586/

F12. Ronald Fisher "established the foundations of modern statistics" (Grokipedia) and argued that "statistical associations alone could not establish causation". In "Cigarettes, Cancer, and Statistics" (Centennial Review, 1958) he asked: "Is it possible, then, that lung cancer-that is to say, the pre-cancerous condition which must exist and is known to exist for years in those who are going to show overt lung cancer-is one of the causes of smoking cigarettes? I don't think it can be excluded." He added, "I think it is more likely that a common cause supplies the explanation. The obvious common cause to think of is the genotype." He was a lifelong pipe smoker and took tobacco-industry funding from 1958 (Grokipedia).
    https://www.york.ac.uk/depts/maths/histstat/fisher274.htm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ronald_Fisher

## Coincidence: a match made by chance

F13. Tyler Vigen, a Harvard law student (A.V. Club; National Geographic: "finishing law school"), runs the website Spurious Correlations, built from public data (the CDC, the USDA, the Census). One chart: "Number of people who drowned by falling into a pool correlates with Films Nicolas Cage appeared in", 1999-2009, r = 0.666. His site: "any sufficiently large dataset will yield strong correlations completely at random"; it now holds "25,237 variables". Grokipedia: the Cage match "is purely coincidental, as no causal mechanism links film releases to drownings"; National Geographic: "Correlations like these are total accidents."
    https://gwern.net/docs/www/tylervigen.com/bdb0831d60f97f488b8d070f920b5c7ca47dccf6.html ; https://tylervigen.com/spurious-correlations ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spurious_relationship ; https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/nick-cage-movies-vs-drownings-and-more-strange-but-spurious-correlations ; https://www.avclub.com/there-s-a-correlation-between-nic-cage-movies-and-swimm-1798268565

## Precision, accuracy and measurement error

F14. Accuracy is how close a measured value comes to the true value; precision is how closely repeated measurements agree (Grokipedia, from the international vocabulary of metrology). "A scale that always reads 5 grams too high yields precise but inaccurate weights." Darts clustered tightly but away from the bullseye show high precision and low accuracy.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Accuracy_and_precision

F15. An error that is the same every time (a systematic error, or bias) spoils accuracy, and repeating the reading with the same instrument repeats it; precision reflects random error. Calibration means "adjusting an instrument to minimize systematic bias by comparing its output to known reference values" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Accuracy_and_precision

F16. The Hubble Space Telescope's primary mirror, 2.4 metres across, was made by Perkin-Elmer (Grokipedia). It was "among the most precisely figured optical mirrors ever made, smooth to about 10 nm" (a hundred-thousandth of a millimetre), but its outer edge was too flat by about 2,200 nm (2.2 micrometres), so its first pictures in 1990 were blurred (spherical aberration; NASA announced the flaw on 27 June 1990). The investigation under Lew Allen found that the reflective null corrector, the testing instrument used to shape the mirror, had been wrongly assembled, one lens being 1.3 mm out of place. Earlier tests with two conventional refractive null correctors had shown the aberration, but "these results were dismissed" because the reflective one was thought more accurate; the board also criticised NASA for relying on a single instrument (Wikipedia). Diagnostic tests in orbit "ruled out misalignment of the secondary mirror" (Grokipedia). Corrective optics (COSTAR and WFPC2) were fitted in December 1993.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_Space_Telescope ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hubble_Space_Telescope

## Why a bigger sample helps

F17. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's hospital problem (Science, 1974): in one town about 45 babies are born each day in the larger hospital and about 15 in the smaller; about 50 percent of babies are boys, the share varying from day to day; for a year each hospital records the days on which more than 60 percent of the babies born were boys. 56 percent of the people asked chose "about the same", 22 percent the larger, 22 percent the smaller. The smaller is right: "smaller samples exhibit greater variability due to sampling error" (Grokipedia); the larger hospital's daily share stays closer to 50 percent (law of large numbers).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insensitivity_to_sample_size ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Insensitivity_to_sample_size

## Survivorship bias: Abraham Wald's bombers

F18. Abraham Wald, born in 1902 in Kolozsvár (now Cluj-Napoca), joined the Statistical Research Group at Columbia University in the summer of 1942. The military gave it hit data from returning bombers, with "concentrations of bullet holes in areas such as wings and fuselages, with comparatively fewer in engines and cockpits" (Grokipedia); engineers first proposed reinforcing the areas with the most holes. Wald took hits to be spread at random over the plane; "only aircraft enduring hits in less critical areas returned for inspection", so the zones with few holes were the vulnerable ones. His memoranda of 1943 (one titled "A Method of Estimating Plane Vulnerability Based on Damage of Survivors") worked the method out; the SRG issued eight such memoranda (Wikipedia). Grokipedia calls the popular tale of Wald directly advising engine armour "apocryphal", lacking "primary corroboration"; the dotted-bomber picture is a hypothetical illustration (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Abraham_Wald ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Survivorship_bias ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivorship_bias ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Wald

F19. Survivorship bias is "a statistical error which results from concentrating on entities that passed a selection process" over those that did not (Wikipedia); Grokipedia: analysis "focuses solely on individuals, groups, or entities that have 'survived'".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivorship_bias ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Survivorship_bias

## Reading a graph: straight or curved

F20. A linear relationship graphs as "a straight line": the output changes "by a fixed amount for every unit increase in the input" (Grokipedia, Linear function); nonlinear relations "curve or bend". Linear growth "increases by a constant additive amount over equal time intervals"; exponential growth multiplies by a fixed factor each period (a constant doubling time), and its graph "rises gradually at first and then accelerates rapidly" (Grokipedia, Exponential growth).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Linear_function ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Exponential_growth

F21. Bacteria multiply by binary fission (one cell splits into two); E. coli "exhibits a doubling time of approximately 20 minutes" in rich broth at 37 degrees Celsius (Grokipedia, Exponential growth; Bacterial growth gives 20 to 60 minutes). When the food runs out ("nutrient exhaustion"), growth stops and the count levels off (the stationary phase).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Exponential_growth ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Bacterial_growth

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. A strong match between two things must mean one causes the other (the slogan this unit answers; F1, F13).
M2. Eating before swimming causes cramps and drowning (F4: it does not).
M3. Readings that agree must be right: precision taken for accuracy (F14).
M4. Taking more readings and averaging removes every error; an error that is the same every time stays (F15, F16).
M5. Small and large samples are equally likely to give an extreme result: 56 percent of Tversky and Kahneman's subjects (F17).
M6. Armour belongs where the returning planes were hit most: the engineers' first proposal (F18).
M7. Steady doubling makes a straight line on a graph; in fact equal additions do, and doubling curves upward ever more steeply (F20).
M8. A mirror flaw found in space must have happened in launch or in space; Hubble's was polished in on Earth, guided by a faulty tester (F16).
