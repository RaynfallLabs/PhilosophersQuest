## Describing a wave: amplitude, frequency, wavelength

F1. Amplitude is "the maximum displacement of a point on a vibrating body or wave from its central position of equilibrium". For a mechanical wave "the energy transported is proportional to the square of the amplitude" (a bigger amplitude carries more energy), and for sound "amplitude correlates directly with perceived loudness". OpenStax: the property of a sound wave that increases when a person speaks more loudly is its amplitude.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Amplitude ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-1-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength

F2. Frequency is "the number of occurrences of a repeating event per unit of time". Its unit, the hertz, is "exactly one cycle per second", named "in honor of the German physicist Heinrich Hertz". For sound, frequency "determines the pitch". Human hearing runs from about 20 Hz to about 20 kHz (20,000 Hz).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Frequency

F3. Wavelength is "the distance between two consecutive corresponding points on a periodic wave that are in phase", for example crest to crest or trough to trough.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wavelength

F4. Wave speed = frequency x wavelength (v = f x wavelength), for all waves. "For a fixed wave speed, the wavelength is inversely proportional to the frequency"; "increasing the frequency shortens the wavelength" (twice the frequency at the same speed, half the wavelength). Sound in air at 20 C: a 1 kHz tone has a wavelength of about 0.34 m; audible sound runs from about 17 m (20 Hz) to about 17 mm (20 kHz).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wavelength ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-1-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength

F5. A loudspeaker's voice coil moves back and forth, "vibrating an attached diaphragm or cone that displaces surrounding air molecules", making the pressure waves of sound. (With F1 and F2: how far the cone moves sets the amplitude and so the loudness; how many times a second it moves sets the frequency and so the pitch.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Loudspeaker ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Amplitude ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Frequency

## Mersenne's strings: frequency and pitch

F6. Marin Mersenne (1588-1648), a French friar of the Minim order, published Harmonie universelle in 1636, which holds "the earliest formulation of what has become known as Mersenne's laws": a stretched string's frequency is inversely proportional to its length, proportional to the square root of its tension, and inversely proportional to the square root of its mass per unit length. St Andrews: he "was the first to publish the laws relating to the vibrating string".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_Mersenne ; https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Mersenne/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Marin_Mersenne ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne%27s_laws

F7. Halving a string's length raises its pitch by an octave, and two strings an octave apart have frequencies in the ratio 1:2 (so the half-length string vibrates twice as often). Harmonie universelle gave "the first absolute determination of the frequency of an audible tone (at 84 Hz)"; Grokipedia gives about 84 air pulsations a second for a low G. OpenStax: "shorter strings vibrate faster and hence produce sounds at higher pitches."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne%27s_laws ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_Mersenne ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Marin_Mersenne ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-1-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength

## The speed of sound, and the old measurements

F8. Sound travels about 343 m/s in dry air at 20 C (about 331 m/s at 0 C), gaining about 0.6 m/s for each degree Celsius.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_sound ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-1-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength

F9. "The speed of sound is independent of the wave's frequency, wavelength, or amplitude in most media" (Grokipedia); OpenStax: its speed is "nearly independent of frequency", so "the music from all instruments arrives in cadence independent of distance".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_sound ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-1-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength

F10. Marin Mersenne's figure for the speed of sound was 1,380 Parisian feet a second, about 448 m/s (Wikipedia; fr.wikipedia gives 1635 and 230 toises a second); it was too high.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_sound ; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitesse_du_son ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Marin_Mersenne

F11. Pierre Gassendi in 1635 timed cannon fired at a distance (about a kilometre, Grokipedia), from the flash to the report, and got about 478 m/s, too high. He showed that the speed is "invariant of pitch" (Wikipedia); fr.wikipedia: Gassendi "montre que les sons graves et aigus se propagent à la même vitesse" (low and high sounds travel at the same speed). Britannica (in a search summary; the page itself refused the fetch) says he correctly concluded that the speed of sound is independent of frequency.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pierre_Gassendi ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_sound ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Gassendi ; https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitesse_du_son

F12. Aristotle "reasoned that high pitches traveled faster than low pitches" (hearinghealthmatters); Britannica (search summary) calls it an error that persisted for many centuries.
    https://hearinghealthmatters.org/waynesworld/2014/sound-quantification

F13. In 1738 the French Académie des sciences charged "MM. de Thury, Maraldi et l'abbé de la Caille" (fr.wikipedia) to time cannon along a line of 14,636 toises (28.5 km) between the tower of Montlhéry and the pyramid of Montmartre. Their findings: "Le son parcourt 173 toises (337,2 m) en une seconde de temps" (sound travels 173 toises, 337.2 m, in one second), the same by day and night and in clear or rainy weather; a crosswind changes nothing, a wind along the line speeds or slows it; and "L'intensité ou la force du son ne changent rien à sa vitesse" (the loudness or force of the sound changes nothing about its speed). Wikipedia: reduced to still air at 0 C their result was 332 m/s, within 1 percent of the modern value. The sound timed was cannon fire: a search summary of the older accounts (Britannica's acoustics pages and nineteenth-century encyclopedias) says cannon were fired at half-hour intervals at Montmartre and Montlhéry, the observers at the other station; the Encyclopaedia Britannica of 1842 tabulates the "French Academicians" of 1738 over baselines of 13,744 to 102,624 feet.
    https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitesse_du_son ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_sound ; https://hearinghealthmatters.org/waynesworld/2014/sound-quantification ; https://jimclifford.ca/early_encyclopedia_britannica/articles/1842/eb_7th_1842_016921.html

## Thunder

F14. Lightning heats the air of its channel to about 30,000 C (NWS: as high as 50,000 F); the air expands explosively and makes the sound wave of thunder. The flash is seen "almost instantaneously"; counting the seconds to the thunder and "dividing that time by 3" gives the distance in kilometres ("3 seconds per kilometer", "5 seconds per mile"; NWS: "5 seconds = 1 mile"). Thunder can typically be heard up to about 16 km (10 miles).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thunder ; https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-science-thunder

## Sound in water and in steel, and why

F15. Sound travels about 1,480 m/s in fresh water at 20 C (Grokipedia 1,482; Wikipedia 1,481; OpenStax 1,480), and about 5,960 m/s in steel (OpenStax; Grokipedia gives 5,000-6,420 m/s for longitudinal waves in steel; Wikipedia 5,120 m/s in iron), so well over ten times as fast in steel as in air.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_sound ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_sound ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-1-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength

F16. Why: the speed rises with the medium's stiffness and falls with its density. OpenStax: "The more rigid (or less compressible) the medium, the faster the speed of sound"; "The greater the density of a medium, the slower the speed of sound"; "Because liquids and solids are relatively rigid and very difficult to compress", sound is faster in them than in gases. In a solid the particles are "tightly packed together and held in relatively fixed positions by strong intermolecular forces", and "this rigidity arises from strong interparticle forces"; solids "resist deformation, compression, and expansion".
    https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-1-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_sound ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solid

F17. Iron's particles are heavier than air's: iron's standard atomic weight is 55.845, against a molar mass of about 29 g/mol (28.9652) for dry air. Iron's density is 7.87 g/cm3 against 1.2 kg/m3 for air at 20 C (several thousand times denser); steel is an iron alloy. So steel's speed comes from its stiffness, in spite of its heavy particles and great density.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Iron ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_of_air

F18. Jean-Daniel Colladon and Charles Sturm measured sound's speed in Lake Geneva in the 1820s (Wikipedia captions 1826; Wirgin's abstract says 1827): an underwater bell "was struck simultaneously with ignition of gunpowder on the first boat", and the flash and the bell's sound were observed from a second boat far off (DOSITS: about 10 miles; Wirgin: 13,487 m). In water at 8 C they found 1,435 m/s, only 3 m/s from today's accepted value. Wikipedia: sound "traveled over four times as fast in water as in air".
    https://dosits.org/people-and-sound/history-of-underwater-acoustics/the-first-studies-of-underwater-acoustics-the-1800s ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Daniel_Colladon ; https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.03777

## Light's speed: Galileo's lanterns and Rømer's moon

F19. Light travels at exactly 299,792,458 m/s in a vacuum, "about 300000 km/s" (Wikipedia). It is slower in water (refractive index about 1.33) and glass.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_light ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light

F20. Galileo (1638) proposed timing the gap between "uncovering a lantern and its perception some distance away" (Wikipedia), with lanterns on distant hilltops and one observer timing the other's response (Grokipedia). He could not tell whether light was instantaneous, but judged that if not it was "extraordinarily rapid" (Wikipedia); the speed was too great to detect with human reaction times over earthly distances (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_light

F21. René Descartes held that the speed of light was infinite (light instantaneous); Robert Hooke called it "so large as to be virtually instantaneous".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light ; https://grokipedia.com/page/R%C3%B8mer%27s_determination_of_the_speed_of_light ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B8mer%27s_determination_of_the_speed_of_light

F22. Ole Rømer, a Danish astronomer working at the Paris Observatory under Giovanni Cassini from 1672, timed the eclipses of Io, Jupiter's innermost large moon, as it came out of Jupiter's shadow ("Io suddenly reappears, as it moves out of the shadow of Jupiter"); Io orbits about every 42.5 hours. When Earth was moving away from Jupiter the eclipses came later than predicted (by about 10 to 12 minutes), and when Earth was moving toward Jupiter they came earlier. He concluded that light has a finite speed, taking about 22 minutes to cross the diameter of Earth's orbit, and presented this to the Académie on 21 November 1676: the "first quantitative evidence that light propagates at a finite speed".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/R%C3%B8mer%27s_determination_of_the_speed_of_light ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ole_R%C3%B8mer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B8mer%27s_determination_of_the_speed_of_light ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light

F23. Christiaan Huygens turned Rømer's timing into "an estimate of speed of light of 220000 km/s" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia's figures run 211,000-230,000). Cassini rejected the finite-speed reading, attributing the discrepancies to irregularities in the satellites' orbits (Grokipedia; Grokipedia's Rømer page: interactions among Jupiter's moons; Wikipedia: the other moons did not seem to show the effect). The idea was not fully accepted until James Bradley's work of 1727-1729.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light ; https://grokipedia.com/page/R%C3%B8mer%27s_determination_of_the_speed_of_light ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ole_R%C3%B8mer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B8mer%27s_determination_of_the_speed_of_light

## Chladni's sand figures

F24. Ernst Chladni (1756-1827) sprinkled fine sand on metal or glass plates and drew a violin bow along the edge; at resonance the sand gathered along the still nodal lines, outlining the patterns now called Chladni figures. He published them in Entdeckungen über die Theorie des Klanges (1787), demonstrated them to the Paris Academy in 1808 with Napoleon in the audience, and is called the "father of acoustics". Robert Hooke had seen nodal patterns in flour on a bowed glass plate on 8 July 1680.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ernst_Chladni ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Chladni ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cymatics

F25. Sand on the moving parts of the plate "bounces off and accumulates on the places that aren't moving (the 'nodes')" (Smithsonian); "The sand moves away from the antinodes and settles in the nodes" (UCSB).
    https://amhistory.si.edu/science/chladni.htm ; https://web.physics.ucsb.edu/~lecturedemonstrations/Composer/Pages/44.45.html

F26. A higher note gives a more detailed pattern: bowing to sound "a much higher tone" "creates a more detailed pattern that has more nodes and smaller open spaces" (Smithsonian); "simpler patterns resonated at lower pitches, and more complex patterns required higher frequencies" (Grokipedia). The plate's lowest ("fundamental") tone gives a large X across the whole plate (Smithsonian).
    https://amhistory.si.edu/science/chladni.htm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ernst_Chladni

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Loudness and pitch are confused with each other: a teaching-research list (Deakin University's documented alternative conceptions, seen only in a search summary; the page refused the fetch) and a curriculum sheet set pitch with how fast the source vibrates and loudness with the strength of the vibration. Related: students take "the level of sound" and "sound intensity" to be the same (Asia-Pacific Forum on Science Learning and Teaching, 2013).
    https://www.eduhk.hk/apfslt/v14_issue1/ozkan/page4.htm
M2. Sound is fastest in air "because sound does not meet with an obstacle when traveling through the atmosphere" (the most common wrong answer in the same study).
    https://www.eduhk.hk/apfslt/v14_issue1/ozkan/page4.htm
M3. High sounds travel faster than low ones (Aristotle's error, F12).
M4. Light takes no time at all to travel (Descartes, F21).
M5. Sound is carried by particles travelling along with it, even in solids (the main misconception found in a study of engineering students, in a search summary). The new bank's T2 unit waves-carry-energy teaches that each particle only moves back and forth near its own place.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26; Grokipedia first)

F27. The shorter string's higher note was known long before Mersenne: "Pythagoras, around 500 BCE, discovered the inverse relationship between the length of a vibrating string and its pitch", and Mersenne "formalized and confirmed the law through precise measurements" (Grokipedia, Mersenne's laws). Wikipedia calls the length rule "the law of Pythagoras" and says Mersenne "investigated and refined these relationships by experiment but did not himself originate them". So musicians had known it since ancient Greece; Mersenne's own step was the frequency law and the first measured frequency (F6, F7).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mersenne's_laws ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne%27s_laws

F28. Rømer's eclipses of Io: "Before opposition, observers can typically see only the ingress (disappearance) of Io into the shadow", and after it only the emergence; Rømer used "telescopic measurements of the immersions and emersions at the Paris Observatory", and the delay kept "building up as Earth receded from Jupiter and diminishing as it approached" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: "the immersion and the emergence cannot be observed for the same eclipse". So he timed Io sliding into the shadow while Earth approached Jupiter and out of it while Earth moved away.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/R%C3%B8mer%27s_determination_of_the_speed_of_light ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B8mer%27s_determination_of_the_speed_of_light

F29. Chladni and Napoleon: Napoleon "awarded him a 6,000-franc honorarium in 1809 following a private demonstration", given at the Tuileries Palace in Paris in 1809 (Grokipedia, Ernst Chladni); H.-J. Stöckmann, "Chladni meets Napoleon" (Eur. Phys. J. Special Topics 145, 2007; read in its abstract and a search summary): through Laplace, Napoleon invited Chladni to the Tuileries one February evening in 1809 for a demonstration of his experiments. This replaces F24's 1808 Academy audience (en.wikipedia only): the card says only that years after 1787 he showed his figures in Paris to Napoleon himself.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ernst_Chladni ; https://epjst.epj.org/articles/epjst/abs/2007/06/st145002/st145002.html

F30. Air does not carry sound inside steel, though many think so: in a study of engineering students, "The main model misconception found was the notion that sound is propagated through the travelling of air particles" "even in solids" (Pejuan, Bohigas and Jaén, UPC). Squeezing air would not speed sound up either: "Pressure has a negligible direct impact on the speed of sound in ideal gases" (Grokipedia, Speed of sound). Sound in solids is "much higher than in gases or liquids, reflecting the greater stiffness of solids" (Grokipedia). Used for the steel question's new wrong choice, "Its particles hold squeezed air that carries the sound".
    https://upcommons.upc.edu/entities/publication/4fd81240-677b-4632-9a5c-18d971b5d11e/full ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_sound

F31. The rope (F4): at one wave speed, twice the frequency gives half the wavelength, so crests a quarter as far apart would take four times the frequency (reasoned from F4's inverse rule).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wavelength
