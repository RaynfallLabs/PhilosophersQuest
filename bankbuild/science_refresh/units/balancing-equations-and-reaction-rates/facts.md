## Balancing an equation

F1. Balancing an equation means adjusting the coefficients (the big numbers in front), "never altering subscripts", so that "the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides of the equation"; formulas must stay unchanged, "as altering subscripts would represent a different substance." This follows from conservation of mass: the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_equation

F2. Hydrogen and oxygen make water: 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O, with four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms on each side. The unbalanced H2 + O2 -> H2O has two oxygen atoms on the left and one on the right. Hydrogen burns in oxygen "yielding water via the highly exothermic reaction 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O", and its combustion emits "primarily water vapor".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_equation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen

F3. H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance from water (H2O); H2 + O2 -> H2O2 has equal atoms on both sides but makes peroxide, not water. MISCONCEPTION: balancing by changing a subscript (writing H2O2 to fix the oxygen count).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_peroxide ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_equation

F4. The Space Shuttle orbiter carried "three RS-25 engines" at its aft end; the main engines "burned liquid hydrogen ... and liquid oxygen" (NASA). The RS-25 "uses ... liquid hydrogen as fuel and liquid oxygen as oxidizer" (Grokipedia).
    https://www.nasa.gov/space-shuttle-recordation/engines/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/RS-25 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-25

F5. Haber's reaction: N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3 (Grokipedia writes it with an equilibrium arrow). Balanced, it has two nitrogen atoms and six hydrogen atoms on each side. Wrong balancings: N2 + 3H2 -> NH3 (N 2 vs 1), N2 + 2H2 -> 3NH3 (N 2 vs 3), N2 + 6H2 -> 2NH3 (H 12 vs 6; the error of counting atoms instead of molecules).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Haber_process ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process

## Fritz Haber and his catalyst

F6. Fritz Haber (1868-1934) was a German physical chemist who devised the catalytic synthesis of ammonia from atmospheric nitrogen and hydrogen; his laboratory demonstration to BASF came in 1909 (Grokipedia gives March-April 1909), using osmium. He won the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (received in 1919-1920).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fritz_Haber ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Haber ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Haber_process

F7. The two nitrogen atoms in N2 are "held together by a very strong triple bond, which must be broken before they can react" (Wikipedia). Industrial plants make ammonia on "a solid catalyst, usually iron"; Alwin Mittasch at BASF found a cheaper iron-based catalyst (from magnetite with promoters) after "testing of over 2,500 compositions" (Grokipedia), and it is still used. Plants run hot and at high pressure (Grokipedia: 400-500 C and 150-300 atmospheres; Wikipedia: 300-500 C and 60-180 bar for modern loops, so no exact figure is used). Caveat: industrial catalysts slowly lose activity by poisoning and sintering and are replaced from time to time (Grokipedia), but the reaction itself does not consume them.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Haber_process

F8. Fertiliser: "The food base of half the current world population is based on the Haber-Bosch process" (Wikipedia); synthetic fertilisers "sustained an estimated 50% of the world's population" (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Haber ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fritz_Haber

F9. Gas warfare: Haber oversaw "the first major deployment of chlorine gas at the Second Battle of Ypres in 1915" (Grokipedia, dated 22 April 1915); he "was on hand personally" when it was first released (Wikipedia), and he is called the "father of chemical warfare".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fritz_Haber ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Haber

## What a catalyst is, and how we know

F10. A catalyst "increases the rate of a chemical reaction without undergoing any permanent change itself" and "is regenerated at the end" (Grokipedia); "When the reaction has finished, you would have exactly the same mass of catalyst as you had at the beginning" (chemguide). It "provides an alternative route for the reaction with a lower activation energy" (chemguide; Grokipedia). Catalysis "impacts only the reaction kinetics and not the equilibrium" (Grokipedia). Berzelius first described catalysis systematically in 1835.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Catalysis ; https://www.chemguide.co.uk/physical/basicrates/catalyst.html

F11. Hydrogen peroxide decomposes into water and oxygen spontaneously, "albeit slowly under ambient conditions", and the reaction is exothermic; manganese dioxide "dramatically accelerates" it and works without being consumed (Grokipedia). Classroom test: after the reaction, filter the manganese(IV) oxide out, "wash, dry, and weigh it. Its mass is still 1 g, which shows it hasn't been used up" (Cognito, GCSE); a glowing splint relights in the gas. Different catalysts reach the same final volume of oxygen, only at different rates (Cognito: manganese(IV) oxide and copper(II) oxide both reach 48 cm3).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_peroxide ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Catalysis ; https://cognito.org/courses/gcse/chemistry-combined/iedexcel/notes/YWF4TFh3Rlg/7.7-catalysts-for-the-decomposition-of-hydrogen-peroxide

F12. The enzyme catalase, found in organisms such as yeast and liver, and in blood, speeds the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide; enzymes are biological catalysts (Grokipedia). (Enzymes in depth are the T5 respiration unit's.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_peroxide ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Catalysis

F13. A car's catalytic converter is a ceramic honeycomb "coated with precious metals such as platinum, palladium, and rhodium", its shape chosen "to maximize surface area for gas contact"; it converts carbon monoxide, unburned hydrocarbons and nitrogen oxides into carbon dioxide, water and nitrogen; the metals "lower activation energies ... without being consumed". Lead from leaded petrol "rapidly deactivated" the metals, so unleaded fuel came in with US converters from 1975.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Catalytic_converter ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Catalysis

## Kinds of reaction

F14. Metal + acid -> salt + hydrogen: "hydrogen production is a characteristic property of the reaction of metals and acids"; magnesium, zinc and iron react with hydrochloric and sulfuric acid, e.g. Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2, and the same with Zn (zinc chloride and hydrogen) (RSC). Grokipedia: hydrogen comes from "metals such as zinc and iron with dilute acids like sulfuric acid or hydrochloric acid" (Cavendish's "inflammable air").
    https://edu.rsc.org/resources/metals-and-acids-experiment/446.article ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen

F15. Gas tests: a lit splint held at the mouth of a tube of hydrogen gives "a distinctive 'squeaky pop'" (Wikipedia; RSC "pop test"); a glowing splint "flares, and re-ignites" in concentrated oxygen (Wikipedia; Cognito). Carbon dioxide is "nonflammable" and is used in fire extinguishers, smothering fires (Grokipedia); it is made by acids acting on carbonates such as calcium carbonate (Grokipedia). Oxygen is not a fuel: "oxygen is not flammable ... It is an oxidizer that supports combustion, but it does not burn on its own" (Science Notes); in the fire triangle oxygen is "the essential oxidizer alongside fuel and an ignition source" (Grokipedia). MISCONCEPTION: every acid fizz is carbon dioxide (students meet the marble and antacid fizz first).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splint_(laboratory_equipment) ; https://edu.rsc.org/resources/metals-and-acids-experiment/446.article ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Carbon_dioxide ; https://sciencenotes.org/is-oxygen-flammable/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Oxygen

F16. Neutralisation: "an acid reacts with a base to form a salt and water"; HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O; it combines "hydrogen ions (H+) from the acid and hydroxide ions (OH-) from the base to produce water" (Grokipedia). The salt is "an ionic compound"; here it is sodium chloride, table salt. Other acids and bases give other salts (H2SO4 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O). MISCONCEPTION: that neutralising only waters the acid and base down, rather than making new substances.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Neutralization_(chemistry) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Acid

F17. Rust is "primarily hydrated iron(III) oxide", formed "through oxidation in the presence of oxygen and moisture"; "dry iron does not rust"; corrosion is "100-fold faster ... in oxygenated water versus deaerated conditions"; "salts in the electrolyte (e.g., NaCl) increase conductivity and corrosion rates" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rust

F18. The RSC rusting practical: tube 1, nail in water open to air; tube 2, nail in boiled water (boiling drives off dissolved air) with "a little oil over the surface to prevent air from reaching the water"; tube 3, nail in salt water; tube 4, nail in air over anhydrous calcium chloride, which absorbs water, under a bung. Left at least three days, "the nails in tubes 2 and 4 do not rust" and "The nail in tube 3 rusts the most"; "water and air (actually oxygen in the air) are essential for rusting"; "Salt can increase the rate of rusting."
    https://edu.rsc.org/experiments/what-causes-iron-to-rust/434.article ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rust

F19. Combustion is an exothermic reaction between a fuel and an oxidizer, usually oxygen from the air, giving heat and usually light; complete combustion of hydrocarbons gives carbon dioxide and water (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Combustion

## What speeds a reaction, and why

F20. Collision theory: "Successful reactions require collisions with sufficient kinetic energy to overcome the activation energy barrier" and proper orientation (Grokipedia); "This minimum energy required is called the activation energy for the reaction" (chemguide). Collision frequency "scales with the product of concentrations" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Collision_theory ; https://www.chemguide.co.uk/physical/basicrates/temperature.html

F21. Temperature: "For many chemical processes, reaction rates are approximately doubled when the temperature is raised by 10 C" (OpenStax; Grokipedia says the same, "due to a greater proportion of successful collisions"); chemguide calls the doubling a rough rule for many reactions near room temperature. From 293 K to 303 K (20 to 30 C) the collision frequency of a gas rises only by "an increase of 1.7% for a 10 degree rise" (it goes as the square root of the kelvin temperature), which cannot explain a doubling; the main effect is the "large increase in the number of the very energetic particles", those at or above the activation energy. The activation energy stays where it is; warming "changes the shape of the graph" (chemguide). Lowering the barrier is what a catalyst does (F10).
    https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/12-2-factors-affecting-reaction-rates ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Collision_theory ; https://www.chemguide.co.uk/physical/basicrates/temperature.html

F22. MISCONCEPTION (particles): students think particles themselves "expand when they are heated", and that particles "may explode burn, contract, expand and / or change shape" (RSC, Beyond Appearances).
    https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-particulate-nature-of-matter-beyond-appearances/4017763.article

F23. Concentration: "phosphorus burns much more rapidly in an atmosphere of pure oxygen than in air, which is only about 20% oxygen" (OpenStax); air is about 21% oxygen by volume, and in oxygen-rich air materials "non-combustible in air ... can ignite readily and burn more vigorously" (Grokipedia). At the same temperature all gases have the same average kinetic energy, and "lighter molecules move faster on average than heavier molecules" (OpenStax), so oxygen molecules (O2, mass 32) are, if anything, slightly slower than nitrogen molecules (N2, mass 28).
    https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/12-2-factors-affecting-reaction-rates ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Oxygen ; https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/9-5-the-kinetic-molecular-theory

F24. Apollo 1: on 27 January 1967, in a plugs-out test at Launch Complex 34, Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee died in a fire in their command module. The cabin held pure oxygen at 16.7 psi, about 2 psi above the air outside; the fire was "most probably initiated by a minor electrical malfunction, such as a short circuit or arcing", and nylon Velcro and polyurethane foam fed it (Grokipedia). The first cry of "Fire!" came at 6:31:04.7 and the cabin wall ruptured at 6:31:19 (Wikipedia). The review board named "A pure oxygen atmosphere at higher than atmospheric pressure" as a major contributing factor; afterwards the launch atmosphere was 60% oxygen and 40% nitrogen.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Apollo_1 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_1

F25. Surface area: "Large pieces of wood smolder, smaller pieces burn rapidly, and saw dust burns explosively" (OpenStax). "Dust has a very large surface area compared to its mass" and burning "can only occur at the surface of a solid or liquid, where it can react with oxygen"; a 1 kg sphere has a surface of 0.048 m2, but as 50-micrometre particles about 120 m2 (Wikipedia). Smaller particles give "increasing surface area for faster combustion and better dispersion in air"; a dust explosion needs the dust dispersed into a cloud (Grokipedia). (Wikipedia also notes each tiny particle catches fire with much less energy than the bulk; no wrong choice may say so.)
    https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/12-2-factors-affecting-reaction-rates ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_explosion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dust_explosion

F26. On 2 May 1878 "a spark ignited airborne flour dust" in the Washburn A Mill in Minneapolis, on the Mississippi; the blast "killed 14 workers instantly" and the fire four more, 18 in all (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: "killing 18 people"). Grain elevators blew up too, among them a series in the 1970s and 1980s that led to US grain-handling rules (Grokipedia). Most deaths come from secondary explosions, when a first blast throws settled dust into the air (Wikipedia; Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washburn_A_Mill ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_explosion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dust_explosion

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- Balancing by changing a formula's small numbers (F1, F3), or counting atoms as molecules (F5).
- Every acid fizz is carbon dioxide (F15); oxygen itself burns (F15, F23).
- Neutralising only dilutes the acid and the base (F16).
- Iron rusts in water alone, or in air alone (F17, F18).
- Heat makes particles swell or break up (F22); warming lowers the energy a reaction needs, which is a catalyst's work, not warmth's (F10, F21).
- A powder is a different, stronger stuff than the lump (F25: same substance, more surface).
- A catalyst is slowly used up, or adds energy (F10, F11).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-27)

F27. Slips in choosing the numbers for N2 + H2 -> NH3, all checked against the balanced N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3 (F5): after a 2 is set in front of NH3 (two nitrogen atoms, six hydrogen atoms on the right), a 3 in front of H2 gives six hydrogen atoms on the left; a 6 gives twelve (atoms counted instead of H2 molecules); a 2 gives four (the NH3 number copied); no number gives two (nitrogen balanced, hydrogen forgotten). Balancing is done "adjusting coefficients - never altering subscripts" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_equation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Haber_process

F28. A heap or layer of dust burns slowly; a cloud of the same dust explodes: "ignition in layers often leads to smoldering rather than explosive combustion unless airflow disperses them", and an explosion "requires the dust to be airborne and within explosive concentration limits" (Grokipedia, Dust explosion). Burning "can only occur at the surface of a solid or liquid, where it can react with oxygen" (Wikipedia, Dust explosion), so dispersal puts air against every particle's surface. Secondary explosions come from "dust layers disturbed by the blast" (Grokipedia). The specks' own drifting speed is not what makes a reaction fast: collision theory concerns the energy of colliding molecules, set by temperature (F20, F21), and fineness is held fixed when the same dust is heaped or dispersed. Mechanical sparks from friction in machinery can ignite dust (Grokipedia), but that is a source of ignition, not why a cloud burns faster than a heap.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dust_explosion ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_explosion
