## Watersheds and divides

F1. A watershed (drainage basin; "catchment" in British use) is all the land from which runoff from rain and snowmelt drains to one common outlet, such as a point on a river, a lake, a bay or the sea. USGS: "all of the land that drains water to the outflow point is the watershed for that outflow location." NOAA: a watershed "channels rainfall and snowmelt to creeks, streams, and rivers, and eventually to outflow points such as reservoirs, bays, and the ocean."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Drainage_basin ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/watersheds-and-drainage-basins ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/watershed.html

F2. Watersheds are bounded by drainage divides, "the boundary of elevated terrain, typically ridges or hills, that separates adjacent drainage basins" (Grokipedia). Water falling on one side of a divide flows into one basin, and water on the other side into another. USGS: "Ridges and hills that separate two watersheds are called the drainage divide." The rivers and streams are the channels running through a basin to its outlet; the divides, not the rivers, form its edges. North America's Continental Divide, along the Rocky Mountains, sends Pacific-bound and Atlantic-bound flows in different directions.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Drainage_divide ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Drainage_basin ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/watersheds-and-drainage-basins

F3. Watersheds nest: "Larger watersheds contain many smaller watersheds" (USGS); smaller catchments feed larger basins in a branching hierarchy (Grokipedia). A watershed can be "as small as a footprint" or as large as all the land draining to Chesapeake Bay (USGS).
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/watersheds-and-drainage-basins ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Drainage_basin

F4. What happens on the land of a watershed reaches the water downstream: "the streamflow and the water quality of a river are affected by things, human-induced or not, happening in the land area 'above' the river-outflow point" (USGS). Running water "often picks up pollutants" that end in the reservoir, bay or ocean (NOAA); land uses such as farming and cities add sediment, nutrients and pollutants, and "alterations in one basin can propagate effects" (Grokipedia).
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/watersheds-and-drainage-basins ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/watershed.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Drainage_basin

F5. The Chesapeake Bay itself lies between the Delmarva Peninsula and the Maryland and Virginia mainland, but its watershed covers about 64,000 square miles (about 166,000 square kilometres) in six states (Delaware, Maryland, New York, Pennsylvania, Virginia, West Virginia) and the District of Columbia, home to about 18 million people. About half the bay's fresh water comes from the Susquehanna River, which rises at Otsego Lake near Cooperstown, New York, and flows 444 miles to the head of the bay at Havre de Grace, Maryland. New York borders Lake Ontario to the south and southeast, and Lake Ontario drains by the St Lawrence River to the Atlantic, so the New York hills of the Susquehanna lie far nearer to Lake Ontario than to the bay (author's map reading: Pennsylvania lies between New York and Maryland).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chesapeake_Bay ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Susquehanna_River ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lake_Ontario ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/watersheds-and-drainage-basins

## Groundwater, aquifers and the water table

F6. Groundwater is water underground that fills the pores (spaces between grains) and cracks in soil and rock. USGS: "Some people think groundwater is like a large lake or river underground, but that isn't the case"; water underground "is more like water in a sponge." Open underground channels are rare (caves).
    https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-groundwater ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/aquifers-and-groundwater

F7. An aquifer is saturated, permeable rock or sediment able to yield useful amounts of water to wells and springs (Grokipedia: "sufficient saturated permeable material capable of yielding significant quantities of water to wells or springs"). CK-12 Middle School Earth Science: "A rock layer must be porous and permeable to be a good aquifer."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Aquifer ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Earth_Science/08%3A_Earth's_Fresh_Water/8.14%3A_Groundwater

F8. Porosity is the share of a material's volume that is open space; permeability is how easily water moves through it, "governed by the size, shape, and interconnectivity of pores rather than their total volume alone" (Grokipedia). CK-12: "If water can move through a rock, the rock is permeable."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Aquifer ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Earth_Science/08%3A_Earth's_Fresh_Water/8.14%3A_Groundwater

F9. Clay is very porous but nearly impermeable: its porosity is often more than 0.5 (more than half its volume is space; clay of bulk density 1.1-1.3 g/cm3 is about 0.51-0.58 pore space), yet "clays typically have very low hydraulic conductivity" because the pore throats are tiny (Wikipedia); Grokipedia: "high-porosity materials like clay can exhibit low permeability if pores are fine and poorly connected", so clay layers act as aquitards. USGS: "Groundwater moves very slowly through low permeability materials such as clay and shale." Sandy soil is about 0.36-0.43 pore space and unsorted gravel about 0.20 (Wikipedia), less than clay, but coarse gravels "yield high permeability" even with lower porosity, so water moves quickly to wells (Grokipedia). Solid granite has porosity below 0.005 (Wikipedia), so it yields water only where it is cracked. Silt grains are between clay and sand in size, and silt lets water through slowly (author's note from the same sources' grain-size ordering).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porosity ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Aquifer ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/aquifers-and-groundwater

F10. The water table is "the upper surface of the zone of saturation" (Grokipedia; USGS: "The upper surface of this zone of saturation is called the water table"): below it the pores and cracks are full of water. It is "the level to which water will rise in a well dug into the ground." It rises in wet seasons and falls in dry ones and where water is pumped out; it is "a subdued replica of the land surface topography," shallower under valleys than under hills.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_table ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/aquifers-and-groundwater

F11. A well gives water only where it reaches below the water table. Dug wells "go dry during periods of drought if the water table drops below the well bottom"; when the water level falls below the pump's intake, the well draws air and has gone dry (USGS).
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/groundwater-wells

F12. "Pumping a well lowers the water level in and around the well to form a cone of depression in the water table"; if the cone reaches nearby wells, "the water level in those wells will be lowered" (USGS). Penn State: a cone of depression can "lower the water table below the level of the well", and "the only solution for this is to drill the well deeper." Drawdown is greatest at the pumping well and fades with distance, and the ground below the lowered water table stays saturated.
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/groundwater-wells ; https://courses.ems.psu.edu/earth103/node/900 ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/aquifers-and-groundwater

F13. Recharge is water from rain and surface water soaking down to refill an aquifer (USGS: "Precipitation eventually adds water (recharge) into the porous rock of the aquifer"). Pumping faster than recharge lowers the water table, dries wells, reduces streamflow, and can cause land subsidence and, near coasts, saltwater intrusion (Grokipedia; USGS). Under the High Plains, in parts of eight states, "water levels have declined more than 100 feet in some areas" (USGS); CK-12 Middle School: "People may use the water in an aquifer faster than it is recharged. When this happens, the water table will lower... Wells may go dry."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Aquifer ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/groundwater-decline-and-depletion ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Earth_Science/08%3A_Earth's_Fresh_Water/8.18%3A_Groundwater_Depletion

F14. Land subsidence: when groundwater is pumped out, the water pressure in the pores drops and the weight above shifts onto the grains, which rearrange and pack together; fine clays and silts compress far more than sands and gravels, and the compaction is largely permanent (Grokipedia). In California's San Joaquin Valley, farmers relied on groundwater from the 1920s, and overpumping caused "aquifer-system compaction and land subsidence"; "some areas had subsided by as much as 28 feet" (about 8.5 metres), with "permanent aquifer-system storage loss" (USGS; Grokipedia gives more than 9 metres in places). Karst sinkholes are a different process: acidic groundwater dissolves limestone and the ground over the void collapses; caves do not form in sand and clay (Grokipedia). CK-12 Middle School: "As the water table lowers, the ground above the aquifer may sink."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Subsidence ; https://www.usgs.gov/centers/land-subsidence-in-california/science/land-subsidence-san-joaquin-valley ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Earth_Science/08%3A_Earth's_Fresh_Water/8.18%3A_Groundwater_Depletion

## People change the water: paving, wetlands, dams

F15. Paving: roads, roofs and parking lots (impervious surfaces) block rain from soaking in, and drains carry the runoff straight to streams, so in developed watersheds "much more water arrives into a stream more quickly," with "more frequent and more severe flooding"; paving reduces "the area where infiltration to groundwater can occur" (USGS). Runoff reaches 70-90 percent of rain in highly urbanised areas against 10-20 percent in undeveloped ones (Grokipedia). Texas's grade-7 standard 7.11A clarifies: "The amount of impervious cover in an area is inversely proportional to the amount of rain that becomes groundwater."
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/impervious-surfaces-and-flooding ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Impervious_surface ; https://teksguide.org/teks/s711a-0/alignments

F16. Less soaking in means less recharge: in urbanised catchments, dry-weather baseflow (the groundwater-fed flow that keeps a stream running when it is not raining) often falls by 20-50 percent "because recharge is curtailed" (Grokipedia); EPA's CADDIS lists decreased infiltration from impervious surfaces as a cause of lower baseflow. CAVEAT: not universal; leaky water and sewer pipes and lawn watering can add water underground, and in a few cities baseflow rose. No question keys the creek's dry-weather flow; the key is only that less RAIN soaks down.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Impervious_surface ; https://19january2021snapshot.epa.gov/caddis-vol2/caddis-volume-2-sources-stressors-responses-urbanization-hydrology_.html ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/impervious-surfaces-and-flooding

F17. Wetlands "function as natural sponges" that trap and slowly release surface water, rain, snowmelt, groundwater and floodwater (EPA); draining 64 million acres of wetland in the Upper Mississippi Basin "intensified the 1993 floods" (Grokipedia). Wetlands also filter: sediment settles and bacteria remove nitrate (Grokipedia). (Context only.)
    https://www.epa.gov/wetlands/why-are-wetlands-important ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Wetland

F18. Dams trap sediment: a reservoir can trap "up to 90-100% of incoming bedload," well-designed reservoirs often trap more than 80 percent of their sediment, and Lake Nasser behind Egypt's Aswan High Dam traps over 95 percent of the Nile's (Grokipedia). Below a dam the clear "hungry water" scours its bed; deltas and coasts, starved of sand and mud, wear back: after the Aswan High Dam was completed in 1970 the Nile delta's Rosetta promontory retreated faster (from about 58 to about 125 metres a year) (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: the dam "has increased coastline erosion surrounding the Nile Delta." USGS: the two Elwha River dams "stopped most of the flow of sediment to the beaches on its delta for nearly 100 years," and the beaches eroded nearly 2 feet a year (1939-2006). Dams also reduce the peak floods that once spread silt over a valley's fields, and block fish migration.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Environmental_impact_of_reservoirs ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aswan_Dam ; https://www.usgs.gov/centers/pcmsc/news/final-beach-erosion-survey-elwha-river-delta-dam-removal

## Dead zones

F19. A dead zone is hypoxia: water so low in dissolved oxygen (below about 2 milligrams a litre) that most marine life dies or moves away (NOAA; Grokipedia). Excess nutrients "that run off land or are piped as wastewater into rivers and coasts can stimulate an overgrowth of algae, which then sinks and decomposes in the water. The decomposition process consumes oxygen" (NOAA); "the bacteria feeding on the dead plankton consume oxygen" (NOAA NCCOS). Fresh river water is less dense than salt water and floats on top (stratification), so the low-oxygen water is "trapped under the fresh layer" near the bottom (NOAA NCCOS). The zone forms in spring and summer and breaks up by autumn as the water mixes (Grokipedia).
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/deadzone.html ; https://coastalscience.noaa.gov/?p=6900 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dead_zone_(ecology)

F20. The Gulf dead zone at the mouth of the Mississippi is fed by nutrients from farming and cities "throughout the Mississippi River watershed" (USGS); the Mississippi-Missouri basin covers about 3.2 million square kilometres, with about 71 percent of its nitrogen load from agriculture (Grokipedia). Its size each summer tracks the spring river flow and nutrient load: NOAA forecasts it from USGS May discharge and nutrient data. 2019: "abnormally high amount of spring rainfall" across the watershed "led to record high river flows and much larger nutrient loading," and NOAA forecast 7,829 square miles (USGS). 2012: Midwest drought brought "near record low levels of water traveling down the Mississippi," which "substantially reduced the amount of nutrients delivered to Gulf," and the survey "found the fourth smallest dead zone on record" (NOAA NCCOS), about 2,889 square miles. The record is 8,776 square miles (2017) (Grokipedia). In the Chesapeake Bay, the 2023 low-oxygen volume was a record low, attributed largely to below-average rainfall (Grokipedia). CAVEAT: river flow alone does not fix the size; nitrate load can vary on its own (2025), so "most likely" is the honest wording.
    https://www.usgs.gov/news/national-news-release/very-large-dead-zone-forecast-gulf-mexico ; https://coastalscience.noaa.gov/?p=6900 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dead_zone_(ecology) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Drainage_basin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chesapeake_Bay

## Plastic in the ocean

F21. Garbage patches are "large areas of the ocean where litter, fishing gear, and other debris" collect; they form in "rotating ocean currents called 'gyres'", which "pull debris into one location, often the gyre's center" (NOAA Marine Debris Program). A gyre is "a large-scale system of rotating surface ocean currents that encompasses an entire ocean basin," driven by wind and turned into closed loops by Earth's rotation (clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere); wind-driven transport pushes floating plastic toward the gyre's middle, where the circulation keeps it (Grokipedia). The Great Pacific Garbage Patch lies in the North Pacific gyre, roughly midway between Hawaii and California (NOAA); the gyre is formed by the California, North Equatorial, Kuroshio and North Pacific currents turning clockwise round the ocean (NOAA Ocean Service), so it gathers what drifts from coasts around the Pacific.
    https://marinedebris.noaa.gov/discover-marine-debris/garbage-patches ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ocean_gyre ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial-coastal/marine-debris/md05.html ; https://response.restoration.noaa.gov/node/688

F22. "'Patch' is a misleading nickname, causing many to believe that these are islands of trash" (NOAA); the debris is "mostly made up (by count) of plastic pieces smaller than 5mm in size," spread from the surface to the sea floor, and a boat can "sail through some areas of the Great Pacific Garbage Patch and see very little to no debris." Marine debris comes from poor waste management, dumping, littering and stormwater runoff (NOAA). Tides only rise and fall, and waves push floating things toward shore (author's note; tides are taught in the T2 unit the-tides).
    https://marinedebris.noaa.gov/discover-marine-debris/garbage-patches

## Overfishing

F23. Overfishing is the "removal of any species of fish from water at a rate that the species cannot replenish" (Wikipedia); Grokipedia: fishing a stock above its maximum sustainable yield. FAO: about a third of assessed marine stocks are overfished (34 percent in 2017; Grokipedia reports 35.5 percent in 2021).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Overfishing

F24. "Slow-growing fish that reproduce late in life, such as orange roughy, are vulnerable to overfishing" (Wikipedia, Overfishing). The orange roughy lives in the deep sea (mostly about 600-1,200 metres), does "not begin to breed until they are at least 20 years old" (stock assessments use 23-40 years), lives more than a century (validated ages up to about 149 years; claims of 200 or more), and has low fecundity; "slow-growing and late to mature, resulting in a very low stock resilience," and many stocks "became severely depleted within 3-20 years" of fishing (Wikipedia; Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_roughy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Orange_roughy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing

F25. Sardines "reach sexual maturity... usually by the end of the first year of life," live typically 3-5 years, and spawn in many batches over a long season: a "fast life-history strategy" (Grokipedia). CAVEAT: sardine stocks still boom and bust and can be overfished; the question asks only which of two populations losing the same share is overfished MORE easily.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sardine

F26. The northern cod off Newfoundland: the catch peaked at 810,000 tons in 1968; by 1992 the stock had fallen to about 1 percent of historic levels and Canada closed the fishery; over 35,000 fishermen and plant workers lost their work; overfishing was the main cause, with cold water and seals at most amplifiers; a limited fishery reopened in 2024 (Wikipedia; Grokipedia). (Context only.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_the_Atlantic_northwest_cod_fishery ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Collapse_of_the_Atlantic_northwest_cod_fishery

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. That groundwater sits in underground lakes or flows in underground rivers (USGS says so in as many words; F6).
    https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-groundwater

M2. That a watershed is the river or the land beside it, or the nearest body of water; in a study of middle- and high-school students' drawings, students understood watersheds "from a very limited scientific perspective," and humans "do not appear to be a part of a watershed, but separate from it" (Shepardson, Harbor and Wee, Journal of Geoscience Education 53, 2005).
    https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/18333.html

M3. That rivers mark the edges of watersheds (author's note: the edges are the divides, the rivers run through the middle; F2).

M4. That the material holding the most water makes the best aquifer (clay is the counterexample; F9).

M5. Dilution: that more river water thins pollution and so shrinks a dead zone; the record shows wet springs bring larger dead zones and drought years smaller ones (F20).

M6. That a garbage patch is a floating island of rubbish (NOAA; F22).

M7. That a well that runs dry has emptied the whole aquifer, or that a well taps a pocket or stream of its own (author's note: the cone of depression is local, and the ground below it stays saturated; F12).

M8. That fishing takes the same toll on any population that loses the same share, whatever its breeding (author's note; F23-F24).
