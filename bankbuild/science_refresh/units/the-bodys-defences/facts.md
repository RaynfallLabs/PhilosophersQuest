## The barriers: skin, mucus, tiny hairs, stomach acid

F1. Unbroken skin is the body's largest and toughest barrier against germs. Its outer layer is made of dead skin cells packed with keratin, which make a dry, slightly acidic surface that germs find hard to get through, and the layer is shed all the time.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Innate_immune_system ("The skin represents the largest and most robust anatomical barrier"; outer layer "composed of dead keratinocytes filled with keratin"; "creates a dry, acidic surface that desiccates and mechanically resists penetration by microbes"; "continuous shedding of the superficial skin layer") ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/42-1-innate-immune-response ("the skin functions as a continuous, impassable barrier to potentially infectious pathogens"; "Pathogens are killed or inactivated on the skin by desiccation (drying out) and by the skin's acidity") ; https://kidshealth.org/en/teens/immune.html ("the skin acts as a barrier to block germs from entering the body")

F2. The skin's surface carries a thin film of sweat and oil (the acid mantle) with a pH of about 4.5 to 5.5, and the acidity holds back the growth of harmful germs.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Skin (the acid mantle is "a hydrolipidic film on the surface" from sebum and sweat that "maintains a pH of 4.5-5.5"; it "inhibits the growth of pathogenic microbes")

F3. Cold viruses get into the body through the moist linings of the nose and throat, not through the skin. They spread in droplets and by "self-inoculation": hands carrying the virus touch the lining of the nose, the eyes or the mouth. People touch their eyes, nose and mouth often without realising it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Common_cold (the cold is "primarily affecting the mucous membranes of the nose and throat"; the viruses "invade and replicate within respiratory epithelial cells"; "A key secondary route involves self-inoculation, where contaminated hands touch the nasal mucosa, eyes, or mouth") ; https://www.cdc.gov/clean-hands/data-research/facts-stats/index.html ("People frequently touch their eyes, nose, and mouth without even realizing it")

F4. A cold's incubation period, from catching the virus to the first signs, is 1 to 3 days; a cold lasts 7 to 10 days on average.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Common_cold ("The incubation period lasts 1 to 3 days"; "The average total duration is 7 to 10 days")

F5. Sticky mucus lines the nose and the airways and traps dust, germs and other particles that are breathed in. The nose and sinuses make about a litre of mucus a day, most of it moved backward by cilia and swallowed without notice.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mucus ("Mucus in the airways captures environmental threats such as dust, pathogens, and irritants"; the nose and sinuses "produce approximately 1 liter (about 1 quart) of mucus daily", "most of which is moved backward by cilia and swallowed unnoticed") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Innate_immune_system ("trapping inhaled microbes and particulates in a gel-like matrix")

F6. The airways are lined with cilia, tiny hair-like structures on the lining cells, which beat together 10 to 20 times a second and push the mucus, with its trapped germs, toward the throat (the pharynx), where it is swallowed or coughed up. The process is called the mucociliary escalator.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Innate_immune_system ("ciliated epithelial cells propel the mucus layer upward via coordinated beating at 10-20 Hz") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mucus (cilia "propels the mucus gel layer toward the pharynx") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mucociliary_clearance ("directed to the pharynx where the transported mucus is either swallowed or coughed up"; also called "the mucociliary escalator") ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/42-1-innate-immune-response ("cilia in the nasal passages and respiratory tract that push the mucus with the pathogens out of the body")

F7. Cigarette smoke shortens the cilia and leaves fewer cells with cilia, which makes the airways more open to infection.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mucociliary_clearance ("Chronic exposure to cigarette smoke shortens cilia length and reduces ciliated cell density"; "These effects collectively heighten vulnerability to infections")

F8. The hairs just inside the nostrils trap larger particles in the air, such as dust, pollen and germs, and work together with the mucus that coats them.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nasal_hair (nasal hairs act as a filter "trapping larger airborne particles such as dust, pollen, and pathogens"; they "work in tandem with the overlying mucus layer to trap inhaled particles")

F9. The stomach's juices hold hydrochloric acid, with a pH of about 1.5 to 3.5. The acid kills most of the bacteria and other germs swallowed with food and drink, and it also helps digest food (it switches on the enzyme pepsin). People whose stomachs make too little acid catch more gut infections.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gastric_acid (gastric acid is "primarily composed of hydrochloric acid (HCl)", "a pH typically ranging from 1.5 to 3.5"; "the acidic conditions eradicate most ingested bacteria and parasites, preventing infections"; "activates pepsinogen ... into active pepsin"; achlorhydria increases "susceptibility to enteric infections") ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/42-1-innate-immune-response ("the low pH of the stomach (which inhibits the growth of pathogens)")

F10. Bile is made in the liver, stored in the gallbladder and released into the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine, where it breaks up fats. It does its work after the stomach, not before.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Bile ("continuously produced by hepatocytes in the liver"; "The gallbladder functions as the principal reservoir"; "secreted into the duodenum to emulsify dietary fats")

## White blood cells and what they do

F11. White blood cells (leukocytes) defend the body against infection. They are made in the bone marrow and travel in the blood, and they are fewer than 1 percent of all blood cells.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/White_blood_cell ("primarily responsible for defending against infections, pathogens, and foreign substances"; "are produced in the bone marrow and circulate through the bloodstream"; they "constitute less than 1% of the total number of blood cells") ; https://kidshealth.org/en/kids/immune.html

F12. White blood cells squeeze out of the small blood vessels into a hurt or infected place, drawn by chemical signals. Neutrophils, the most common white blood cells, arrive first, within hours.
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/42-1-innate-immune-response ("Neutrophils and monocytes leave the capillaries") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/White_blood_cell ("Migration of WBCs from the bloodstream into tissues is primarily driven by chemotaxis"; "Neutrophils are the most abundant type of white blood cell") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Inflammation ("neutrophils predominate in the early phase (within hours)")

F13. Many white blood cells, called phagocytes (neutrophils and macrophages among them), destroy germs by engulfing them, taking them inside, and digesting them (phagocytosis).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/42-1-innate-immune-response ("A neutrophil is also a phagocytic leukocyte that engulfs and digests pathogens"; "macrophages and dendritic cells engulf pathogens and cellular debris through phagocytosis") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/White_blood_cell (neutrophils "primarily engulf and destroy bacteria through phagocytosis") ; https://kidshealth.org/en/kids/immune.html ("phagocytes ... chew up invading organisms")

F14. Other parts of the blood have other jobs: platelets form the clots that plug a cut and stop bleeding, and red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to the body. Mucus is made by the linings of the airways, not by white blood cells.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Platelet ("platelets primarily function in hemostasis by forming clots to prevent excessive bleeding following vascular injury") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Red_blood_cell ("the transport of oxygen from the lungs to body tissues via hemoglobin") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mucus

F15. Inflammation shows as redness, heat, swelling and pain. The place goes red because the small blood vessels there widen, and warm because more warm blood flows in; the swelling comes from fluid and white blood cells moving out of the vessels.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Inflammation ("the classic cardinal signs of redness (rubor), heat (calor), swelling (tumor), pain (dolor)"; "Redness results from vasodilation of arterioles and capillaries"; "Heat stems from this enhanced perfusion, delivering warmer arterial blood") ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/42-1-innate-immune-response ("the localized redness, swelling, heat, and pain that result from the movement of leukocytes and fluid")

F16. Pus is a thick, whitish-yellow fluid that forms where the body fights an infection, most often a bacterial one. It is made mostly of white blood cells, chiefly neutrophils, dead and dying after the fight, in a protein-rich fluid with broken-down tissue and germs.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pus ("a thick, whitish-yellow or greenish fluid"; produced "particularly in response to bacterial infection"; its formation "results from the influx of neutrophils and other immune cells to the site of infection"; neutrophils are the main cell type, with "liquefied tissue debris, proteins, and microorganisms") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pus ("dead leukocytes (white blood cells) from the body's immune response (mostly neutrophils)"; "As the neutrophils die off from toxins and old age, they are destroyed by macrophages, forming the viscous pus")

## Fever

F17. A fever is a controlled rise in body temperature, above about 38 degrees Celsius: the body's temperature control centre in the brain (the hypothalamus) is set higher, and the body makes heat to reach it, which is why a fever often starts with chills and shivering. Immune cells set it off by releasing chemical signals.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fever (fever is "a temporary elevation in an individual's core body temperature above the normal hypothalamic set-point", "a regulated increase orchestrated by the body's thermoregulatory center", "typically exceeding 100.4 F (38 C)"; chills or rigors "as the body generates heat to reach the elevated hypothalamic set point"; endogenous pyrogens are "proinflammatory cytokines released by immune cells")

F18. A fever helps the body fight infection: the higher temperature slows the multiplying of many germs, and it speeds up the defences, such as neutrophils moving to the infected place.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fever ("Fever inhibits pathogen proliferation through thermal disruption of microbial processes"; "modest temperature elevations confer a clear survival advantage by impairing pathogen replication"; "Fever plays an adaptive role in enhancing immune defenses", including "accelerated neutrophil migration to sites of infection") ; https://www.livescience.com/health/viruses-infections-disease/how-do-fevers-kill-germs (higher temperatures stress germs and slow their growth, and immune cells work better)

F19. Fever is different from overheating (hyperthermia). In a fever the brain raises its set temperature on purpose; in hyperthermia, as in heatstroke, the body's temperature control has failed.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hyperthermia ("rather than an intentional elevation of the temperature set point as seen in fever"; "hyperthermia results from failed thermoregulation rather than a hypothalamic set-point elevation")

## The lymph nodes

F20. Lymph nodes are small, bean-shaped organs of the lymphatic system. They filter the fluid called lymph, trap germs, and hold many white blood cells (lymphocytes, the B cells and T cells). Groups of them lie under the skin in the neck, the armpits and the groin, and deeper in the chest and belly.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lymph_node ("a small, bean-shaped structure that is a key component of the lymphatic system"; "functioning primarily to filter lymph fluid from tissues, trap pathogens and abnormal cells"; "housing lymphocytes and other immune cells"; "concentrated in areas such as the neck, armpits, groin, chest, and abdomen") ; https://aboutkidshealth.ca/swollen-lymph-nodes ("They act like tiny filters, catching viruses and bacteria for white blood cells to destroy"; "Most lymph nodes are found in groups under the skin in the armpit, groin and neck")

F21. Lymph nodes near an infection swell, and people call them swollen glands: tender lumps on the sides of the neck, under the chin, in the armpits or the groin. They swell because white blood cells in them multiply (and more arrive) to fight the germs. A cold or strep throat often makes the nodes in the neck swell; they go down within 1 to 2 weeks.
    https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/swollen-glands/ ("Glands (known as lymph glands or lymph nodes) swell near an infection to help your body fight it"; "Swollen glands are usually a sign the body is fighting an infection"; "on each side of the neck", "under the chin", "in the armpits", "around the groin"; "should go down within 1 to 2 weeks") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lymphadenopathy (reactive enlargement from "polyclonal proliferation of lymphocytes and macrophages in response to antigenic stimuli" and "influx of additional immune cells into the node"; triggers include "viruses like the common cold ... or bacteria such as those causing strep throat") ; https://aboutkidshealth.ca/swollen-lymph-nodes (with a cold or strep throat "they can grow to about two centimetres in the neck area")

## The body's memory of a germ it has beaten

F22. After the body has beaten a germ, it remembers it: memory B cells and memory T cells stay behind, and if the same germ comes again they mount a faster, stronger defence, in 1 to 3 days instead of the first time's week or more.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Immunological_memory ("the capacity of the immune system to recognize and mount a faster, stronger, and more effective response"; memory B cells and memory T cells are "long-lived") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Adaptive_immune_system (the primary response "requires 4-7 days" / "peaks after 7-10 days"; on re-exposure to the same antigen, "a faster onset (within 1-3 days) and greater magnitude") ; https://kidshealth.org/en/kids/immune.html (lymphocytes "help the body remember the invaders and destroy them")

F23. B cells make antibodies, proteins that lock onto one particular germ; antibodies usually stay in the body in case the same germ comes back.
    https://kidshealth.org/en/kids/immune.html ("B lymphocytes are triggered to make antibodies"; "These proteins lock onto specific antigens"; "antibodies usually stay in our bodies in case we have to fight the same germ again") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/White_blood_cell ("B cells mediate humoral immunity by differentiating into plasma cells that produce antibodies")

F24. Chickenpox is caused by a virus (varicella-zoster). Having chickenpox once usually protects a person for life, and second cases are rare and milder. Someone who has had a disease such as chickenpox usually will not get sick from it again.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chickenpox ("Natural infection with varicella-zoster virus confers lifelong immunity in most cases"; "over 90% protection against reinfection, although rare mild breakthrough cases can occur") ; https://kidshealth.org/en/kids/immune.html ("That's why someone who gets sick with a disease, like chickenpox, usually won't get sick from it again")

F25. Adults who have never had chickenpox can still catch it, and it is often worse in adults. It spreads easily: up to about 90 percent of household contacts who have never had it catch it. (After recovery the virus stays hidden in nerve cells and can come back late in life as shingles, so no question says the virus is gone.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chickenpox ("Adults, pregnant women, infants, and immunocompromised individuals often experience more severe illness"; "a higher proportion of infections occur in adults due to delayed primary exposure"; "Among susceptible household contacts, the secondary attack rate can reach up to 90%"; "the virus establishes a lifelong latent infection in sensory neurons")

F26. More than 200 different viruses cause colds (rhinoviruses alone come in over 100 types). Beating one cold protects only against that one virus, so people catch cold after cold.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Common_cold ("caused by more than 200 different respiratory viruses"; rhinoviruses "exist in over 100 serotypes"; "Immunity following a common cold episode provides only short-term protection against the specific viral strain encountered"; "Recurrence is common due to the diversity of circulating viruses")

F27. The body's memory works against viruses as well as bacteria: measles and chickenpox, both viruses, usually leave lifelong protection.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Immunological_memory ("lifelong immunity in cases such as measles") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chickenpox (F24)

F28. Cold weather does not itself cause colds; colds are more common in winter because people crowd together indoors.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Common_cold ("low temperatures do not directly cause the common cold"; winter peaks come from "increased indoor crowding during colder months") ; https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/catching-cold ("the cold weather itself does not cause the common cold"; "one does not catch a cold merely by being wet and cold")

## Washing hands

F29. Washing hands with soap and water removes germs. Soap and the rubbing of scrubbing lift dirt, grease and germs off the skin so the water can rinse them away, and soap works better than water alone. Health experts say to scrub for at least 20 seconds.
    https://www.cdc.gov/handwashing/show-me-the-science-handwashing.html ("Using soap to wash hands is more effective than using water alone" because "the surfactants in soap lift soil and microbes from skin"; "Soap and friction help lift dirt, grease, and microbes ... from skin so they can then be rinsed off") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hand_washing (soap "emulsifies lipids and oils that trap pathogens, and suspends microbes for easier rinsing"; "bacterial counts on hands drop by 77-92% with soap and friction compared to 56% with water alone"; "Continue scrubbing for at least 20 seconds") ; https://www.cdc.gov/clean-hands/data-research/facts-stats/index.html ("Handwashing with soap removes germs from hands")

F30. The temperature of the washing water does not seem to change how many germs come off; warm and cold water work about the same.
    https://www.cdc.gov/handwashing/show-me-the-science-handwashing.html ("The temperature of the water does not appear to affect germ removal")

F31. Teaching people to wash their hands cuts the number who get sick with diarrhoea by 23 to 40 percent and colds and other breathing illnesses by 16 to 21 percent. Germs from unwashed hands get into food and drink.
    https://www.cdc.gov/clean-hands/data-research/facts-stats/index.html ("Reduces the number of people who get sick with diarrhea by 23-40%"; "Reduces respiratory illnesses, like colds, in the general population by 16-21%"; "Germs from unwashed hands can get into foods and drinks")

## Facts behind the wrong choices

F32. Many disease-causing bacteria grow best near the body's own core temperature of 37 degrees Celsius, so the ordinary warmth inside the body does not kill them.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mesophile (mesophiles grow "optimally at moderate temperatures, typically between 20 C and 45 C", with "human pathogens often exhibiting a preferred temperature near 37 C, the core body temperature")

F33. The food pipe (oesophagus) carries food and drink from the throat to the stomach; its glands make mucus for lubrication and to shield its lining, not to kill germs.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Esophagus ("serves as the conduit for food and liquids from the pharynx to the stomach"; "mucous glands ... that secrete acidic mucin for lubrication"; "additional mucus to shield the squamous mucosa from gastric acid")

F34. The ear canal runs from the outer ear to the eardrum and is lined with skin (its epidermis is stratified squamous epithelium, like the skin's); earwax in it holds back germs.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ear_canal ("The epidermis of the external auditory canal consists of stratified squamous epithelium"; the canal "connects the auricle (pinna) to the tympanic membrane (eardrum)"; cerumen has "antimicrobial and lubricating properties")

F35. After scrubbing, the soap is rinsed away with the loosened dirt and germs, so it leaves no protective coat behind (author's inference from the rinse); soaps with added germ-killing ingredients bring no extra health benefit to ordinary users.
    https://www.cdc.gov/handwashing/show-me-the-science-handwashing.html ("Rinsing the soap away also minimizes skin irritation"; "there is no added health benefit for consumers ... using soaps containing antibacterial ingredients compared with using plain soap")

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Children use the word "germs" for all microbes and often hold a weak idea of germs as living things that act; they also blame colds on cold weather or being chilled and wet (F28).
    https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/catching-cold ("Children at this age use the word germs for all microbes"; "the word cold reinforces the connection with environmental causes") ; Badani and Schonfeld (2002), Health Education 102(6): 300-309, https://doi.org/10.1108/09654280210446847 (misconceptions about the cold "identified across all grade levels" and "did not decrease as children acquired more factual information")

M2. The fever is often taken for part of the harm the germ does, the germ's own heat or its damage. In fact it is the body's response: "A fever is a physiological response to the virus, not a cause" (NSTA), set by the brain (F17, F19).
    https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/catching-cold

M3. "Sweating out" a fever or a cold is a folk belief with no evidence behind it; people sweat when the fever breaks because the brain's set point drops back and the body cools itself.
    https://www.healthline.com/health/sweating-out-a-fever ("there's no evidence that sweating it out will help you feel better faster"; "making yourself sweat more doesn't treat the fever or its cause"; as the set point drops, "your sweat glands kick in and start producing more sweat to cool you off")

M4. Hot water is widely believed to be what gets germs off the hands; it is not (F30). Germs on the hands are on the surface; soap lifts them off, it does not draw them out of the skin (F29).

M5. The belief that a disease like chickenpox is only for children, so a grown person cannot catch it, is wrong (F25). (Author's note, from F25's sources.)

M6. Students mix up the jobs of the parts of the blood: white blood cells (fight germs), platelets (clots) and red blood cells (oxygen). (Author's judgment from the standard 6th-grade blood lesson; F11, F13, F14.)

F36. How sure the fever facts are: Evans, Repasky and Fisher (2015, Nature Reviews Immunology) say fever "confers a survival benefit during infection", that fever-range temperatures "cause a greater than 200-fold reduction in the replication rate of poliovirus in mammalian cells", and that "Thermal stress further increases neutrophil recruitment to local sites of infection", but that the evidence is "largely based on experimental models employing LPS or fever-range hyperthermia as surrogates for pathogen-induced fever". Science News Explores, on a mouse study in which fever helped immune cells reach germs faster: "But researchers still need to prove it." So the bank says a fever is THOUGHT to slow some germs and help white blood cells work faster. (No source says a fever sweats germs out (M3), dries up mucus, or keeps more germs from getting in.)
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786079 ; https://www.snexplores.org/article/fever-can-help-immune-cells-attack ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fever

F37. How long swollen glands last: lymph nodes swollen by a viral infection "will shrink to normal size on their own in about two to four weeks" (AboutKidsHealth, SickKids); the NHS says they "should go down within 1 to 2 weeks". So: within a few weeks.
    https://aboutkidshealth.ca/swollen-lymph-nodes ; https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/swollen-glands/
