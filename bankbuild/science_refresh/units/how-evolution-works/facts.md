## Darwin and Wallace

F1. Charles Darwin conceived natural selection in September 1838 (Grokipedia: on 28 September 1838, reading Malthus, in Notebook D) and wrote that "favourable variations would tend to be preserved, and unfavourable ones to be destroyed." He wrote a pencil sketch in 1842 and expanded it into an Essay in 1844, but did not publish; in 1856 he wrote that he had "been at work for 18 years, & yet could not publish for several years." From 1838 to 1858 is twenty years.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Publication_of_Darwin's_theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publication_of_Darwin%27s_theory

F2. Alfred Russel Wallace, collecting in the Malay Archipelago (1854-1862), worked out the same mechanism in February 1858 while ill with fever; lying in bed "he thought about Malthus's idea of positive checks on human population" (Wikipedia). His autobiography puts him on Ternate, though his journal suggests he was on Gilolo (Halmahera). He posted the essay, "On the Tendency of Varieties to Depart Indefinitely from the Original Type", from Ternate around March 1858 (sources give 2 March and 9 March), and Darwin received it at Down House on 18 June 1858. Grokipedia: natural selection was "independently formulated" by Darwin and by Wallace, "inspired by Malthusian population pressures."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alfred_Russel_Wallace ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Publication_of_Darwin's_theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Russel_Wallace ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_selection

F3. Charles Lyell and Joseph Hooker arranged a joint presentation without consulting Wallace. On 1 July 1858 the Linnean Society of London heard Wallace's essay, an extract from Darwin's unpublished 1844 Essay and Darwin's 1857 letter to Asa Gray, read by the Secretary. Neither Darwin nor Wallace attended (Darwin was at his infant son's funeral; Wallace was in the Malay Archipelago), and there was no discussion. Wallace later accepted the arrangement and "never expressed bitterness in public or in private" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Publication_of_Darwin's_theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publication_of_Darwin%27s_theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Russel_Wallace

F4. Thomas Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population (1798) argued that "population tends to increase geometrically while food production grows only arithmetically," so growth must be held back by checks such as famine, disease and war (Grokipedia's summary).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thomas_Robert_Malthus

F5. Rival books a student may confuse with Malthus (for honest wrong choices): Charles Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830-1833) argued that Earth's features "result from gradual, observable natural processes operating over immense periods of time"; it was Darwin's "geological 'bible'" on the Beagle. William Paley's Natural Theology (1802) argued from design: "every manifestation of design, which existed in the watch, exists in the works of nature." Jean-Baptiste Lamarck's Philosophie Zoologique (1809) held that use strengthens an organ and the gain is conserved in the descendants (F22).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Charles_Lyell ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_Theology_or_Evidences_of_the_Existence_and_Attributes_of_the_Deity ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lamarckism

## What natural selection needs

F6. The conditions: heritable variation ("the raw material upon which natural selection acts"; "For variation to enable evolutionary change, it must exhibit heritability"); populations "produce more offspring than can survive to reproductive age, leading to competition for limited resources"; and differential reproduction, when individuals with certain heritable traits "produce more surviving offspring." Fitness is an organism's relative success in passing its genes to later generations.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_selection

F7. Wilhelm Johannsen's pure lines (published 1903): he raised lines of the common bean, a self-fertilising plant, each line from a single plant; within a line, seed weight still varied. He sowed the heaviest and the lightest seeds of a line separately for up to four generations in some lines; within pure lines selection produced "no lasting heritable shifts," though selection in the mixed starting population did change the mean (Grokipedia). Within a line, "populations homozygous for all traits, i.e. without genetic variation," still varied in seed size, and the variation "was attributable to resource provision to the mother plant" and to where seeds and pods sat on the plant (Wikipedia). Lesson: only inherited variation can respond to selection. Johannsen coined "phenotype", "genotype" and "gene" (Elemente der exakten Erblichkeitslehre, 1909).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wilhelm_Johannsen ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Johannsen

## Populations evolve, individuals do not

F8. "Evolution is the change in a population's genetic composition over time, specifically over generations" (Lumen Learning); population genetics measures evolution as "shifts in allele frequencies within populations," while natural selection "primarily operates at the level of the individual organism" (Grokipedia). "Populations, not individual organisms, evolve" (UC Museum of Paleontology, Understanding Evolution). A change in a population's size, or in its members' bodies with no change in which genes pass on, is not evolution.
    https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-biology2/chapter/misconceptions-of-evolution/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_selection ; https://evolution.berkeley.edu:443/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/misconceptions_about_evolution_2020.pdf

## Speciation and gene flow

F9. The biological species concept (formalised by Ernst Mayr, 1942): a species is "one or more populations of organisms that actually or potentially interbreed in nature to produce fertile offspring," reproductively isolated from other such groups. Horses and donkeys are separate species because their hybrids, mules, are sterile. Species are not defined by looks: populations within one species can differ greatly in form, and look-alike populations can be separate species (the meadowlarks split by song and genome in 2022).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Species

F10. Allopatric speciation: "physical separation that splits a continuous population into distinct groups, leading to divergence over time," until reproductive isolation develops. Gene flow works against divergence: "ongoing migration homogenizes allele frequencies and erodes divergence," and "Speciation fundamentally requires curtailing gene flow."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Speciation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allopatric_speciation

F11. Gene flow "is the movement of genetic material between populations of the same species," by moving organisms or by travelling reproductive cells such as pollen. It "homogenizes genetic variation across populations"; high rates "unify allele frequencies across groups, preventing the accumulation of distinct genetic profiles that could lead to species formation." It can also bring in alleles a population lacked ("introducing novel alleles"). Barriers such as mountains, rivers and oceans restrict it, and "limited gene flow promotes genetic divergence between isolated populations, potentially leading to speciation." Only mutation makes new alleles (F12, and the T4 unit genes-chromosomes-and-mutations).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gene_flow

## Genetic drift: bottlenecks and founders

F12. Genetic drift is "random fluctuations in allele frequencies within a population caused by sampling error during reproduction," acting without regard to an allele's effect; "in small populations, drift can cause rapid and significant shifts," and it "can lead to the dominance of neutral or even slightly deleterious alleles purely by chance." A bottleneck is a drastic fall in numbers; a founder effect is a new population started by a few individuals, who "carry only a subset of the genetic variation" of the source. Sewall Wright first used the term "genetic drift" (Grokipedia gives his 1931 paper). Mutation is the source of new alleles: "Mutations serve as the ultimate source of genetic novelty in evolution by introducing new alleles into populations," and they are random in timing and place, not directed by need (Grokipedia, Mutation).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_drift ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Population_bottleneck ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Founder_effect ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mutation

F13. Northern elephant seals were hunted for the oil in their blubber in the 1800s. The low point is given variously: "as few as 20 individuals by around 1890" and "10-30" (Grokipedia), "20-40" (Wikipedia), "~20" in 1892 (Hoelzel and colleagues, 2024). A remnant survived on Guadalupe Island off Mexico; protected by Mexico (1922) and later the United States, they now number over 200,000 (Wikipedia; Grokipedia "over 200,000"; Lowry and colleagues estimated about 225,000 in 2010).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Northern_elephant_seal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Population_bottleneck ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_elephant_seal ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11009102/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5892393

F14. The seals' genetic variety is very low: at one stretch of mitochondrial DNA (the control region), northern seals show only 2 types (haplotypes), while 48 southern elephant seals showed 26 (PMC5892393, citing Hoelzel and colleagues 1993); their nuclear DNA too shows "reduced diversity compared with southern elephant seals" (PMC11009102). Pre-bottleneck museum specimens and archaeological remains "directly demonstrate the reduction in genetic variation" (PMC5892393); Weber and colleagues (Current Biology, 2000) found that the species "historically possessed additional genotypes to those present now"; Hoelzel and colleagues (2024): "The loss of diversity was also evident in comparisons of pre- and post-bottleneck NES DNA," with pre-bottleneck heterozygosity about 0.00142 against about 0.000176 today. The comparison with the southern species is suggestive only: two species can differ for other reasons.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5892393 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11009102/ ; https://escholarship.org/uc/item/7rk6h9g6

F15. Pingelap, a Pacific atoll: a typhoon in 1775 left "only about 20 survivors" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia "as few as 20 immediate survivors"). The ruler of the time, Doahkaesa Mwanenihsed, "is now believed to have been a carrier" of the recessive gene for achromatopsia, total colour blindness. Today the condition "is now prevalent in almost 10% of the population," with "a further 30% being unaffected carriers" (Wikipedia); Grokipedia gives 4-10 percent, "about one in ten residents" in one place. Elsewhere it is rare: "1 in 33,000" in the United States (Wikipedia), "1 in 30,000 to 50,000 births" worldwide (Grokipedia). Wikipedia credits "the founder effect and inbreeding"; Grokipedia calls it "a severe genetic bottleneck." A fisherman with the condition "has difficulty seeing in bright sunlight." It is inherited, not caused by sunlight.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pingelap ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pingelap ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_drift

## Molecular evidence

F16. The chimpanzee draft genome was published on 1 September 2005 in Nature. Chimpanzee and human DNA share about 98.8 percent identity where it lines up (1.23 percent single-letter divergence; Grokipedia also gives about 98.7 percent). Counting insertions and deletions the figure is about 96 percent (NHGRI: directly comparable DNA "almost 99 percent identical", "96 percent" with insertions and deletions; Grokipedia says indels add roughly 1.5 percent of alignable sequence, so the second figure varies by method). The differences between humans and chimpanzees are "approximately 60 times less than that seen between human and mouse" (NHGRI).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chimpanzee_genome_project ; https://www.genome.gov/15515096/2005-release-new-genome-comparison-finds-chimps-humans-very-similar-at-dna-level

F17. Humans and chimpanzees "diverged from a common ancestor about 6 million years ago" (NHGRI); Grokipedia: about 6-7 million years ago. The mouse lineage diverged from the human "around 75 million years ago" (NHGRI, an estimate). MISCONCEPTION: that humans evolved from chimpanzees; living species such as humans and apes "share a common ancestor; one did not evolve directly from the other" (Pearson study guide). Chimpanzees have 24 pairs of chromosomes, humans 23 (a fusion in the human line).

## Richard Lenski's long-term evolution experiment

F18. Richard Lenski began the experiment on 24 February 1988 at the University of California, Irvine, and moved it to Michigan State University in 1990 (Grokipedia). Twelve "initially identical, asexual populations" of Escherichia coli come from one ancestor; half carry a neutral marker (Ara+) that lets competitors be told apart. "Each day, 0.1 mL of culture is transferred (1:100 dilution) into 9.9 mL of fresh defined medium (DM25)," about 6.6 generations a day. The populations were frozen early in 2020 because of the COVID-19 pandemic, at over 73,000 generations, and later resumed (Wikipedia); they passed 80,000 generations in August 2024 (Wikipedia) and exceeded 82,000 by November 2025 (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment

F19. The frozen fossil record: samples are frozen "at 500-generation (75-day) intervals"; "The bacteria in these samples remain viable, and can be revived at any time"; the record "permits the isolation and comparison of living exemplars of ancestral and evolved clones" and lets populations be restarted after contamination (Wikipedia). Fitness is measured by "competing bacteria from a later-generation sample against the ancestral strain"; "Relative fitness is expressed as the ratio of the realized growth rates of the evolved and ancestral bacteria" (Lenski and colleagues, PMC5607360); the marker is "neutral (that is, does not affect fitness)." By 50,000 generations "the grand mean relative fitness had risen by approximately 70%" (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5607360 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment

F20. Parallel change: "All twelve of the experimental populations show an increase in cell size," and all twelve showed "a similar pattern of rapid improvement in fitness that decelerated over time" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: "all 12 replicate populations exhibited a consistent increase in cell size"). Six of the twelve evolved defects in DNA repair (hypermutators), so the count of mutations is no measure of fitness.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment

F21. Citrate: the inability to grow aerobically on citrate "is considered a defining characteristic of E. coli as a species"; the DM25 broth "contains a large amount of citrate (about 11 times the concentration of glucose)," present "from the experiment's start as a chelating agent," and "it remained unused for over 30,000 generations." Blount, Borland and Lenski (PNAS, 2008): "A citrate-using (Cit+) variant finally evolved in one population by 31,500 generations"; the first emerged "between 31,000 and 31,500 generations"; the population was Ara-3, and none of the other eleven had Cit+ variants.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2430337/

F22. The replays (Zachary Blount, Christina Borland and Richard Lenski, 2008): "We observed no Cit+ mutants among 8.4 x 10^12 ancestral cells, nor among 9 x 10^12 cells from 60 clones sampled in the first 15,000 generations." In the replay experiments every Cit+ mutant arose from a clone sampled at generation 20,000 or later ("no Cit+ mutants" came from "any of the other 600 cultures that used clones isolated before generation 20,000"). They "reject the hypothesis that a rare mutation could have produced a Cit+ variant with equal probability" at any time, conclude that "some potentiating mutation arose by 20,000 generations," and that "the evolution of this phenotype was contingent on the particular history of that population." Wikipedia: Cit+ re-evolved "only when starting from clones isolated from after generation 20,000"; "historical contingency can have a profound and lasting impact."
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2430337/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment

F23. The final step was a mutation: "The Cit+ trait was actualized by a duplication mutation that created a new regulatory module": a 2,933-base-pair stretch containing the citT gene (a citrate transporter) was duplicated, putting a copy under the control of the rnk promoter, which is active when oxygen is present.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/E._coli_long-term_evolution_experiment

## Lamarck, the respectable rival, and Weismann's mice

F24. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck set out his theory in Philosophie Zoologique (1809): first law, "the more frequent and sustained use of any organ will strengthen this organ little by little"; second law, the change "is conserved through generation in the new individuals descending from them." The giraffe's neck is the usual illustration (stretching to browse higher leaves), though Grokipedia notes Lamarck did not spell it out in detail. Darwin too "viewed the inheritance of acquired traits not as the primary driver of evolution" but as real and secondary.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lamarckism

F25. Darwin's pangenesis (1868): "every unit or cell of an organism's body throws off minute granules called gemmules," which gather in the reproductive organs and shape the offspring, "enabling the inheritance of acquired characteristics." Francis Galton's blood transfusions between rabbit varieties gave 87 of 88 offspring of the pure original colour, and he called pangenesis "incorrect."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pangenesis

F26. August Weismann cut the tails off 68 white mice and their descendants across five generations (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: begun 1888, published 1889): "901 young were produced by five generations of artificially mutilated parents, and yet there was not a single example of a rudimentary tail or of any other abnormality in this organ." He ran it against claims that mutilations were inherited (a tailless cat with tailless kittens). Grokipedia: Weismann took the result to show that "somatic mutilations do not affect the germ plasm," which "undercut the gemmule mechanism."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Weismann ; https://grokipedia.com/page/August_Weismann ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pangenesis

F27. The germ plasm theory: inheritance runs through "the germ cells," such as egg and sperm, "a specialized substance within germ cells that remains separate from the somatic cells"; "Genetic information cannot pass from soma to germ plasm and on to the next generation" (the Weismann barrier), which "rules out the inheritance of acquired characteristics" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia). Honest limits: a mutilation is an injury, not a change made by use or effort, so the mice "has been criticized for not adequately testing adaptive acquired traits" (Grokipedia); Lamarck's claim concerned characteristics "acquired through effort, or will" (Wikipedia). What retired Lamarckism was the germ-plasm idea plus Mendelian genetics, which "entrenched particulate inheritance, directly undermining Lamarckism's core tenet" (Grokipedia). Modern caveat: some epigenetic marks pass on, but reprogramming "typically erases acquired marks, limiting stable transmission beyond one or two generations," and "Epigenetic effects do not substantiate classical Lamarckism" (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Weismann ; https://grokipedia.com/page/August_Weismann ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lamarckism

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- An individual animal evolves in its lifetime (F8: populations evolve; a body's response to cold or food is not evolution).
- Any change in a population is evolution, including growth in numbers or fatter animals after a good year (F8: only a change in which genes pass on).
- Picking the biggest always breeds bigger offspring (F7: only inherited differences respond).
- Inheritance blends parents' traits like paint (F7, F25: an old rival idea).
- Species are told apart by looks or diet (F9: by whether they can interbreed and have fertile young).
- Migration speeds the splitting of species, or creates new genes (F10, F11: it keeps populations alike; only mutation makes new alleles).
- A rare gene becomes common only if it helps (F12, F15: drift can spread even a harmful one).
- Humans descend from chimpanzees, or chimpanzees are turning into humans (F17).
- Bacteria "learn" to eat a new food by long exposure (F22, F23: a mutation, built on earlier ones).
- A body's gains or injuries are handed on (F24-F27).
