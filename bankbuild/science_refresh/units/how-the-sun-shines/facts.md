## The core

F1. The Sun's core reaches about 15 million degrees Celsius (Grokipedia: about 15.7 million K at the centre; Wikipedia: 15,000,000 K; NASA: "about 27 million degrees Fahrenheit (15 million degrees Celsius)"). Its central density is about 150 g/cm3, and it spans roughly the inner 20-25 percent of the Sun's radius.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_core ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_core ; https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/

F2. In the core, hydrogen nuclei (protons) fuse into helium, about 99 percent of it by the proton-proton chain and about 1 percent by the CNO cycle. Each helium nucleus made releases 26.7 MeV, "converting about 0.7% of the initial hydrogen mass into energy." The Sun fuses roughly 600-620 million tonnes of hydrogen every second, and about 4 million tonnes of mass leave it as energy each second.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Proton–proton_chain ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_core ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_core

F3. Fusion in the core is slow: at the very centre it gives about 276.5 watts per cubic metre, a power density "similar to an active compost heap" and lower than that of an adult human's metabolism (Wikipedia). The first step, two protons joining, has a characteristic time of about a billion years per proton (Wikipedia; Grokipedia gives both 10^9 and about 9 billion years, so no figure is used in the questions). The Sun is bright because its core is vast. (The old bank said "reptile metabolism"; Wikipedia now compares it with a compost heap and a human body.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_core ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Proton–proton_chain

F4. The Sun is about 74 percent hydrogen and 24 percent helium by mass; oxygen is third, at about 0.6 percent, then carbon and neon.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun

## How we know it is not burning, or shrinking (1850s-1900s)

F5. Lord Kelvin (William Thomson) argued in 1862 ("On the Age of the Sun's Heat", Macmillan's Magazine) that chemical burning could not power the Sun: the most energetic chemical action "would only generate about 3,000 years' heat" (quoted by Bahcall). OpenStax: a Sun of coal or wood "could not produce energy at its present rate for more than few thousand years."
    https://arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/0009259v3 (Bahcall, How the Sun Shines) ; https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-1-sources-of-sunshine-thermal-and-gravitational-energy

F6. Earlier, J. R. Mayer proposed that meteors or asteroids falling into the Sun make its heat (OpenStax: it would take an Earth's mass of meteorites every 100 years). Hermann von Helmholtz proposed in 1854 (Grokipedia gives 1856) that the Sun shines by slowly contracting under its own gravity, and Kelvin developed the idea; the Helmholtz-Thomson contraction theory was the standard account for more than forty years, though it gave the Sun only about 20-30 million years (Kragh: about 20 million; Bahcall: about 30 million; OpenStax: roughly 100 million at most). A shrinkage of about 40 metres a year would supply the Sun's present output (OpenStax).
    https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02834 (Kragh, The Source of Solar Energy, ca. 1840-1910) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kelvin–Helmholtz_mechanism ; https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-1-sources-of-sunshine-thermal-and-gravitational-energy

F7. The short solar timescale clashed with geology: Charles Darwin estimated about 300 million years for the "denudation of the Weald" (Bahcall), and called the age problem "one of my sorest troubles" (Grokipedia). Geologists and physicists later showed that Earth, and so the Sun, is far older (OpenStax), and contraction "cannot be the primary source of solar energy."
    https://arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/0009259v3 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kelvin–Helmholtz_mechanism ; https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-1-sources-of-sunshine-thermal-and-gravitational-energy

F8. After radium was found to give off heat steadily (1903), radioactivity was briefly considered as an alternative to contraction (Kragh); W. E. Wilson suggested in Nature, July 1903, that radium could account for the Sun's energy. It was wrong in its particulars.
    https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02834 ; https://skullsinthestars.com/2010/12/21/the-first-glimmer-of-a-nuclear-sun-radium-and-solar-energy-1903/

F9. Heat and light from the Sun cross empty space as radiation, which "requires no material medium for propagation"; sunlight takes about 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach Earth, about 150 million km away.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_radiation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun ; https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/istp/outreach/workshop/michels/tsld017.htm

## Eddington's guess (1920) and the answer to his critics

F10. Francis Aston built the first mass spectrograph at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge in 1919 (Grokipedia). Arthur Eddington gave the address "The Internal Constitution of the Stars" to the British Association at Cardiff on 24 August 1920. He cited Francis Aston's measurement that "the mass of the helium atom is less than the sum of the masses of the four hydrogen atoms", "a loss of mass in the synthesis amounting to about 1 part in 120" (Wikipedia: about 0.8 percent), and proposed that stars shine by building helium from hydrogen. Aston presented his result at the same meeting the next day. Eddington wrote that "Lord Kelvin's date of the creation of the sun is treated with no more respect than Archbishop Ussher's." He rejected gravitational contraction because it could not supply stars' output over their lifetimes (Grokipedia).
    https://bfschutz.com/2020/08/24/eddington-in-cardiff-100-years-ago-today-the-first-proposal-that-stars-are-powered-by-fusion/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Eddington ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Arthur_Eddington ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Francis_William_Aston

F11. Nuclei all carry positive charge and repel. In the Sun's core the electric (Coulomb) barrier between two protons is about 0.5 MeV, "far exceeding the typical thermal energy of about 1 keV in the solar core" (Grokipedia), so a typical proton has hundreds of times too little energy to get close enough to fuse. Critics urged that stars were not hot enough; Eddington answered in his 1926 book: "We do not argue with the critic who urges that the stars are not hot enough for this process; we tell him to go and find a hotter place."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Coulomb_barrier ; https://todayinsci.com/E/Eddington_Arthur/EddingtonArthur-Star-Quotations.htm ; https://physicsworld.com/a/explorer-of-stars-and-souls-arthur-stanley-eddington/

F12. Quantum tunnelling: a particle can end up on the far side of a barrier even when it lacks the energy to climb over it, because of its wave nature (Grokipedia). George Gamow used it in 1928 to explain alpha decay; in 1929 Robert Atkinson and Fritz Houtermans applied Gamow's tunnelling theory to fusion in stars (Wikipedia), assuming a core at 40 million degrees (Kragh). The proton-proton chain is "heavily reliant on tunnelling through the ~500 keV barrier at core temperatures of ~15 million K" (Grokipedia). Heat strips atoms of their electrons, but a nucleus keeps its charge (its protons).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Quantum_tunnelling ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Coulomb_barrier ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_d%27Escourt_Atkinson ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00072

F13. Hans Bethe went to the 1938 Washington Conference on Theoretical Physics on stellar energy; with Charles Critchfield he worked out the proton-proton reactions, and in 1939 he published "Energy Production in Stars" with the CNO cycle. His central-temperature estimate was within 20 percent of today's value (Bahcall). He received the 1967 Nobel Prize in Physics "for his contributions to the theory of nuclear reactions, especially his discoveries concerning the energy production in stars."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Bethe ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hans_Bethe ; https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1967/bethe/facts/ ; https://arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/0009259v3

## The slow trip out

F14. Energy made in the core is carried outward through the radiative zone by light that is absorbed and re-emitted in random directions (a random walk), and then through the outer convection zone (about 0.7 to 1.0 of the Sun's radius) by rising and sinking gas. NASA: "about 170,000 years to get from the core to the top of the convection zone"; Grokipedia: "a typical photon takes an estimated 170,000 years to cross the zone"; NASA Goddard: 170,000 years through the radiative zone, about 10 days through the convection zone, then 8 minutes to Earth. HONEST RANGE: published estimates run from about 10,000 years (Shu) through about 44,000 (a crude estimate) to 170,000 and, with smaller step lengths, more than half a million years; Koberlein: most estimates fall between about 20,000 and 150,000 years. The figure is a statistical estimate, not one photon's trip.
    https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun ; https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/istp/outreach/workshop/michels/tsld017.htm ; https://www.physics.unlv.edu/~jeffery/astro/star/photon_escape.html ; https://briankoberlein.com/post/thats-so-random/ ; https://www.iflscience.com/the-sunlight-that-is-reaching-us-now-is-thousands-of-years-old-if-not-a-lot-older-but-took-under-9-minutes-to-travel-to-earth-83564

F15. Neutrinos made in the core cross the whole Sun in about 2 seconds (Grokipedia), and about 2 percent of fusion's energy leaves the Sun this way.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_core ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Proton–proton_chain

## The solar neutrinos

F16. Ray Davis's Homestake experiment: a 378,000-litre (100,000-gallon) tank holding 615 tonnes of perchloroethylene, a cleaning fluid rich in chlorine, 1,478 metres down the Homestake Gold Mine in Lead, South Dakota, deep "to shield against cosmic rays". Solar electron neutrinos turned chlorine atoms into argon-37, which was counted. Its main run was 1970-1994 (the experiment 1967-1994). The result was "approximately one-third of the predicted flux" from John Bahcall's standard solar models. Davis was awarded half of the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physics.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Homestake_experiment ; https://arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/0009259v3

F17. The three possibilities: "The experiment was wrong", "The standard solar model was wrong", "The standard picture of neutrinos was wrong"; "At the time, it seemed that explanations 1 or 2 were most likely" (University of Oregon). A key astrophysical proposal was a cooler core (Grokipedia); particle-physics proposals included neutrinos decaying on the way to Earth (Grokipedia Homestake). Helioseismology later backed the standard solar model to about 0.1 percent.
    https://pages.uoregon.edu/soper/Sun/homestake.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_neutrino_problem ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Homestake_experiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_neutrino_problem

F18. The Sudbury Neutrino Observatory, 2,100 metres down the Creighton nickel mine near Sudbury, Ontario, used 1,000 tonnes of heavy water, which could count neutrinos of all kinds together as well as electron neutrinos alone. In 2001 it found the total matched the solar models while electron neutrinos were only about 34-35 percent of it: solar electron neutrinos change into the other kinds (muon and tau) on the way to Earth, which requires neutrinos to have mass. Davis's chlorine caught only electron neutrinos. Arthur McDonald shared the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physics with Takaaki Kajita.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sudbury_Neutrino_Observatory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_neutrino_problem ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_neutrino_problem

## The Sun's age

F19. The oldest solids in the solar system, calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions in meteorites, date to 4,567.3 million years ago by lead isotopes; this "is commonly used to define the age of the Solar System" (Wikipedia). The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old, "mainly from radiometric dating of meteorites" (Grokipedia); Bahcall: "the sun is 4.6 billion years old" from meteorites; NASA rounds it to 4.5 billion. Helioseismology agrees.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium–aluminium-rich_inclusion ; https://arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/0009259v3 ; https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/

F20. Earth's oldest rocks (the Acasta Gneiss, Canada) are about 4.03 billion years old and its oldest mineral grains (Jack Hills zircons) about 4.4 billion, younger than Earth (4.54 billion), because plate tectonics and mantle convection destroyed most early crust; Earth's age itself comes from meteorites.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Age_of_Earth

F21. Halfway: the Sun's steady (main-sequence) life is expected to last about 10 billion years in all (Grokipedia); NASA: "a little less than halfway through its lifetime" with "another 5 billion years or so" to go. Fusion has depleted the central hydrogen by about half (Grokipedia: from about 70 percent to about 34-35 percent by mass at the centre).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_core ; https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_core

## Sunspots

F22. Sunspots are cooler regions of the surface, made by concentrations of intense magnetic fields that "inhibit the convective transport of heat from the solar interior" (Grokipedia); umbra fields of 2,000-4,000 gauss against about 1 gauss for the quiet Sun. Umbra temperatures: 3,700-4,200 K (Grokipedia Sun), 3,000-4,500 K (Wikipedia), against a photosphere of about 5,772 K (about 5,500 degrees Celsius). They look dark only by contrast: "a single sunspot would shine brighter than the full moon, with a crimson-orange color" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sunspot ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunspot ; https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/

F23. Christoph Scheiner first took the spots for "small dark clouds or satellites orbiting the Sun" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: "clusters of small moons". Galileo, in his Letters on Sunspots (1613), argued that they lie on or just above the Sun's surface and are carried round by its rotation, because near the edge they appear "as thin as a thread".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sunspot ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_on_Sunspots

F24. George Ellery Hale found magnetic fields in sunspots in 1908 at Mount Wilson, from the Zeeman splitting of their spectral lines, with fields up to about 3,000 gauss; Hale's polarity law: the magnetic polarities of sunspot pairs reverse each 11-year cycle, so the full pattern repeats about every 22 years. NASA: about every 11 years the Sun's poles change their magnetic polarity.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/George_Ellery_Hale ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunspot ; https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/

F25. Heinrich Schwabe, an apothecary (pharmacist) of Dessau, Germany, looked for a hypothetical planet inside Mercury's orbit (Vulcan); "For 17 years, from 1826 to 1843, on every clear day" he scanned the Sun and recorded sunspots. He found a "probable ten-year period" in their number, published in 1843 ("Solar Observations during 1843"). It "at first attracted little attention"; Rudolf Wolf began regular counts, Humboldt used the data in Kosmos, and Schwabe won the Royal Astronomical Society's Gold Medal in 1857. The cycle averages about 11 years, "varying from just under 10 to just over 12" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: average 10.8).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Heinrich_Schwabe ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunspot ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sunspot

## Flares, the Carrington Event and space weather

F26. Solar flares are sudden releases of magnetic energy stored in complex fields near sunspots, powered "primarily by magnetic reconnection in the Sun's corona"; they come mostly from active regions near sunspot groups and are most frequent near the maximum of the 11-year cycle (Grokipedia). NASA: flares are "bursts of light and particles triggered by the release of magnetic energy on the Sun"; coronal mass ejections are "immense clouds of magnetized particles blasted into space". Space weather can disturb satellites, GPS and radio, and power grids: the March 1989 storm blacked out the Hydro-Quebec grid, about 6 million people for up to 9 hours.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_flare ; https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/

F27. The Carrington Event: just before noon on 1 September 1859 Richard Carrington, at his observatory at Redhill, Surrey, and Richard Hodgson independently saw a white-light flare among a group of sunspots, lasting about five minutes. The coronal mass ejection arrived about 17.6 hours later. Auroras were seen as far south as Cuba, Hawaii, Mexico and Colombia; people in the north-eastern United States could read a newspaper by them. Telegraph systems across Europe and North America failed; pylons threw sparks, operators got shocks, paper caught fire. On the Boston-Portland line operators disconnected their batteries and sent messages for about two hours on the induced current alone. The cause was a "geomagnetically induced current" (Wikipedia). Carrington suspected a link between the flare and the storm, but wrote that "one swallow does not make a summer" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carrington_Event ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrington_Event ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Christopher_Carrington

## Stars forge the elements

F28. B2FH: E. Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge, William A. Fowler and Fred Hoyle, "Synthesis of the Elements in Stars", Reviews of Modern Physics 29, 547-650 (1957), describes stars as "the primary sites for the synthesis of nearly all chemical elements" heavier than hydrogen and helium, by eight processes, including the slow s-process in giant stars and the rapid r-process in explosive events such as supernovae. Fowler shared the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics with Chandrasekhar.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/B2FH_paper ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_nucleosynthesis

F29. Stars fuse hydrogen to helium, then helium to carbon and heavier elements, up to iron in massive stars; heavier elements come from neutron capture. A massive star ejects its elements in a supernova, and dying stars of all kinds sweep what they made out into space, seeding new stars (Wikipedia; Natural History Museum). Hydrogen, helium and a little lithium were made in the Big Bang.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_nucleosynthesis ; https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/are-we-really-made-of-stardust.html

F30. NASA: "The hydrogen in your body and present in every molecule of water came from the Big Bang"; "The carbon in your body was made by nuclear fusion in the interior of stars, as was the oxygen"; "Much of the iron in your body was made during supernovae". NHM: "nearly all the elements in the human body were made in a star." By mass the body is about 65 percent oxygen, 18.5 percent carbon and 9.5-10 percent hydrogen; hydrogen is about 62 percent of its atoms.
    https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2026-september-14-where-your-elements-came-from/ ; https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/are-we-really-made-of-stardust.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_of_the_human_body

F31. How we know stars make elements now: technetium has no stable isotopes (its longest-lived, technetium-97, has a half-life of 4.21 million years), yet its lines appear in the light of some red giant stars; the 1952 detection (Paul Merrill) "was unambiguous proof of nucleosynthesis in stars" (Wikipedia), since it must have been made there recently, by the s-process (Grokipedia). (Not used in the questions.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Technetium ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technetium_star

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- The Sun is a fire, and it cannot burn only because there is no air or oxygen in space (F4: the Sun holds oxygen; F5: the real reason is that any burning would be spent in a few thousand years).
- Heat cannot cross empty space (F9: radiation needs no medium).
- Fusion happens all through the Sun, or at its surface, so sunspots and flares are spots where fusion stops or bursts (F1, F2: fusion is in the core only; F22, F26: spots and flares are magnetic).
- Sunspots are holes, clouds or objects passing in front of the Sun (F23: Scheiner's small moons; F22).
- Light leaving the core reaches Earth in eight minutes (F14: eight minutes is only the last leg).
- The Sun's gravity holds light back, or heat leaves by conduction (F14: the light is absorbed and re-emitted; the outer third convects).
- Heat makes nuclei lose their charge, or pressure alone pushes them together (F12).
- Everything in a body is "star stuff" (F30: the hydrogen is from the Big Bang).
- The Earth's oldest rocks give the Sun's age (F19, F20).
- Auroral particles fall straight onto telegraph wires (F27: the current was induced by the changing magnetic field).

## Added in revision (2026-11-27)

F32. The first solids of the Solar System: calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions in meteorites are "among the first solids condensed from the cooling protoplanetary disk", "the oldest dated solids, except the presolar grains" (stardust from older stars, which predates the Sun), dated by lead isotopes to 4,567.30 +/- 0.16 million years; this "is commonly used to define the age of the Solar System" (Wikipedia). So the Sun's age comes from the Solar System's first solids, not simply from the oldest grains in meteorites.

F33. The Sun is slowly brightening on the main sequence: "increasing in brightness by about 1% every 100 million years" (Wikipedia, Sun); Grokipedia (Future of Earth): "a luminosity rise of approximately 1% every 110 million years". Its core hydrogen runs out in about 5 billion years, when it leaves the main sequence (Wikipedia, Sun; Grokipedia, Sun).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Future_of_Earth ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun

F34. The idea was abandoned: the Sun is gas, and hotter, not darker, below its surface (F1). Galileo's Letters on Sunspots (1613) came first (F23).
