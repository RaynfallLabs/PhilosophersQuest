## What a plate is, and how fast it moves

F1. Earth's rigid outer shell, the lithosphere, is the crust together with the uppermost mantle, and it is broken into plates: about a dozen large ones and many smaller ones (counts differ by source). Oceanic lithosphere is about 50-100 km thick; continental lithosphere about 100-200 km. Plates are therefore thicker than the crust alone.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lithosphere

F2. The plates ride on the asthenosphere, "a hotter, more ductile zone that allows lithospheric plates to move slowly" (Grokipedia); it is solid rock that flows, with only about 0.2-2 percent partial melt.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lithosphere ; https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/intro/misconception_list.html

F3. Plates move "at rates of a few centimeters per year", typically 1 to 10 cm a year (Grokipedia). USGS: "Tectonic plates move roughly at the same rate that your fingernails grow", each at its own speed and in its own direction, and GPS measures the motion "within a fraction of a millimeter per year". NOAA gives an average of about 1.5 cm a year between land masses, and almost 5 cm a year for coastal California against the continent's interior.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics ; https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/how-fast-do-tectonic-plates-move ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/tectonics.html

## What moves them

F4. Mantle convection, "the slow, creeping motion of the Earth's solid silicate mantle" in which "hotter, less dense material rises while cooler, denser material sinks", is described as the primary mechanism driving plate tectonics. Its heat comes mostly from radioactive decay (uranium, thorium, potassium) and from heat left over from Earth's formation, with some from the core. Mantle flow runs about 1 cm a year in the upper mantle and up to about 10 cm a year in the asthenosphere. NOAA: "The heat from radioactive processes within the planet's interior causes the plates to move".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mantle_convection ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/tectonics.html

F5. The cold slabs that sink at subduction zones are the downwelling part of mantle convection, and slab pull, the drag of a plate's sinking edge, is estimated at 50-70 percent of the force driving plate motion; ridge push is smaller.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mantle_convection ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics

F6. Earth's rotation (a "polar-fleeing" centrifugal force) and the tidal pull of the Moon and Sun were once proposed to move continents; Harold Jeffreys calculated such forces to be "orders of magnitude too weak". (Used only as a wrong choice; the story belongs to the Wegener unit.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Continental_drift

## Divergent boundaries

F7. At a divergent boundary plates move apart; hot mantle rock rises, partly melts as the pressure drops, and the magma rises and cools into new oceanic crust (basalt, about 5-10 km thick). Under the oceans this builds the mid-ocean ridge system, about 65,000 km long, "the longest mountain chain on Earth". The ridge stands high because its new rock is hot and expanded; the sea floor deepens away from it as the rock cools. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads about 2.5 cm a year, the East Pacific Rise 6-16 cm a year.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Divergent_boundary ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mid-ocean_ridge

F8. Divergence inside a continent makes a continental rift: rift valleys with volcanoes and shallow earthquakes. If divergence persists for millions of years it can "lead to the formation of new ocean basins"; the Red Sea is "a young continental rift in transition toward oceanic spreading", and the north of the East African Rift (Afar) is in a "proto-oceanic stage". Wikipedia: "Successful rifting leads to seafloor spreading along a mid-oceanic ridge"; a failed rift can instead be left as an aulacogen.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Divergent_boundary ; https://grokipedia.com/page/East_African_Rift ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rift

F9. Divergent boundaries make crust; this production is balanced by crust consumed at subduction zones, so Earth's surface does not grow.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Divergent_boundary ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Convergent_boundary

F10. The expanding Earth hypothesis (Roberto Mantovani from 1889, Ott Christoph Hilgenberg in 1933, Samuel Warren Carey from 1958) held that Earth's radius grows, opening new ocean basins without subduction. It was rejected: Benioff zones and volcanic arcs show plates sinking, no mechanism could supply the energy, and space geodesy finds no expansion. A NASA-led team under Xiaoping Wu (JPL, 2011) measured the average change in Earth's radius at about 0.1 mm a year, statistically insignificant: "the solid Earth is not getting larger at present, within current measurement uncertainties."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Expanding_Earth ; https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-research-confirms-its-a-small-world-after-all/

## Convergent boundaries

F11. There are three kinds of convergent boundary. Ocean-continent: the ocean plate sinks, making a deep trench, a continental volcanic arc and mountains (the Andes). Ocean-ocean: one ocean plate sinks beneath the other, making an island arc. Continent-continent: the plates collide "without significant subduction" and the crust shortens and thickens into mountain belts (the Himalayas).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Convergent_boundary ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics

F12. Oceanic crust (about 2.9 g/cm3) is denser than continental crust (about 2.7 g/cm3), and oceanic lithosphere grows colder, thicker and denser with age until it is gravitationally unstable, so "the denser oceanic plate sinks into the mantle beneath the less dense plate". Where two ocean plates meet, the denser, typically older and colder, one sinks. Continental lithosphere is buoyant and resists subduction.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Convergent_boundary ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics

F13. Trenches are deep, V-shaped depressions where a plate bends down and sinks; the Mariana Trench reaches about 11 km at the Challenger Deep.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Convergent_boundary ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics

F14. Arc volcanoes: water released from the sinking slab lowers the melting point of the hot mantle rock above it, which partly melts ("flux melting"); the magma rises to feed a line of volcanoes on the overriding plate, about 100-300 km inland from the trench for a continental arc. An island arc is "a curved chain of volcanic islands and seamounts formed above a subduction zone", parallel to a deep trench (the Aleutian, Mariana and Japanese arcs); many of its volcanoes are active at once, and it shows no age-ordered line of volcanoes like a hot spot chain.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Island_arc ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Convergent_boundary

F15. In a continent-continent collision the crust is folded and thrust-stacked, thickening from about 35 km to about 70 km under the Himalayas; arc volcanism stops ("cessation of arc volcanism"); earthquakes continue along thrust faults. India and Asia still converge about 4-5 cm a year, and GPS shows Everest rising about 2 mm a year.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Continental_collision ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Convergent_boundary

## Transform boundaries and earthquakes

F16. At a transform boundary "two tectonic plates slide horizontally past one another" along a strike-slip fault, "without creating or destroying crustal material"; they are "rarely associated with volcanism" but have frequent earthquakes. Most transform faults lie on the ocean floor, offsetting segments of mid-ocean ridges; on land the San Andreas Fault moves about 5 cm a year.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Transform_fault ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics

F17. Elastic rebound: friction locks a fault while the plates keep moving, so the rock on either side bends and stores strain (Wikipedia: it "may build at the rate of a few centimeters per year"); when the strain exceeds the rock's strength the fault slips suddenly and the rock snaps back, and the stored energy goes out partly as seismic waves. Harry Fielding Reid proposed it (1910, after a 1908 report) from the displacement of the ground along the San Andreas Fault in the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. Creeping sections of a fault slip gradually "without major seismic events".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Elastic-rebound_theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic-rebound_theory

F18. In the 1906 earthquake a fence near Bolinas, photographed by G. K. Gilbert, "was offset about 8.5 feet" (about 2.6 m) along the fault; an offset of 20 feet was seen near Point Reyes.
    https://earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/events/1906calif/18april/offset.php ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Elastic-rebound_theory

## Hot spots and island chains

F19. A hot spot is a place where "exceptionally hot material rises as a plume from deep within the interior", making volcanoes "independent of tectonic plate boundaries". As a plate moves over a "relatively fixed" hot spot it leaves a line of volcanoes that grow older with distance from the active one. Older volcanoes cool and sink as the plate beneath them cools, and erosion wears them down until they drown as seamounts. J. Tuzo Wilson proposed the idea in 1963 to explain the Hawaiian Islands.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hotspot_(geology) ; https://www.soest.hawaii.edu/GG/HCV/haw_formation.html

F20. The Hawaiian chain: the Pacific Plate moves north-west over the hot spot at 5-10 cm a year; Kauai is about 5.1 million years old, the Big Island's volcanoes under half a million, and the islands grow older toward the west-north-west. The youngest volcano of the chain, the undersea Kamaʻehuakanaloa (formerly Lōʻihi), lies about 35 km south-east of the island of Hawaiʻi, its summit about 975 m below sea level, and may break the surface in roughly 10,000 to 100,000 years.
    https://www.soest.hawaii.edu/GG/HCV/haw_formation.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kamaʻehuakanaloa_Seamount ; https://www.usgs.gov/news/volcano-watch-island-hawaii-sits-end-long-old-chain-125-volcanoes (USGS: a chain of "more than 125 volcanoes" over 3,720 miles; the islands "systematically increase in age towards the west-northwest"; Kauai 5.0-5.5 million years)

## Where earthquakes and volcanoes happen

F21. About 90 percent of earthquakes occur at plate boundaries, and the Pacific Ring of Fire holds about 75 percent of Earth's volcanoes (Grokipedia). USGS: the circum-Pacific belt, along plate boundaries where mostly oceanic plates are sinking, has "about 81 percent of our planet's largest earthquakes"; the Alpide belt (Java to the Mediterranean, through the Himalayas) about 17 percent; the Mid-Atlantic Ridge is a third zone. Damaging earthquakes do strike away from these belts, but rarely.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics ; https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/where-do-earthquakes-occur

F22. A passive margin is a coast where continent meets ocean floor with no active plate boundary (the Atlantic coasts of the Americas, Africa and Europe); it has "subdued seismicity alongside sparse volcanism".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Passive_margin

F23. The contracting Earth hypothesis, "prevalent in the 19th and early 20th centuries", held "that mountain building is caused by the contraction of the Earth" as it cooled, like "the wrinkles that form on a drying apple"; mountains "mainly result from plate tectonic motions" (A Dictionary of Earth Sciences).
    https://encyclopedia.com/science/dictionaries-thesauruses-pictures-and-press-releases/contracting-earth-hypothesis

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. "The edge of a continent is the same thing as a plate boundary"; "Only continents move"; "Crust and Lithosphere (or plates) are synonymous terms" (F1, F22).
    https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/intro/misconception_list.html

M2. "Asthenosphere is liquid"; "Magma comes from molten layer beneath Earth's crust": plates floating on a sea of magma (F2, F14).
    https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/intro/misconception_list.html

M3. "Divergent ocean ridges are due to vertical uplift or convergence, rather than divergence"; "Oceans are responsible for oceanic crust" (F7).
    https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/intro/misconception_list.html

M4. Plate speed at the extremes: "Plate movement is imperceptible on a human timeframe", or so "rapid enough that continent collision can cause financial and political chaos" (F3).
    https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/intro/misconception_list.html

M5. "Earthquakes occur from collapse of subterranean hollow spaces" (an old explanation), and that plates stand still and then lurch, rather than a locked fault giving way (F17).
    https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/intro/misconception_list.html

M6. That the sinking plate itself simply melts to feed arc volcanoes: a common textbook shortcut. The magma comes mostly from the mantle rock above the slab, melted by water from the slab (F14). No question keys either version; contexts say "water it carries down helps the hot rock above it melt".

M7. Respectable wrong theories of the past: the expanding Earth (F10) and the contracting Earth (F23).

M8. Earth's spin or the tides moving the plates (F6).

F25. The Sun's heat reaches only the top layer of the ground: the seasonal swing fades within about 8-20 m of the surface, and below that the ground's temperature rises with depth by the geothermal gradient (the same fact the earths-layers unit sourced). So sunlight cannot drive the plates.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_source_heat_pump ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Geothermal_gradient

Wording note for F17: Grokipedia says the rocks beside a locked fault "deform elastically under continuous stress" and store the strain like a bent spring; seventh-grade texts call the build-up "stress", so the question says the fault stuck "while stress built". The plates' "great plates ... wider than a continent" (a major plate carries a whole continent and ocean floor beside it) and "roughly 100 kilometres thick" come from F1.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F27. The forces that move a plate, ranked: slab pull "accounts for approximately 70% of the total driving force for plate motions", and ridge push is "typically 5-10% of slab pull's effect" (Grokipedia Plate tectonics, which also calls mantle convection "the primary mechanism driving plate tectonics"); plate velocities are "driven primarily by subduction", the sinking slabs being the downwelling part of mantle convection (Grokipedia Mantle convection, F5); Wikipedia: slab pull "is therefore most widely thought to be the greatest force acting on the plates", since the asthenosphere is too weak to drag plates along by friction. So the key names the churning of hot AND cold rock (hot rock rising, cold plate edges sinking), and the context ranks the sinking edge's pull first and the ridges' push well behind.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mantle_convection ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics

F28. USGS: "An earthquake is caused by a sudden slip on a fault. The tectonic plates are always slowly moving, but they get stuck at their edges due to friction. When the stress on the edge overcomes the friction, there is an earthquake." Earthquakes are a result of the plates' motion, not what drives it (a wrong choice in d8c219c10d).
    https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-earthquake-and-what-causes-them-happen

F29. Old mountain ranges can lie inside a plate: the Appalachians formed "primarily through a series of Paleozoic orogenies beginning around 480 million years ago", "one of the oldest mountain systems on Earth", after which "tectonic quiescence ensued"; they stand along North America's Atlantic side, a passive margin with no active plate boundary (F22). So only YOUNG mountain belts follow today's plate edges (51db9da9bd's context).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Appalachian_Mountains ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Passive_margin

Hot spot chain, re-checked for the revised 6669574224: the Hawaiian islands "systematically increase in age towards the west-northwest", and "the volcanoes are moved away from where they form by motion of the underlying Pacific crustal plate"; the active volcanoes over the hot spot are at the south-east end (USGS).
