## The cycle and interphase

F1. A cell's life is a cycle: interphase, made of three parts (G1, S and G2), then mitosis. Interphase "occupies the majority of the cycle", about 90 percent of it (Grokipedia Interphase; Wikipedia: a typical cell spends "90% of its life" in interphase). A mammalian cell's whole cycle takes about 24 hours under good conditions, and mitosis "typically lasts 30-60 minutes".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_cycle ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Interphase ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interphase

F2. In G1 the cell grows and makes proteins and organelles; in S (synthesis) it copies (replicates) its DNA, doubling the amount while the number of chromosomes stays the same; in G2 it goes on growing and makes ready for mitosis. Each chromosome therefore enters mitosis as two identical copies (sister chromatids).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interphase ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Interphase ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mitosis

F3. MISCONCEPTION: interphase is a rest. Walther Flemming described it in 1882 as a "resting stage" between divisions; that view was wrong (Grokipedia Interphase). Wikipedia: interphase "was formerly called the 'resting phase'", but a cell in interphase is "very busy".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Interphase ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interphase

F4. Alma Howard (1913-1984, a Canadian-born English radiobiologist) and the physicist Stephen Pelc followed the uptake of radioactive phosphorus-32 into the nuclei of dividing cells in the root tips of the broad bean (Vicia faba), using autoradiographs. They reached the "surprising conclusion that DNA replication occurs during a limited period in interphase", which they called the S phase; the gap before it they called G1 and the gap after it G2, and they coined the term "cell cycle". Wikipedia and Grokipedia date the work to 1953 (a study guide gives 1951 for a first report), so no year is used in the questions. MISCONCEPTION it overturned: that DNA is copied during division itself.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alma_Howard ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_cycle

## Walther Flemming names mitosis

F5. Walther Flemming studied cell division in salamanders (Grokipedia: fixed epithelial tissues from salamander larvae; Wikipedia: the fins and gills, both living and stained) using aniline dyes. A material in the nucleus took up the dye strongly, and he named it chromatin (from the Greek chroma, colour). He coined the word mitosis, from the Greek for thread, for the threads of the dividing nucleus, in his 1882 book Zellsubstanz, Kern und Zelltheilung. He did not know Gregor Mendel's work. (The two sources disagree on whether he saw the threads split lengthwise into identical halves, so that is not used.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Walther_Flemming ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walther_Flemming ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitosis

## The stages of mitosis

F6. Prophase: the chromatin condenses into compact, visible chromosomes (shortened "thousands of times" against interphase, Grokipedia), so that they can be "accurately aligned and separated later in mitosis"; the spindle of microtubules begins to form. In prometaphase the nuclear envelope breaks up and spindle fibres attach to the kinetochores, protein structures at each chromosome's centromere.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mitosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitosis

F7. Metaphase: the chromosomes line up across the cell's middle (the metaphase plate). For the copies to part correctly, "the two kinetochores on the sister chromatids must be attached to opposite spindle poles", which ensures each daughter cell gets one copy of each chromosome. The spindle checkpoint holds back anaphase "until each chromosome is properly attached to the spindle"; "Even a single unattached kinetochore can maintain the spindle checkpoint."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spindle_checkpoint ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mitosis

F8. Anaphase: the proteins holding the sister chromatids together are cut, and shortening microtubules pull the two copies of each chromosome to opposite ends of the cell. Telophase: a new nuclear envelope forms round each set, and the chromosomes relax (decondense). The result is two "genetically identical cells in which the total number of chromosomes is maintained." MISCONCEPTION: that mitosis halves the chromosomes or splits each one into two halves of its DNA.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitosis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mitosis

F9. Errors: in nondisjunction the sister chromatids fail to separate, giving daughter cells with unequal chromosome numbers, too few or too many (aneuploidy). When the spindle checkpoint fails, it "can lead to chromosome missegregation, aneuploidy and even tumorigenesis"; aneuploidy is "the most common characteristic of human solid tumors" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mitosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spindle_checkpoint

F10. The other checkpoints are different checks: the G1/S checkpoint weighs growth, nutrients and DNA integrity before DNA is copied, and the G2/M checkpoint keeps a cell out of mitosis "until DNA replication is complete and any damage is repaired". Copying errors and DNA damage are their business, not the spindle checkpoint's.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_cycle ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_cycle_checkpoint

## Cytokinesis

F11. Cytokinesis is the division of the cell itself, a separate process after the nucleus divides. An animal cell is pinched in two by a contractile ring of actin filaments and myosin at its equator, which draws in a cleavage furrow. A plant cell has a rigid wall (and its inner turgor pressure "would resist furrow ingression", Grokipedia), so it builds a cell plate instead: vesicles from the Golgi apparatus, guided by the phragmoplast, fuse in the middle of the cell, and the plate grows from the centre outward until it joins the parent wall, laying down callose and then cellulose. MISCONCEPTION: that a plant cell pinches in two like an animal cell.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cytokinesis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytokinesis

## Why the body needs mitosis

F12. Mitosis serves growth and development, tissue repair, and "replenishing cells lost to normal turnover or damage".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mitosis

F13. A human red blood cell has no nucleus and lasts about 120 days (about four months); about 200 billion are made every day in the bone marrow. The blood-forming (haematopoietic) stem cells of the marrow make all the mature kinds of blood cell.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Red_blood_cell ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Stem_cell

F14. The lining of the intestine turns over every 3-5 days in humans (Grokipedia also gives 2-5), driven by stem cells dividing at the base of the crypts.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Intestinal_epithelium

F15. Some cells leave the cycle for good: the resting state G0 is "irreversible in post-mitotic cells such as neurons and cardiomyocytes" (nerve cells and heart muscle cells).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_cycle

F16. Traditional chemotherapy drugs "are cytotoxic by means of interfering with cell division" (Wikipedia); they "target rapidly dividing cells, including cancer cells", and "can also affect healthy cells that divide quickly, such as those in the bone marrow, digestive tract, and hair follicles" (Grokipedia). Hence the most common side effects: fewer blood cells made in the marrow (myelosuppression), mouth sores and hair loss. Some, the vinca alkaloids and taxanes, act on the mitotic spindle and halt mitosis. MISCONCEPTION: that such drugs harm only cancer cells.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemotherapy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemotherapy

## Differentiation

F17. An adult human has about 37.2 trillion cells, each carrying its own copy of the genome, with exceptions such as red blood cells, which lack a nucleus. Differentiation "almost never involves a change in the DNA sequence itself"; "Each specialized cell type in an organism expresses a subset of all the genes" and cell type is settled "at the level of gene expression" (Wikipedia). Grokipedia: differentiation changes gene expression "without modifying the underlying DNA sequence", guided by "extrinsic signals from the microenvironment, such as growth factors, hormones, and mechanical forces". It begins with the zygote, the fertilised egg, which can give rise to every cell type.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_differentiation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cellular_differentiation

F18. The respectable wrong theory: August Weismann held that the germ plasm ("effectively, genes") "was successively reduced during division of somatic cells", so that cells specialise by losing hereditary material; "As modern genetics developed, it became clear that this idea is wrong in most cases" (Wikipedia). Gurdon, from his frogs: "the loss or permanent inactivation of genes does not necessarily accompany the normal differentiation of animal cells", and specialisation "involves the differential activity of genes present in all cells" (Scientific American). MISCONCEPTION: that a specialised cell keeps only the genes for its own job.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germ_plasm ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/transplanted-nuclei-and-cell-differentiation-gurdon/

## Stem cells

F19. Stem cells are undifferentiated cells that can renew themselves and can also differentiate into specialised cells. Embryonic stem cells are pluripotent: they "can form any cell type in the body". Adult stem cells are multipotent: the haematopoietic stem cells of the bone marrow make "all mature blood cell types". Induced pluripotent stem cells are made by reprogramming body cells.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Stem_cell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_differentiation

F20. John Gurdon, working with the African clawed frog (Xenopus laevis), used ultraviolet light to destroy the egg's own nucleus ("irrevocably damaging the native DNA", ASU) and put in the nucleus of an intestinal (gut lining) cell from a feeding tadpole; his 1962 paper is "The developmental capacity of nuclei taken from intestinal epithelium cells of feeding tadpoles". Only a few transfers worked (Grokipedia gives figures from about 1 to 2.5 percent). The donor nuclei carried a mutation, kept in Michail Fischberg's laboratory, that leaves one nucleolus where normal frog cells have two: "the nuclei of cells carrying the mutation never have more than one", and the frogs grown from transplanted intestine nuclei "carried the mutant nuclear marker in their cells" (Scientific American). ASU: tadpoles from transplanted mutant nuclei showed the mutant traits, which "indicated that the donor nucleus, not the egg's own DNA, directed development". Grokipedia: the marker ensured that the traits were "traced to the donor nucleus rather than residual host material".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/John_Gurdon ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/john-bertrand-gurdon-1933 ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/developmental-capacity-nuclei-taken-intestinal-epithelium-cells-feeding-tadpoles-1962-john-b ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/transplanted-nuclei-and-cell-differentiation-gurdon/

F21. The doubts: critics suggested the successes came from "possible contamination by residual undifferentiated cells or incomplete enucleation of host eggs" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: at first "he could not conclusively show that the transplanted nuclei derived from a fully differentiated cell". The nucleolus marker answered the second doubt but not the first, since a stray unspecialised cell from the same tadpole would carry the marker too. In 1975 a group at the Basel Institute for Immunology "transplanted a nucleus from an antibody-producing lymphocyte" (a white blood cell) into an egg "and obtained living tadpoles" (Wikipedia). Many scientists, among them Robert Briggs and Thomas King's laboratory, doubted Gurdon's results (ASU).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/John_Gurdon ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gurdon ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/john-bertrand-gurdon-1933

F22. Shinya Yamanaka and Kazutoshi Takahashi chose "24 candidate genes expressed in embryonic stem (ES) cells", delivered them by retroviruses into mouse embryonic and adult fibroblasts (connective-tissue cells), and found that a few cells were reset; "the researchers removed one factor at a time from the pool of twenty-four" and found four factors, Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc, "which were each necessary" (Wikipedia), and the four were "sufficient to induce pluripotency" (Grokipedia). Published in Cell on 25 August 2006. Only about 0.01-0.1 percent of the cells were reset (Wikipedia; Grokipedia gives 0.01-0.05 percent). The reset cells, induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells, are "capable of developing into any cell type" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Shinya_Yamanaka ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Induced_pluripotent_stem_cell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_pluripotent_stem_cell

F23. The 2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to John Gurdon and Shinya Yamanaka "for the discovery that mature cells can be reprogrammed to become pluripotent."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/John_Gurdon ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Shinya_Yamanaka ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gurdon

F24. c-Myc is a proto-oncogene, a gene that can drive cancer; "25% of mice transplanted with c-myc-induced iPS cells developed lethal" tumours (teratomas). Leaving c-Myc out is possible, but the reset is slower and less efficient.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_pluripotent_stem_cell ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Induced_pluripotent_stem_cell

## The cycle's controls

F25. Tim Hunt, in the late summer of 1982 at the Marine Biological Laboratory in Woods Hole, used embryos of the sea urchin Arbacia punctulata in seawater with radioactive methionine to label new proteins. Some proteins built up between divisions, then "underwent sudden, near-complete degradation precisely at the onset of each mitosis"; he and his colleagues named them cyclins in 1983, for their rise and fall through each cycle (Wikipedia: "their concentration varies in a cyclical fashion"). Cyclins switch on the cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), which drive the cell through the cycle.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tim_Hunt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclin

F26. Leland Hartwell, working with budding (baker's) yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, isolated "over 140 temperature-sensitive cell division cycle" (cdc) mutants, which showed the cycle as "a series of discrete, interdependent events"; CDC28 controls a commitment point he called "Start". He proposed the checkpoint concept in the late 1980s (Hartwell and Weinert, Science 1989, "Checkpoints: controls that ensure the order of cell cycle events"): checkpoints "delay cell division when cellular insults are generated" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Leland_H._Hartwell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leland_H._Hartwell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_cycle_checkpoint

F27. Ted Weinert and Leland Hartwell (Science 241, 317-322, 1988) irradiated yeast with X-rays. Normal cells arrested or delayed division in G2, those hit in G2 delaying "for a time proportional to the extent of damage before resuming cell division". Irradiated rad9 mutants "do not delay cell division in G2, but continue to divide for several generations and die". But "efficient DNA repair can occur in irradiated rad9 cells if irradiated cells are blocked for several hours in G2 by treatment with a microtubule poison" (a drug that stops division). They concluded that the RAD9-dependent response detects potentially lethal DNA damage and holds the cell in G2 until the damage is repaired; "The RAD9 gene product is essential for arrest of cell division induced by DNA damage." Grokipedia: mutants lacking RAD9 "failed to arrest, leading to increased chromosome loss".
    https://scholars.houstonmethodist.org/en/publications/the-rad9-gene-controls-the-cell-cycle-response-to-dna-damage-in-s/ ; https://www.yeastgenome.org/reference/S000046013 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Leland_H._Hartwell

F28. The 2001 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Leland Hartwell, Tim Hunt and Paul Nurse "for their discoveries of key regulators of the cell cycle".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tim_Hunt ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Leland_H._Hartwell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclin

## Cancer

F29. Cancer is cell division escaping its controls: "Dysregulation of these controls, often through mutations in cyclins, CDKs, or checkpoint genes" leads to uncontrolled proliferation, a hallmark of cancer; loss of the checkpoint protein p53 is "the most frequent genetic alteration in cancers", found in about half of tumours, and lets damaged cells go on dividing (Grokipedia). Hartwell: checkpoint defects "could underlie the genomic instability characteristic of tumor cells" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_cycle ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Leland_H._Hartwell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_cycle_checkpoint

F30. Contact inhibition: normal mammalian cells in culture "formed orderly monolayers and halted division upon reaching confluence" (once they cover the dish in a single layer); "Loss of contact inhibition, however, is a hallmark of cancer", and cancer cells "continue proliferating, forming multilayered foci" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: "Cancerous cells typically lose this property and thus divide and grow over each other". (Grokipedia's example cell line is left out of the questions.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Contact_inhibition ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_inhibition

F31. "Unlike normal cells, cancer cells don't stop growing and dividing when there are enough of them", and they can ignore the signals that tell them to die; they "often reproduce very quickly" too (Cancer Research UK). MISCONCEPTION: that cancer simply means faster division; many cancers do divide fast, but the defining fault is that division does not stop.
    https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/what-is-cancer/how-cancer-starts/cancer-cells ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Contact_inhibition

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- Interphase is a resting stage (F3: it is when the cell grows and copies its DNA, F2, F4).
- DNA is copied during mitosis, as the chromosomes appear or part (F4).
- Mitosis splits each chromosome into halves of its DNA, or halves the number (F8).
- A plant cell pinches in two as an animal cell does (F11).
- A specialised cell keeps only the genes for its own job (F18, F20).
- Cancer drugs harm only cancer cells (F16).
- Cancer is just cells dividing faster (F30, F31).
