# Telling Materials Apart: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-25. Grokipedia first; school and museum sources to corroborate and for the misconceptions.
Map line G5.P1 (TEKS 5.6A, NGSS 5-PS1-3).

## Facts

F1 Mass is the amount of matter in an object. A balance finds an object's mass by comparing it with known masses; the kilogram is the base unit, and small objects are measured in grams. Mass stays the same wherever the object is. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass ("quantifies the amount of matter in an object"; "balances determine mass by comparing the unknown object to standard masses"; "The kilogram (kg) is the base unit of mass"); TEKS 5.6A names mass first among the properties a 5th grader compares: https://teksguide.org/teks/s56a-0/alignments

F2 Volume is the amount of space an object takes up. One millilitre is one cubic centimetre. https://grokipedia.com/page/Volume ("the measure of the three-dimensional space occupied by matter"; a millilitre is one cubic centimetre)

F3 A bigger object does not always have more mass than a smaller one: mass and volume are different properties. https://www.acer.org/au/news/article/three-science-concepts-primary-students-struggle-to-grasp (ACER: "27% of 8,433 students believed bigger objects always have more mass than smaller objects", a misconception "highly resistant to change"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass; https://grokipedia.com/page/Volume

F4 A magnet pulls strongly on iron, nickel and cobalt (ferromagnetic metals) and on most steel, which is mostly iron. Electromagnet cranes lift steel scrap. https://grokipedia.com/page/Ferromagnetism ("iron, nickel, cobalt"; "strong attraction to external fields"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnet ("iron, nickel, and cobalt"; cranes "lift and transport ferrous metals like steel scrap"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Steel ("an alloy consisting primarily of iron and up to 2.1 percent carbon"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-primary-ks2/units/simple-forces-including-magnets/lessons/magnetic-and-non-magnetic-materials ("Iron is magnetic, so is steel because it contains iron"; "Most other metals are not magnetic")

F5 Most other metals are not pulled by a magnet. Aluminium is only very slightly drawn (far too little to notice with an ordinary magnet); copper, silver and gold are diamagnetic and are even pushed very slightly away. https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnet (aluminium "only slightly drawn toward magnets"; copper shows "no permanent magnetism"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Diamagnetism ("water, copper, silver, gold, bismuth"); Oak National Academy exit quiz: "silver - non-magnetic" (URL in F4)

F6 Recycling centres pull steel cans out of mixed rubbish with big magnets; aluminium cans are sorted by a different machine. https://grokipedia.com/page/Materials_recovery_facility ("Ferrous metals, including steel cans, are then extracted using overhead or drum magnets"; aluminium removed by eddy current separators); https://grokipedia.com/page/Steel (steel collected for recycling thanks to "steel's magnetic properties")

F7 Whether a thing floats or sinks in water depends on how heavy it is for its size compared with water (its density compared with water's), not on its weight alone: a thing less dense than water floats, a thing denser than water sinks. https://grokipedia.com/page/Buoyancy ("if less than 1, the object floats; if greater than 1, it sinks"; "wooden blocks floating in lakes or oceans"); TEKS 5.6A names "relative density (sinking and floating using water as a reference point)": https://teksguide.org/teks/s56a-0/alignments

F8 Pine is a softwood, with a density of about 300-500 kg per cubic metre (specific gravity 0.30-0.51), well under water's 1,000 kg per cubic metre, so a pine log floats however big and heavy it is. https://grokipedia.com/page/Softwood (softwood comes from "conifers such as pines"; specific gravity 0.30-0.50, table up to 0.51 for loblolly pine); https://grokipedia.com/page/Wood (most species 320-720 kg/m3; a few, such as lignum vitae, are denser than water)

F9 Ordinary stone is far denser than water: granite is 2.65-2.75 grams per cubic centimetre (water is 1), so even a tiny pebble sinks. (Pumice, a rock full of gas holes, is the famous exception that floats; no question uses "every rock sinks".) https://grokipedia.com/page/Granite ("a density typically ranging from 2.65 to 2.75 g/cm3")

F10 The volume of a jagged object such as a rock is found by displacement: sunk in water in a measuring (graduated) cylinder, a stone raises the water level by its own volume. 5th-grade lessons teach this. https://grokipedia.com/page/Displacement_(fluid) ("submerging a stone in a measuring cylinder raises the water level by an amount corresponding to the stone's volume"; the method suits "irregular solids like rocks"); https://www.lessonplanet.com/teachers/volume-of-irregular-objects (grade 5: "5th graders identify the volume of irregular objects using the process of water displacement"); https://inquiryproject.terc.edu/curriculum/curriculum4/4_mineralmaterials/inv4_2/index.html (grade 4: rock volume by the rise of water in a container)

F11 Salt and sugar dissolve readily in water: about 36 grams of salt in 100 grams of water at 25 C, and about 200 grams of sugar in 100 millilitres at 20 C. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium_chloride ("a solubility of 360 g per kg of water at 25 C"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sucrose ("dissolving at approximately 200 g per 100 mL at 20 C")

F12 Sand is mostly silica (quartz), which is effectively insoluble in water: only about 6-14 milligrams dissolve in a litre. Stirred into water, sand settles to the bottom. https://grokipedia.com/page/Silicon_dioxide ("Silica sand, primarily composed of silicon dioxide"; "effectively insoluble under standard conditions")

F13 Dissolving is not melting. Melting is one substance changing state, from solid to liquid, when heated; dissolving is one substance spreading through another (two substances interacting). Dissolved sugar seems to disappear, but it is still there: its mass stays, and it comes back when the water evaporates. https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/sugar-water (NSTA probe, Grade 5: "melting involves a change of state and does not require the interaction between two substances as dissolving does"; students "see the sugar 'disappear' but may not understand where it goes"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium_chloride (salt "isolated by evaporation or crystallization")

F14 Metals are good conductors of heat (copper 401 W/m K); air is a very poor one (about 0.026 W/m K), and wood, plastic, wool and rubber are thermal insulators. Heat flows from warmer things to cooler things. https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_conductivity ("Metals and alloys are characterized by exceptionally high thermal conductivities"; copper 401; air "approximately 0.026 W/m K"; polymers 0.1-0.3); https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_conduction ("from regions of higher temperature to regions of lower temperature"); https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2919 (Ohio State, grades 3-5: "heat moves from warmer objects to cooler ones")

F15 A metal object and a wooden one left in the same place are at the same temperature, yet the metal feels colder, because metal conducts heat away from a warm hand much faster than wood does. A hand senses the flow of heat, not the temperature. https://annex.exploratorium.edu/xref/exhibits/cold_metal.html ("all of the materials should be at room temperature"; "Metal is a better conductor than wood or styrofoam and hence feels the coldest"); https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/1829 ("metal conducts heat much better than wood does"; "Since the metal conducts better, that cools your hand faster, and that's what you feel"); https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2919 ("Objects in the same surroundings reach the same temperature regardless of material")

F16 The park-bench test (a metal arm and a wooden seat) is a Year 6 (age 10-11) assessment task: on a sunny day the metal feels hotter, on a cold winter's day it feels colder. https://arbs.nzcer.org.nz/resources/heating-metal-and-wood

F17 Insulation slows the flow of heat; it does not make heat. Wool, fur, down and foam keep things warm by trapping still air, a poor conductor. A thermometer inside an empty mitten stays at room temperature: the mitten makes no heat of its own, and the warmth inside a worn mitten comes from the hand. https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_insulation ("designed to reduce the transfer of thermal energy"; "insulation traps still air"; fibrous insulation works by "the trapping of air"); https://www.tmj4.com/weather/science-experiments/how-do-mittens-actually-work-find-out-with-this-simple-experiment (empty mitten: "The temperature remains largely unchanged"; "the mitten doesn't produce its own heat"; it is "an insulator" trapping the body's warmth); https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2919 (correct idea: warm clothes "keep things warm by trapping heat")

F18 Metals such as copper, silver, gold, aluminium and steel conduct electricity. Glass, rubber, porcelain, air and plastics are insulators, and plastic is used to coat wires. https://grokipedia.com/page/Electrical_conductor (names copper, silver, gold, aluminum and steel as conductors); https://grokipedia.com/page/Insulator_(electricity) ("Common examples of insulators include glass, rubber, porcelain, and air"; plastics such as PVC used in wiring)

F19 A classroom conductivity tester is a battery, a bulb and wires with a gap: an object touched across the two loose ends completes the circuit, and the bulb glows only if the object conducts. Grades 3-5. https://www.teachengineering.org/activities/cub_conduct ("The materials that complete the circuit and let current flow will make the bulb glow")

F20 Hardness is tested by scratching: a harder material scratches a softer one, never the other way round. On the Mohs scale talc (1) is the softest and diamond (10) the hardest; a fingernail is about 2.5, glass and a steel knife blade about 5.5, a steel file about 6.5, quartz 7. A mineral that a knife blade scratches but a fingernail cannot is between them in hardness. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mohs_scale (each mineral "can scratch those of lower rank but not those of equal or higher rank"; the reference list and the common objects)

F21 Colour alone can fool: pyrite ("fool's gold") has gold's brass-yellow colour and metallic shine, but it is much harder (Mohs 6-6.5 against gold's 2.5-3) and brittle, while gold is soft and bends. https://grokipedia.com/page/Pyrite; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gold ("pure gold scratches easily"; "exceptional malleability and ductility")

F22 The grade's standards. NGSS 5-PS1-3: "Make observations and measurements to identify materials based on their properties", with example properties "color, hardness, reflectivity, electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, response to magnetic forces, and solubility". TEKS 5.6A lists mass, magnetism, physical state, relative density (sinking and floating with water as the reference), volume, solubility in water, and conducting or insulating thermal and electric energy. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ps1-3-matter-and-its-interactions; https://teksguide.org/teks/s56a-0/alignments

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Bigger objects always have more mass (mass and size are the same thing). ACER, F3.

M2 All metals, or all silver-coloured things, are pulled by a magnet. Oak National Academy lists "All metals and silver coloured objects are magnetic and are attracted to a magnet" (URL in F4); NASA eClips "Magnets and Metals" is built to "confront the misconception that all metals are attracted to magnets": https://science.nasa.gov/eclips/videos/magnets-and-metals

M3 Heavy things sink and light things float, whatever they are made of; or big things sink and small things float. https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/3181-building-science-concepts-floating-and-sinking (Science Learning Hub, "Alternative conceptions": "The weight of an object determines whether it floats or sinks – light objects float and heavy objects sink"; "small objects float and large objects sink"); the Victoria science continuum (education.vic.gov.au, Pages/floatsink.aspx) says the same, "heavy objects sink and light objects float regardless of their size, shape or the type of material" (seen in search; the page itself would not load).

M4 Mass and volume are confused (a balance is thought to show size). Same sources as M1; the author's choice of lure for the volume question.

M5 Dissolving is melting; a dissolved solid has vanished or turned into water. NSTA Sugar Water probe, Grade 5 (F13); the Learning in Science Project found 25% of students interviewed used "melt" and "dissolve" as if they meant the same (via the Victoria science continuum, education.vic.gov.au, Pages/meltdissolve.aspx, summarised in search; the page itself would not load); RSC: "Younger students may be satisfied with the idea that solutes 'disappear' when they dissolve": https://edu.rsc.org/resources/chemical-misconceptions-ii-mass-and-dissolving/1084.article (title line seen in search).

M6 Metal is naturally colder than wood or plastic; cold is a thing that flows from one object to another. Ohio State, Beyond Penguins (grades 3-5): "metal is naturally cooler than plastic"; "Cold is transferred from one object to another". https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2919

M7 Warm clothes (sweaters, mittens, hats, blankets) make heat of their own. Ohio State (above): "Objects that keep things warm (sweaters, mittens, blankets) are sources of heat"; NSTA's "The Mitten Problem" probe is written to find this idea. Watson and Konicek (1990), Phi Delta Kappan, "Teaching for conceptual change: Confronting children's experience": a primary class believed hats and sweaters were hot and put thermometers in them, expecting a rise (via https://mirjamglessmer.com/2015/04/22/conceptual-change-and-wooly-hats/).

M8 "Soaking up" heat or cold: NZCER flags "Metal absorbs/soaks up heat" as an incorrect explanation in the bench task (F16). The "soaks up warmth from the air" lure for the wool hat is the author's mirror of it (author's judgment).

M9 For hardness, the direction of the scratch test (which one is harder) is easily reversed by a child who has not learned the rule (author's judgment; no survey found).

## Notes and cautions

- Not every steel is magnetic (some stainless steels are not): say "iron and most steel", never "all steel".
- Not every rock sinks (pumice floats) and not every wood floats (lignum vitae sinks): the floating question names pine and a pebble only.
- A steel paper clip laid flat with great care can float on water by surface tension; the floating question does not use one.
- Dry wood is only a weak electrical insulator and graphite (pencil lead) conducts: the conductivity question's wrong choices are rubber, plastic and glass only.
- Mass and weight are introduced at this grade, but the difference is not tested (map line G5.P1; NGSS assessment boundary): no choice turns on it.
- Lanes: lab, rock, nature and body examples only; no kitchen scenes (no spoons, pans or drinks).
- Overlaps with other T1 units: the measuring unit owns "which tool measures what" (balance, graduated cylinder); the circuits unit owns wires and their plastic coats; states of matter and mixtures (evaporating salt water to get the salt back) are their own units. Here they appear only in contexts.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-25)

F23 Wool gives off a small amount of heat as it takes up water vapour (an exothermic heat of sorption), which helps a little in damp conditions; it is not a steady heater, and a hat or mitten lying in a room reads no warmer than the room (F17). So a hat that "makes heat of its own" is partly true, but one that "keeps making heat, like a heater" is wrong. https://grokipedia.com/page/Wool ("During absorption, an exothermic heat of sorption reaction releases a small amount of heat", "helping to keep the wearer warm when conditions are damp"; crimp "creates numerous insulating air pockets that trap still air")

F24 A magnet does not pull salt or sand: only iron (and nickel, cobalt, most steel) is pulled. Table salt is diamagnetic, with a tiny susceptibility (about -1.4 x 10^-5), "not magnetic" next to iron. In a primary separating task (NZCER, Level 3) only the iron filings attach to the magnet, while salt dissolves in water and sand does not and can be filtered. https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/Tables/magprop.html (sodium chloride -1.4 x 10^-5; "they are not magnetic"); https://arbs.nzcer.org.nz/resources/iron-filings-sand-and-salt ("Attaches to a magnet" for the iron filings; "Salt: Dissolves in water"; "Sand: Does not dissolve/can be filtered"); https://sites.tufts.edu/middleschoolcheme/?p=1540 ("Why does the magnet work on the iron but not the sand?")

F25 Dry, solid salt does not conduct electricity; salt conducts only when it is dissolved in water or melted, when its ions can move. Sand (silica, quartz) is an electrical insulator. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_(chemistry) ("these materials do not typically conduct electricity to any significant extent when the substance is solid"; dissolved or melted "they can conduct electricity because the ions become completely mobile"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium_chloride (in the molten state "sodium and chloride ions become mobile and can conduct electricity"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Silicon_dioxide ("Silicon dioxide (SiO2) functions as a critical insulator", for its high electrical resistivity)

F26 Salt melts only at 800.7 degrees Celsius and silica (sand) at about 1,710 degrees Celsius, so sunshine cannot melt either. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium_chloride ("a high melting point of 800.7 °C"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Silicon_dioxide (about 1710 °C; amorphous SiO2 about 1713 °C)

M10 Children think hard things sink and soft things float. Science Learning Hub, alternative conceptions: "Soft objects are more likely to float than hard objects", beside "The weight of an object determines whether it floats or sinks" and "The size of an object determines whether it floats or sinks". https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/3181-building-science-concepts-floating-and-sinking
