# How Hurricanes Form (T4, 8th grade, earth strand)

Sources were read on 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; a second source is given
for anything a child could be taught wrong. No old-bank topic was quarried.
Lane: how storms form and how we know; no named storm's human story. One
named storm (John, 1994) sets a scene for the naming question only.

## Warm water: the fuel

F1. A tropical cyclone forms only over warm sea water: Grokipedia gives "sea surface temperatures of at least 26.5°C (79.7°F)", the warm water reaching down "approximately 50–60 meters"; Wikipedia gives "The commonly accepted minimum temperature range for this to occur is 26–27 °C"; NOAA Ocean Explorer says "at least 80° Fahrenheit (27° Celsius)"; NOAA NESDIS says sea surface temperatures "usually must reach a temperature of about 80 degrees Fahrenheit or greater to fuel a hurricane". (About 26 degrees Celsius or warmer, at this grade's level.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclone ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone ; https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/hurricanes.html ; https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/news/we-may-be-past-the-peak-hurricane-season-far-over

F2. The Atlantic hurricane season runs from 1 June to 30 November, and its statistical peak day is 10 September (NOAA: "early September marks the peak"). Deep-tropics ocean temperatures "increase with each day of summer sun" (NOAA). NOAA's Mark DeMaria: if sea temperature were the only factor, the season would crest in late September or early October, "when tropical Atlantic sea surface temperatures are near their annual peak". Wind shear that "gradually fades through June and July, reaching a minimum by mid to late August" also helps set the exact date (NOAA), pulling the peak a little earlier than the sea alone would.
    https://www.noaa.gov/stories/peak-of-hurricane-season-why-now ; https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/news/we-may-be-past-the-peak-hurricane-season-far-over

F3. Water has a high specific heat capacity (4.184 J/g°C), and "This slow absorption and release of heat in oceans contrasts with the quicker response over land" (Grokipedia, Seasonal lag): the sea warms slowly through the summer and lags behind the Sun.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Seasonal_lag ; https://www.noaa.gov/stories/peak-of-hurricane-season-why-now

F4. Earth is closest to the Sun (perihelion) around 3 January and farthest (aphelion) around 4 July; the seasons come from the tilt of Earth's axis, and the distance effect is "much less significant" (Wikipedia). The sunlight reaching Earth peaks "in early January at perihelion" (Grokipedia, Solar constant). The Sun's own output changes only about 0.1 percent, over its 11-year cycle, not with Earth's seasons.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_orbit ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_constant

F5. The trade winds are "persistent easterly surface winds" that "reliably blow towards the equator"; their strength varies with the seasons, and they "serve as primary steering currents, directing developing storms westward across ocean basins" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Trade_winds

## The heat engine: condensation

F6. How a storm builds: warm seawater evaporates into the air; the moist air rises, and "as its water vapor condenses, it releases heat that keeps the air rising"; air near the surface flows in to replace it (NOAA Ocean Explorer). NASA: "Surface pressures fall when water vapor condenses in rising air and releases energy, or latent heat"; "The heat boosts the air (increases the buoyancy), so it continues rising"; "A chain reaction (or feedback mechanism) is now in progress".
    https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/hurricanes.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/hurricanes-the-greatest-storms-on-earth

F7. "Tropical cyclones operate as thermodynamic heat engines" (Grokipedia), turning heat taken from the ocean into the motion of their winds, mainly through the latent heat released when water vapour condenses; the release in updrafts "warms the mid-troposphere" (Grokipedia, Tropical cyclogenesis). In a hurricane "the subsequent condensation in eyewall clouds releases heat that fuels updrafts" (Grokipedia, Latent heat). Condensing vapour gives off about 2.5 million joules for every kilogram (Grokipedia, Latent heat). NASA Space Place: "Tropical cyclones are like giant engines that use warm, moist air as fuel."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclogenesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Latent_heat ; https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/hurricanes/en/

F8. Which changes of state give off heat: condensation releases latent heat, "warming the surrounding air", and freezing releases heat too; evaporation absorbs it ("the absorption of latent heat of vaporization cools the surface"), and melting absorbs it. Water evaporating from the sea "stores energy in water vapor, which is then transported aloft" (Grokipedia, Latent heat).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Latent_heat

F9. Over the oceans the heat carried up hidden in water vapour (the latent heat flux) is "often exceeding sensible heat flux", the heat carried as plain warmth of the air (Grokipedia, Latent heat); the Bowen ratio, sensible heat to latent heat, is "<0.1" over tropical oceans (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Latent_heat ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowen_ratio

F10. A hurricane's cloud tops are very cold: the outflow at the top of the storm is "typically around -70°C (200 K)" (Grokipedia), so its high clouds are frozen already.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclone

## Earth's spin: the turn and the equator

F11. Earth's spin (the Coriolis effect) sets the storm turning: NASA: "The storm takes the distinctive, spiraling hurricane shape because of the Coriolis Force"; NOAA Ocean Explorer: storms form "between about 5 and 20 degrees of latitude", where the water is hot enough and Earth's rotation makes them spin.
    https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/hurricanes-the-greatest-storms-on-earth ; https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/hurricanes.html

F12. Hurricanes almost never form close to the equator: the Coriolis parameter is f = 2Ω sin(latitude), "At the equator, f = 0" (Grokipedia, Coriolis force); formation needs to be "at least 5° latitude poleward of the equator" because "equatorial regions lack sufficient rotational force for cyclonic organization" (Grokipedia, Tropical cyclone); "cyclones rarely form within 5° of the equator" (Wikipedia). A rare exception, Typhoon Vamei, formed on 26 December 2001 at 1.4° N (Grokipedia, Tropical cyclogenesis).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Coriolis_force ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclone ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclogenesis

F13. Near the equator the trade winds converge and the air rises: the Intertropical Convergence Zone has warm, humid air with "persistent cloud cover, intense convection, and heavy precipitation in the form of thunderstorms", and its winds are "light and variable", the doldrums (Grokipedia, ITCZ). So the equator's air is neither dry, nor sinking, nor windy.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Intertropical_Convergence_Zone

F14. Tropical cyclones turn counter-clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern, "driven by the Coriolis effect" (Grokipedia); Earth's spin deflects moving air "to the right of the motion in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere" (Grokipedia, Coriolis force). NASA Space Place: "Storms that form north of the equator spin counterclockwise", south of it clockwise, because of "Earth's rotation on its axis". The Coriolis parameter grows from zero at the equator to its largest at the poles, so it is stronger, not weaker, farther from the equator.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Coriolis_force ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone ; https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/hurricanes/en/

## One storm, several names

F15. Hurricanes and typhoons are the same kind of storm, which meteorologists call tropical cyclones; NOAA: the only difference between a hurricane and a typhoon is the location where the storm occurs. They are called hurricanes in the North Atlantic, central North Pacific and eastern North Pacific, typhoons in the Northwest Pacific, and tropical cyclones in the South Pacific and Indian Ocean (NOAA). Grokipedia, Typhoon: the distinctions are "primarily nomenclature-based". NASA: "The scientific name for a hurricane, regardless of its location, is tropical cyclone."
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/cyclone.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Typhoon ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/hurricanes-the-greatest-storms-on-earth

F16. Hurricane John formed on 11 August 1994 in the eastern Pacific, south-southeast of Acapulco; it crossed the International Date Line in late August and was "renamed Typhoon John by the Joint Typhoon Warning Center"; it crossed back east on 8 September and regained hurricane strength; it lasted about 31 days and travelled 13,180 km (Wikipedia; Grokipedia). Its duration record was passed by Cyclone Freddy (36 days) in 2023 (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hurricane_John_(1994) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_John_(1994)

## The eye, the eyewall, the rain bands

F17. The eye has "light winds often below 15 mph (24 km/h), clear or partly cloudy skies" and "the lowest atmospheric pressure"; its clear skies come from sinking air, which "warms and dries the atmosphere, suppressing cloud formation and convection"; it has a warm core, warmer than its surroundings by about 5–10 °C (Grokipedia, Eye). NASA: the eye is "a cloud-free area of sinking air and light winds that is usually from 10 to 65 kilometers in diameter". Wikipedia: "At the center of a mature tropical cyclone, air sinks rather than rises."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Eye_(cyclone) ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/hurricanes-the-greatest-storms-on-earth ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone

F18. The eyewall is "a ring of towering thunderstorms with the storm's strongest winds" (Grokipedia, Eye); Wikipedia: the eyewall is "where the greatest wind speeds are found"; NPS: "the area of strongest winds, intense thunderstorms, and heavy rain".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Eye_(cyclone) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone ; https://www.nps.gov/articles/anatomy-of-a-hurricane.htm

F19. When the eye passes over, the calm is a trap: early sailors "misinterpreted the eye's calm as the storm's conclusion", and advisories warn "the most intense winds return shortly after the eye passes" (Grokipedia, Eye). NPS: "Then the eyewall approaches from the opposite direction, and the chaos starts all over again." (The winds circle the centre, so the far side of the eyewall blows from the opposite way.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Eye_(cyclone) ; https://www.nps.gov/articles/anatomy-of-a-hurricane.htm

F20. "Spiraling outward from the eyewall, rainbands form elongated zones of organized convection" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclone

## How we know: the first flight into an eye

F21. On 27 July 1943 Lt. Col. Joseph B. Duckworth flew a two-seat AT-6 Texan trainer from Bryan Field, Texas, into a hurricane crossing the upper Texas coast, with Lt. Ralph O'Hair; the crew passed through turbulence, heavy rain and near-darkness into an eye where they "could see the sun and the ground" (watchers.news, from the 1943 report); Grokipedia: conditions "abruptly calmed with bright cloud formations overhead and a clear view of the underlying countryside"; the eye was about 9-10 miles wide. The Monthly Weather Review of November 1943 called it "the first time, to our knowledge, that a plane has been intentionally flown through the center of a hurricane". On a second flight that day the weather officer Lt. William Jones-Burdick recorded temperatures inside the eye. Why Duckworth flew is disputed (a bet with British trainee pilots, or curiosity, as Weatherwise said in 2024), so no question rests on it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/1943_Surprise_Hurricane ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1943_Surprise_Hurricane ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Duckworth ; https://watchers.news/epicenter/1943-texas-hurricane-eye-flight-duckworth/

F22. Dropsondes are instrument packs dropped from aircraft that fall under a parachute and radio back pressure, temperature, humidity and wind (by GPS) as they fall (Grokipedia); they are "typically the only current method to measure the winds and barometric pressure through the atmosphere and down to the sea surface within the core of tropical cyclones", dropped by Hurricane Hunters into the eyewall and the eye (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dropsonde ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dropsonde

## Storm surge and the Saffir-Simpson scale

F23. Storm surge is "the abnormal rise in seawater level during a storm", above the predicted tide; "wind accounts for the majority of the surge", with the low pressure adding about 1 cm for each hectopascal; it is "the deadliest hazard from such events due to drowning and structural destruction", and "storm surge accounted for 49% of direct fatalities from Atlantic tropical cyclones between 1963 and 2012" (Grokipedia, Storm surge). NOAA: "The surge is caused primarily by a storm's winds pushing water onshore"; "The greatest threat to life actually comes from the water — in the form of storm surge." Edward Rappaport's 2014 study of US deaths in Atlantic tropical cyclones, 1963-2012 (reported by Fox Weather): storm surge 49 percent, rain floods and mudslides 27, high surf 6, wind 8, tornadoes 3. (A later NHC summary, seen only in a search result, puts freshwater flooding first for 2013-2022; hence "often" the deadliest, not always.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Storm_surge ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/stormsurge-stormtide.html ; https://www.foxweather.com/learn/water-from-hurricanes-tropical-storms-kills-more-in-us-than-wind

F24. Herbert Saffir, an engineer, and Robert Simpson, a meteorologist and then director of the US National Hurricane Center, developed the scale in the early 1970s (1971); it has five categories, Category 1 from 74 mph (119 km/h), Category 5 from 157 mph (252 km/h), rated by "maximum sustained wind speeds measured as 1-minute averages" (Grokipedia; Wikipedia; NPS). Its early versions carried storm surge and pressure ranges; in 2009 the NHC "eliminated pressure and storm surge ranges from the categories", and since 15 May 2010 it is the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale, a wind-only scale (Wikipedia). Grokipedia, Tropical cyclone: the scale "excludes considerations of storm surge heights, rainfall totals, or forward speed".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Saffir–Simpson_scale ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclone ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffir%E2%80%93Simpson_scale ; https://www.nps.gov/articles/saffir-simpson-hurricane-scale.htm

F25. A low category does not mean a small surge: Hurricane Ike was downgraded to Category 2 as it approached Texas in 2008, yet it "caused storm surge typical of a category 4 or 5 hurricane", and 112 people died (NPS). The NHC cited Ike and Katrina, whose surges were larger than the old scale's estimates, as reasons for taking surge off the scale (Wikipedia).
    https://www.nps.gov/articles/saffir-simpson-hurricane-scale.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffir%E2%80%93Simpson_scale

## Weakening: the fuel cut off

F26. Hurricanes weaken over land and over cold water because they lose their fuel: NOAA Ocean Explorer: "Once they move over cold water or over land and lose touch with the hot water that powers them", they weaken and break apart; NASA lists "the storm moving over cooler water that can't supply warm, moist tropical air" and "the storm moving over land, again cutting off the source of warm, moist air"; NASA Space Place: they "usually weaken when they hit land" because they lose their energy source. Wikipedia adds the friction of the land as a second cause over land; "A tropical cyclone can dissipate when it moves over waters significantly cooler than 26.5 °C".
    https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/hurricanes.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/hurricanes-the-greatest-storms-on-earth ; https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/hurricanes/en/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. A typhoon is a different, stronger storm than a hurricane. NOAA: the only difference is where it occurs (F15).
M2. The seasons, and the warm months, come from Earth's distance to the Sun. Earth is nearest the Sun in early January (F4).
M3. Water draining from sinks, baths and toilets turns opposite ways in the two hemispheres. Grokipedia calls this a "widespread misconception" that "persists in popular culture despite lacking scientific basis for everyday scenarios"; the swirl of a drain is set by its shape and how the water started moving (Coriolis force page). A hurricane's turn, by contrast, is set by Earth's spin every time (F14).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Coriolis_force
M4. The calm of the eye means the storm is over (F19).
M5. A hurricane's wind is its deadliest part. NOAA: the greatest threat to life comes from the water, the storm surge (F23).
M6. A storm is powered directly by the Sun's light on it. Its engine runs on heat from the sea, carried up in water vapour and set free when the vapour condenses (F6-F9); the Sun's part is warming the sea beforehand.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F27. Duckworth's flight was "the first documented intentional flight into a hurricane" (NWS Houston); by Ralph O'Hair's account, reaching the eye "was not the purpose of the flight, but really an accident". So the stem says only the first known flight made on purpose into a hurricane. The weather officer William Jones-Burdick flew back into the storm with Duckworth on the second flight; Neal Dorst (Weatherwise, 2024) credits him with the first temperature observations made while flying into a hurricane (read through the NOAA repository record and a search summary), while Grokipedia says he recorded winds and pressure in the eye. The context says only that he recorded temperatures inside the storm.
    https://www.weather.gov/hgx/projects_1943surprisehurricane ; https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/56748 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/1943_Surprise_Hurricane

F28. A hurricane cools the sea beneath it: "The storm's own winds drive vertical mixing and Ekman pumping, entraining colder subsurface water to the surface", "creating a cold wake that can drop SSTs by 2–5°C in the core region", "particularly for slow-moving or looping systems"; mixing and upwelling can lower the surface temperature "by 2-5°C within hours under high winds"; the forecasters' intensity scheme (SHIPS) uses the ocean's heat content as well as its surface temperature (Grokipedia). NASA (Scott Braun): "If the hurricane mixes the [top] layer of the ocean, it taps into colder water below"; "When the storm cools the surface, it reduces the intensity of the weather system."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclone ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/hurricane-nicoles-cold-water-wake-88961/

F29. A tropical cyclone is "a warm-core, non-frontal synoptic-scale cyclone, originating over tropical or subtropical waters" (the WMO definition, as given by UNDRR): it draws in no polar air.
    https://www.undrr.org/understanding-disaster-risk/terminology/hips/mh0309

F30. The Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale's own descriptions warn of the wind's debris: Category 1, "People, livestock, and pets struck by flying or falling debris could be injured or killed", with the risk rising in each higher category; and the scale "does not address storm surge, rainfall-induced floods, and tornadoes" (NHC scale table, seen in a search result). Wikipedia: the scale "excludes flood ranges, storm surge estimations, rainfall, and location".
    https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/pdf/sshws.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffir%E2%80%93Simpson_scale

F31. Around a low, air flows in and rises, giving "cloud formation, precipitation, and unsettled weather conditions" (Grokipedia, Low-pressure area). The eye has the storm's lowest pressure yet a clear sky, because its air sinks (F17): low pressure by itself does not clear the sky.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Low-pressure_area

F32. Ice from a hurricane's frozen cloud tops does not reach the sea: "The freezing level is very high in a hurricane", often near 500 millibars (about 5.8 km up), and the storm's warm core "will usually melt hail before it reaches the ground" (Jeff Haby, The Weather Prediction). The cloud tops themselves are near -70 degrees Celsius (F10).
    https://theweatherprediction.com/habyhints2/403/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_cyclone
