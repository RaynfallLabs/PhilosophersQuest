## What a virus is, and whether it is alive

F1. A virus is not a cell. It is genetic material (a nucleic acid, DNA or RNA but never both) wrapped in a protective coat of protein called the capsid; some viruses also carry an outer envelope of membrane taken from the host cell. Grokipedia: "an acellular infectious agent composed of a nucleic acid genome" in "a protective protein capsid".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Virus ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/6-1-viruses ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/21-1-viral-evolution-morphology-and-classification

F2. A virus copies itself only inside a living host cell, using the cell's own machinery: viruses are "obligate intracellular parasites that depend entirely on host cells for replication" (Grokipedia); virions "lack ribosomes, enzymes for energy production" (Grokipedia); "Viruses do not have their own metabolism and require a host cell to make new products. They therefore cannot naturally reproduce outside a host cell" (Wikipedia). OpenStax: viruses "use the host's cellular machinery to replicate."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Virus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/14-4-mechanisms-of-other-antimicrobial-drugs

F3. Whether a virus is alive is still argued. Grokipedia calls the question "unresolved" and says most biologists classify viruses as non-living, though debate continues; Wikipedia: "Scientific opinions differ on whether viruses are a form of life," and viruses have been called "organisms at the edge of life."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Virus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus

F4. Size: most virions are about 20 to 250 nanometres across (OpenStax Biology), or 20 to 300 nm (Wikipedia), "one-hundredth the size of most bacteria" (Wikipedia); OpenStax Microbiology gives 20 nm up to 900 nm for large viruses, "much smaller than prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells." The tobacco mosaic virus is a rod about 300 nm long and 18 nm wide.
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/21-1-viral-evolution-morphology-and-classification ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/6-1-viruses ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_mosaic_virus

F5. Viruses evolve: their genes change over time by mutation and natural selection; RNA viruses copy their genes with many errors, and influenza's slow drift is why the flu shot is reformulated each year (Grokipedia). Wikipedia treats viral evolution as a field of evolutionary biology.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Viral_evolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_evolution

F6. A bacterium, by contrast, is one whole living cell, a prokaryote: no nucleus, its DNA loose in the cytoplasm, but with its own cell membrane, ribosomes and (in most kinds) a cell wall. (Taught at T2 in cells-the-building-blocks-of-life; used here only as background and as the honest mix-up in a wrong choice.)
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-2-prokaryotic-cells ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Prokaryote

## How we learned viruses exist and are tiny (the tobacco mosaic story)

F7. Charles Chamberland's porcelain filter (1884) has pores smaller than bacteria and was used to strip bacteria from water; OpenStax: its pores of about 0.1 micrometre remove "all bacteria" of 0.2 micrometre and up. Dissolved poisons pass through it: Wikipedia says its use showed that diphtheria and tetanus toxins "could still cause illness even after filtration."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_virology ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/6-1-viruses ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamberland_filter

F8. Dmitri Ivanovsky, a Russian botanist, pressed sap from tobacco plants with mosaic disease (which mottles the leaves) through porcelain Chamberland filters "that retained bacteria" in 1892, and the filtered sap still gave the disease to healthy plants. He reported it to the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences in February 1892.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dmitri_Ivanovsky ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Virus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_mosaic_virus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus

F9. Ivanovsky did not conclude that he had found a new kind of agent; he still blamed bacteria. Wikipedia: he "suggested the infection might be caused by a toxin produced by bacteria, but did not pursue the idea"; EBSCO: "Ivanovski's conclusion was that a toxin was probably associated with the disease," following Behring's diphtheria toxin; OpenStax: he concluded it "must be an extremely small bacterium or bacterial spore"; Grokipedia: a "soluble toxin secreted by bacteria" or bacteria too small to culture. On its own, the filter result showed only that the cause passed where bacteria could not; it could not tell a poison from a living agent.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_virology ; https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/beijerinck-discovers-viruses ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/6-1-viruses ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dmitri_Ivanovsky ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contagium_vivum_fluidum

F10. Martinus Beijerinck, a Dutch microbiologist, repeated the filter experiments in 1898 and found the filtered sap still infectious. He passed the sap from plant to plant: "By passing the sap from plant to plant, Beijerinck demonstrated that it was capable of reproduction, unlike a toxin, which would have lost viability as it became increasingly diluted" (EBSCO). Wikipedia: he "disproved Ivanovsky's toxin theory by demonstrating infection in series" and concluded "the agent can replicate and multiply in living plants."
    https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/beijerinck-discovers-viruses ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contagium_vivum_fluidum ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martinus_Beijerinck ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_mosaic_virus

F11. Beijerinck could not grow the agent on any nutrient medium; it "required living tissue in which to reproduce" and preferred young, growing leaves (EBSCO); it "multiplied only in cells that were dividing" (Wikipedia, History of virology); it "multiplied only within the living protoplasm of host cells" (Grokipedia). It stayed active for months even when dried (EBSCO). He called it a contagium vivum fluidum, "contagious living fluid", and named it a virus to mark it as not a bacterium.
    https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/beijerinck-discovers-viruses ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_virology ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Martinus_Beijerinck ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martinus_Beijerinck

F12. Wendell Stanley purified tobacco mosaic virus into needle-shaped crystals in 1935 (Grokipedia; Wikipedia), and the crystals "retained full infectivity when redissolved and applied to healthy plants" (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: TMV "remains active even after crystallization"). Stanley concluded in 1935 that it was pure protein; analyses in 1936-1937 (with Bawden and Pirie among the workers) found about 6 percent RNA, so it is a nucleoprotein, and Wikipedia lists the pure-protein view among his conclusions later shown wrong. He shared the 1946 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The crystals "blurred the line between living organisms and chemical molecules" (Grokipedia). (A Hamburg university page dates true crystals to 1937, so the stem avoids leaning on the year.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wendell_Meredith_Stanley ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendell_Meredith_Stanley ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_mosaic_virus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_virology

F13. The first electron-microscope pictures of tobacco mosaic virus were made in 1939 (Kausche, Pfankuch and Ruska); the electron microscope itself was built in 1931.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_mosaic_virus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Virus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_virology

## Antibiotics: bacteria, not viruses

F14. Antibiotics kill bacteria (bactericidal) or stop them growing and multiplying (bacteriostatic). They work by "selective toxicity", attacking "structures and processes unique to bacteria": the peptidoglycan cell wall, and the bacterial ribosomes (70S, unlike the 80S ribosomes of human cells).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antibiotic ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-2-prokaryotic-cells

F15. Penicillin blocks the cross-linking that holds a bacterium's peptidoglycan wall together; it acts most on growing, dividing cells, which then burst (osmotic lysis).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Penicillin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antibiotic

F16. Antibiotics "are ineffective against viral illnesses like the common cold or influenza" (Grokipedia, Antibiotic). Why: a virus has none of the bacterial parts the drugs attack. It has no cell wall (its whole structure is nucleic acid, a protein coat, a few viral enzymes and sometimes a lipid envelope, OpenStax) and no ribosomes or energy-making enzymes of its own (F2); it copies itself with the host cell's own machinery (F2). The cold is caused by more than 200 respiratory viruses, rhinoviruses the most common; Cochrane: antibiotics "provide no benefit for typical cold symptoms and increase adverse effects" (Grokipedia, Common cold).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antibiotic ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Common_cold ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/14-4-mechanisms-of-other-antimicrobial-drugs ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Virus

F17. Unneeded prescriptions for viral illnesses add to resistance: Grokipedia names "Overuse in human medicine, including unnecessary prescriptions for viral infections" among the drivers of resistance, and calls resistance "primarily driven by the misuse and overuse of antibiotics in humans, animals, and plants." Grokipedia also says up to 70 percent of antibiotics went to farm animals in the 2010s, much of it once for growth promotion, and that the European Union banned antibiotic growth promoters in animal feed from 1 January 2006.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antibiotic ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antimicrobial_resistance ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Common_cold

## Fleming's dish and the forgotten half

F18. Alexander Fleming came back from holiday to his laboratory at St Mary's Hospital, London, on 3 September 1928 (Wikipedia; Grokipedia gives both 3 and 28 September, so the stems say "September 1928"). A dish of Staphylococcus bacteria had been contaminated with a blue-green mould, and "the colonies of staphylococci immediately surrounding the fungus had been destroyed" (Wikipedia); Grokipedia: a "clear zone of inhibition" around the mould, later identified as Penicillium notatum.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Fleming ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_penicillin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alexander_Fleming

F19. Fleming grew the mould in broth and filtered it, giving "a clear, straw-colored liquid termed 'mold juice'" (Grokipedia) that strongly inhibited staphylococci, streptococci and pneumococci; the active agent was in the liquid, not the mould itself (Grokipedia, Fleming). He named it penicillin on 7 March 1929, "to avoid the repetition of the rather cumbersome phrase 'mould broth filtrate'" (Wikipedia), and published in the British Journal of Experimental Pathology in June 1929 (paper: "On the Antibacterial Action of Cultures of a Penicillium, with Special Reference to their Use in the Isolation of B. influenzae").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_penicillin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alexander_Fleming ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2048009/

F20. Fleming found the crude filtrate harmless when injected: "no toxicity when the crude mold juice filtrate was injected into mice and rabbits" and in vitro tests "confirmed no toxicity to leukocytes" (Grokipedia, Discovery); "no adverse effects beyond those of plain broth" in rabbits (Grokipedia, Fleming). His 1929 summary (point 7) says penicillin "is non-toxic to animals in enormous doses" and does not interfere with "leucocytic function" more than ordinary broth (seen only as quoted in search results from the paper's reprint in the Bulletin of the World Health Organization, 2001; the reprint page itself refused the fetch, so the questions lean on Grokipedia and Wikipedia here). Wikipedia adds that the Oxford worker Jennings found it "had no effect on white blood cells."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_penicillin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alexander_Fleming ; https://scielosp.org/article/bwho/2001.v79n8/780-790 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin

F21. Why penicillin then sat unused for about a decade: it was unstable and could not be purified. Grokipedia: "Penicillin degraded rapidly, making it difficult to isolate and purify"; Harold Raistrick's team in 1932 got only a crude extract and could go no further "due to the substance's extreme instability." Wikipedia: Fleming's "problem was the difficulty of producing penicillin in large amounts, and moreover, isolation of the main compound," and "penicillin languished largely forgotten in the 1930s." Fleming's own assistants, Craddock and Ridley, "could not isolate penicillin" (Wikipedia). Fleming also "was more concerned with its application for bacterial isolation" than with medicine (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_penicillin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alexander_Fleming ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Fleming ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin

F22. The Oxford team: Ernst Chain drew Howard Florey's attention to Fleming's paper, Florey won funding in 1939, and the team was the biochemist Chain, the pathologist Florey and the biochemist Norman Heatley. Heatley proposed moving the drug from a solvent "back into water by changing its acidity," which purified it, and grew the mould in bedpan-like vessels (Wikipedia, Heatley); Science History Institute: Heatley improved the "excruciatingly inefficient" first process.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_penicillin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Heatley ; https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/howard-walter-florey-and-ernst-boris-chain/

F23. The mouse test of Saturday 25 May 1940: eight mice were given a lethal dose of streptococci and four of them were then treated with penicillin. Wikipedia: "By 3:30 am on Sunday all four of the untreated mice were dead," while the treated mice survived; Heatley's diary: "I stayed at the lab until 3:45 a.m., by which time all four control animals were dead"; Science History Institute: "Sixteen-and-a-half hours later the four mice that had received penicillin were alive," and testing penicillin in mice was "the crucial step that Fleming had not taken." The results were published in The Lancet of 24 August 1940.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_penicillin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Heatley ; https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/howard-walter-florey-and-ernst-boris-chain/

F24. Albert Alexander, a policeman, was one of the first patients: "Within a day of being given penicillin, Alexander started to recover"; penicillin was recovered from his urine "but it was not enough"; he relapsed in early March and died on 15 March 1941 (Wikipedia; Grokipedia). The drug worked; there was too little of it.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_penicillin

F25. The 1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was "shared equally among Sir Alexander Fleming, Ernst Boris Chain, and Sir Howard Walter Florey" (Grokipedia) "for the discovery of penicillin and its curative effect in various infectious diseases" (Wikipedia). The rule of at most three winners "excluded key contributors such as Norman Heatley" (Grokipedia); Oxford gave Heatley an honorary Doctorate of Medicine in 1990, "the first given to a non-medic in Oxford's 800-year history" (Wikipedia). The press made Fleming alone famous, which "led to the misconception that he was entirely responsible"; Fleming himself called this "the Fleming myth" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_penicillin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Fleming ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Heatley

## Resistance

F26. Bacteria that destroy penicillin were known before it was ever given to a patient: Edward Abraham and Ernst Chain first isolated penicillinase, a bacterial enzyme that destroys penicillin, in 1940, from E. coli. Resistance in Staphylococcus aureus was documented from 1942, and penicillin-destroying staphylococci were isolated as early as 1944.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta-lactamase ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_penicillin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antimicrobial_resistance

F27. Fleming's warning in his Nobel lecture of 1945: "The time may come when penicillin can be bought by anyone in the shops. Then there is the danger that the ignorant man may easily underdose himself and by exposing his microbes to non-lethal quantities of the drug make them resistant" (Wikipedia quotes it in parts).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Fleming ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alexander_Fleming

F28. Resistance genes spread not only down the generations but sideways, between living bacteria, even of different species (horizontal gene transfer). Wikipedia: "Horizontal gene transfer is the primary mechanism for the spread of antibiotic resistance in bacteria," and "Genes responsible for antibiotic resistance in one species of bacteria can be transferred to another species of bacteria." Grokipedia: conjugation, the passing of genes "through direct cell-to-cell contact," often of plasmids (small extra loops of DNA), is "the predominant mechanism"; it spreads resistance "across species and genera"; resistance mutations arise at only about one in a million to one in a billion per generation and "contribute less to rapid, widespread resistance compared to HGT." (Kept general: no mechanism detail beyond "cell to cell".)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antimicrobial_resistance ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Bacterial_conjugation

F29. A drug does not make the resistance; it favours what is already there. Grokipedia: "Spontaneous mutations or horizontal gene transfer introduce genetic variation in bacterial populations," and antibiotic exposure then favours the resistant cells. Antibiotics are mostly natural products: soil microbes such as Streptomyces make them "to suppress rival microbes," and "Antimicrobial resistance predates human antibiotic use, arising naturally in microbial ecosystems."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antimicrobial_resistance ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antibiotic

F30. Vanessa D'Costa, Christine King, Gerard Wright and colleagues (Nature 477, 2011, "Antibiotic resistance is ancient") studied DNA from 30,000-year-old permafrost in Beringia, at sites such as Dawson City in Yukon, Canada (Scientific American; the National Geographic piece was summarised as "Alaskan", so the stem says only "the far north"). They found genes giving resistance to beta-lactam, tetracycline and glycopeptide (vancomycin) antibiotics; they rebuilt the vancomycin-resistance genes and found they "have the same shape, and do the same thing, as their modern counterparts" (National Geographic). To show the DNA was truly ancient, they found mammoth DNA of the right age and "nothing from species that are common today, like elk, moose or spruce," worked in clean rooms, and coated their drill and cores with glowing bacteria to check nothing from outside reached the inside. The authors wrote that their results disprove the idea that resistance is a modern phenomenon. National Geographic adds that this is no reason to relax: heavy use in medicine and farming still favours these old genes.
    https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2011Natur.477..457D/abstract ; https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/bacteria-resisting-antibiotics-for-at-least-30000-bc ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/lab-rat/ancient-resistance-8211-ice-age-bacteria-that-could-fight-off-antibiotics/ ; https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/178039

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- Antibiotics cure colds and flu: 64 percent of about 10,000 people in 12 countries believed it in the World Health Organization's survey of November 2015 (F16).
  https://www.who.int/news/item/16-11-2015-who-multi-country-survey-reveals-widespread-public-misunderstanding-about-antibiotic-resistance
- Resistance happens when the patient's body becomes resistant: 76 percent in the same survey (F28, F29: the bacteria change, not the body).
- The drug "teaches" each bacterium to resist, or makes it change its genes to suit the drug (F29, F30: the genes were there before any medicine).
- A virus is a tiny bacterium, or a cell with no nucleus (F1, F6: a virus is not a cell at all).
- A virus is pure protein, the view Stanley himself held in 1935 (F12).
- Viruses never change (F5: they evolve, and fast).
- The filter result alone proved the cause was alive (F9, F10: it could not tell a poison from a living agent until Beijerinck's plant-to-plant passage).
- Fleming made penicillin into a medicine (F21-F25: the Oxford team did; Fleming's own name for the misconception was "the Fleming myth").
- Penicillin harmed white blood cells or animals (F20: it did not).

## Added at revision (2026-11-27)

F31. Size is not what makes a thing alive or not: the giant virus Mimivirus is about 750 nm across, "visible under light microscopy and larger than many bacteria", and giant viruses surpass "many bacterial cells, such as Mycoplasma species at around 200-300 nm" (Grokipedia, Giant virus); giant viruses still multiply inside host cells (amoebae). Used for the size lure in the Stanley question.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Giant_virus

F32. No patent held penicillin back in the 1930s. Fleming "continued to send samples of the mould to researchers" (Wikipedia, History of penicillin; Roger Reid at Johns Hopkins had a sample from Fleming in 1935), and Fleming said: "I found penicillin and have given it free for the benefit of humanity." Chain later wanted a patent, but Florey objected; Andrew Moyer patented methods for producing and isolating penicillin in 1945 (Wikipedia). The Norwegian encyclopedia (SNL): it was unclear whether penicillin, a naturally produced substance, could be patented, and in the USA the production process was patented in the 1940s.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin ; https://snl.no/Alexander_Fleming

F33. How shared genes are recognised: detection of horizontal gene transfer relies on "phylogenetic incongruences, gene composition anomalies, and comparative genomics", including "high-identity matches to distantly related taxa" (Grokipedia, Horizontal gene transfer); HGT "drives the acquisition of antibiotic resistance genes". In multicellular eukaryotes "the rate is generally lower" (with rare exceptions such as bdelloid rotifers), hence "as a rule" for animals in the context.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Horizontal_gene_transfer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer

F34. Resistance is older than our drugs because microbes make antibiotics: "Antimicrobial resistance predates human antibiotic use"; "Soil actinomycetes, such as species of Streptomyces, produce antimicrobial compounds to suppress rival microbes"; the environmental resistome includes "ancient ARGs in permafrost dating back 30,000 years" (Grokipedia, Antimicrobial resistance). Fact-check wording (fc2f10c230): people used plant remedies in prehistory, so the stem says "long before anyone made an antibiotic drug" and the context "30,000 years before the first antibiotic drug", not "any medicine".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antimicrobial_resistance ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antibiotic ; https://www.sciencenews.org/article/ancient-dental-plaque-tells-tales-neandertal-diet-and-disease
