## The atom's parts: what is where

F1. An atom is made of positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons, packed together in the nucleus, and a cloud of negatively charged electrons around it. (Grokipedia: "composed of positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons", "surrounded by a cloud of negatively charged electrons".)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atom ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Neutron ; https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/21-1-nuclear-structure-and-stability

F2. The nucleus holds over 99.9 percent of the atom's mass.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atom ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atomic_nucleus

F3. An electron has about 1/1836 of a proton's mass. A neutron's mass is slightly greater than a proton's.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atom ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electron ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Neutron

F4. The nucleus is tiny next to the atom: its radius is less than 1/10,000 of the atom's (a nucleus is about 10^-15 m across, an atom about 10^-10 m), so the atom is mostly empty space.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rutherford_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atom

F5. In the modern picture the electrons have no fixed paths: they occupy "probabilistic orbitals", a cloud of probability, around the nucleus. Bohr's orbits of 1913 were replaced by the quantum model in the 1920s; the Bohr model is now called obsolete but is still used for teaching. The modern atom keeps "the distinct quantized energy levels of its electrons" (Grokipedia, Emission spectrum), and electrons still occupy energy levels around the nucleus (Grokipedia, Electron).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atom ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Bohr_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Emission_spectrum ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electron

## What decides what

F6. The number of protons in the nucleus, the atomic number, identifies the element (Grokipedia: "uniquely identifies the element").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atom ; https://www.shmoop.com/teks/science/middle-school/8th-grade/TEKS-112-20-b-5/TEKS-112-20-b-5-B (TEKS 2017 8.5B: protons determine an element's identity)

F7. Lead has atomic number 82; its most common isotope, lead-208, has 126 neutrons. Gold has atomic number 79; its only stable isotope, gold-197, has 79 protons and 118 neutrons. Gold is the most malleable metal and one of the softest pure metals (Mohs hardness 2.5).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lead ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gold

F8. Chrysopoeia was the alchemists' goal of turning base metals such as lead into gold; it was "never achieved through chemical means". Chemical reactions, classically, change "only ... the positions of electrons", "with no change to the nuclei (no change to the elements present)"; turning one element into another (transmutation) takes a nuclear reaction, which "differs fundamentally from chemical reactions, which involve electron rearrangements without affecting nuclear identity".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chrysopoeia ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nuclear_transmutation

F9. A neutral atom has zero net charge because its electrons balance its protons exactly; an atom that loses or gains electrons is charged but is still the same element (its protons are unchanged).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atom ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium (sodium loses its valence electron to form the Na+ ion)

F10. An alpha particle is two protons and two neutrons, identical to a helium-4 nucleus, with a charge of +2; Rutherford and Royds showed in 1909 that alpha particles are helium atoms stripped of two electrons. An alpha particle has about 7,300 times the mass of an electron. Radium gives off alpha particles.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alpha_particle ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger%E2%80%93Marsden_experiments

F11. Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost shell, and they "determine an element's reactivity and chemical properties"; they are the electrons that take part in chemical reactions (atoms "interact via their valence electrons").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Valence_electron ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atom ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction ; https://www.shmoop.com/teks/science/middle-school/8th-grade/TEKS-112-20-b-5/TEKS-112-20-b-5-B (TEKS 2017 8.5B: valence electrons determine chemical properties, including reactivity)

F12. Sodium (atomic number 11, electrons [Ne]3s1, i.e. shells of 2, 8 and 1) and potassium (atomic number 19, [Ar]4s1, i.e. 2, 8, 8 and 1) each have one valence electron, easily lost. Both are soft, silvery-white metals; sodium reacts vigorously with water and potassium violently, both giving hydrogen gas and a hydroxide. Sodium's only stable isotope is sodium-23 (12 neutrons); potassium is 93 percent potassium-39 (20 neutrons). The alkali metals each have one valence electron, "making them highly reactive".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Potassium ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alkali_metal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Valence_electron

## J. J. Thomson: the electron and his model

F13. Cathode rays are a stream of electrons given off by the cathode (the negative electrode) of a glass tube with most of the air pumped out. Before 1897 their nature was argued over: William Crookes and Arthur Schuster thought they were "radiant matter, that is, electrically charged atoms"; Eilhard Wiedemann, Heinrich Hertz and Eugen Goldstein believed they were "aether waves". Hertz found no bending in an electric field, but his tube held too much gas.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cathode_ray ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode_ray

F14. J. J. Thomson, at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge in 1897, bent cathode rays with an electric field (once the vacuum was good) and with a magnetic field. The rays bent toward the positive plate, "as if they were negatively electrified". The ratio of mass to charge "came out the same whatever the material of the electrode" and was "independent of the nature of the gas". His ratio was about a thousandth of the hydrogen ion's (10^-7 against 10^-4); by modern measure the particles are about 1,800 times lighter than a hydrogen atom, the lightest atom. He called them corpuscles, later named electrons, and proposed them as the stuff from which all the chemical elements are built; the discovery challenged the idea that atoms cannot be divided.
    J. J. Thomson, "Cathode Rays", Philosophical Magazine 1897: https://web.lemoyne.edu/giunta/thomson1897.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/J._J._Thomson ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cathode_ray ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode_ray

F15. Thomson's model of 1904: "a sphere of uniform positive electrification" enclosing the negative corpuscles, the positive charge exactly cancelling the negative so the atom is neutral. The nickname "plum pudding" came later; Thomson did not use it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Plum_pudding_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/J._J._Thomson

F16. Because its positive charge was spread thin, Thomson's model predicted that alpha particles fired through a gold foil would be deflected only slightly, a few degrees at most (Wikipedia: "all the alpha particles should have gone straight through").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Plum_pudding_model ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger%E2%80%93Marsden_experiments

## The gold foil: Geiger, Marsden and Rutherford

F17. Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden did the scattering experiments under Ernest Rutherford's direction at the University of Manchester, 1908-1913, the large-angle bounces first in 1909. Marsden was an undergraduate studying under Geiger (AIP: a 19-year-old third-year honours physics student when he joined in 1908). Marsden did most of the hands-on measurements.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Geiger%E2%80%93Marsden_experiments ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger%E2%80%93Marsden_experiments ; https://history.aip.org/exhibits/rutherford/sections/alpha-particles-atom.html

F18. They fired alpha particles from radium at thin metal foils, mostly gold, chosen because gold is the most malleable metal and can be made very thin. The 1909 paper speaks of "a layer of 6 x 10-5 cm. of gold"; a gold foil 1 micrometre thick is about 2,800 atoms thick, so the foils were many atoms deep. Each alpha particle striking a zinc sulfide screen made a faint flash (a scintillation), counted by eye through a microscope in a darkened room.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger%E2%80%93Marsden_experiments ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Geiger%E2%80%93Marsden_experiments ; https://history.aip.org/exhibits/rutherford/sections/alpha-particles-atom.html ; https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/rutsca.html

F19. What they saw: most alpha particles passed through the foil with little or no deflection; a small fraction were scattered through large angles, and some were reflected back toward the source. (Figures: about 1 in 8,000 reflected from thick platinum in 1909; roughly 1 in 20,000 scattered past 90 degrees by thin gold.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Geiger%E2%80%93Marsden_experiments ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rutherford_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plum_pudding_model

F20. Rutherford's paper of 1911 concluded that most of the atom's mass and charge sit in a very small central body, with the rest of the atom mostly empty space; most alpha particles pass far from it, and the rare close encounters cause the large deflections. His paper said the calculations held "whether the central charge were positive or negative"; the nucleus is in fact positive, its charge carried by its protons (F1).
    https://history.aip.org/exhibits/rutherford/sections/alpha-particles-atom.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rutherford_model ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger%E2%80%93Marsden_experiments

F21. Electrons could not turn an alpha particle back: an alpha particle has about 7,300 times an electron's mass (F10), and the electrons' "combined mass is low", so "they have a negligible effect on the alpha particle".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger%E2%80%93Marsden_experiments ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alpha_particle

F22. In a lecture at Cambridge on 15 October 1936 Rutherford called the back-scattering "quite the most incredible event that has ever happened to me in my life", "almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger%E2%80%93Marsden_experiments ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rutherford_model ; https://history.aip.org/exhibits/rutherford/sections/alpha-particles-atom.html

## Niels Bohr: energy levels

F23. Niels Bohr moved to Manchester in early 1912 to work with Rutherford. In his model of 1913 the electrons occupy only certain stationary states (energy levels); an electron in one does not radiate, and it gives off or takes in light (a photon) only when it moves between levels, the photon's energy matching the difference. This explained hydrogen's emission spectrum. Under classical physics an orbiting electron would radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus (in about 10^-8 seconds). Bohr won the 1922 Nobel Prize in Physics.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Bohr_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Niels_Bohr

F24. Hydrogen gas at low pressure carrying an electric current gives off light in distinct lines (a line spectrum), not a smooth continuous band; its visible lines are red (656 nm), cyan (486 nm), blue (434 nm) and violet (410 nm). Each element's line pattern is its own.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Emission_spectrum ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Balmer_series

## James Chadwick: the neutron

F25. Rutherford proposed in 1920 a neutral particle of about the proton's mass inside the nucleus, which he expected to be hard to detect. Until 1932 the nucleus was thought to be made of protons and "nuclear electrons" (nitrogen-14 as 14 protons and 7 electrons).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_the_neutron ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_the_neutron

F26. Walther Bothe and Herbert Becker (1930) found that beryllium struck by alpha particles from polonium gives off "an unusually penetrating radiation", thought to be gamma rays because electric fields did not affect it. Irène Joliot-Curie and Frédéric Joliot (1932) found that it knocked high-energy protons out of paraffin wax, and read it as gamma rays.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_the_neutron ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_the_neutron ; https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Chadwick

F27. James Chadwick, at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge under Rutherford, repeated the work in February 1932, aiming the radiation at paraffin and at various gases. He argued that gamma rays could not give protons such a kick without impossibly high energies, and his recoil measurements showed a neutral particle with about the proton's mass. He published "Possible Existence of a Neutron" in Nature (1932) and won the 1935 Nobel Prize in Physics alone.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Chadwick ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_the_neutron ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_the_neutron

F28. A neutron has zero electric charge, so it leaves no ionization track and passes through matter, which made it hard to detect.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Neutron ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_the_neutron

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Atoms are solid spheres, "rather than mostly empty space". (RSC)
M2. Electrons orbit the nucleus like planets round the Sun ("miniature solar systems"). (RSC)
M3. Electron shells are rigid physical structures. (RSC)
M4. Atomic number is mixed up with atomic mass (and so, often, with the count of all the particles). (RSC)
M5. Electrons live in the nucleus: a real belief of physicists until 1932 (F25), and a common slip in class.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-25; sources read on that date)

F29. In 1897 Thomson's mass-to-charge ratio for the cathode-ray particles was over a thousand times lower than a hydrogen ion's, "suggesting either that the particles were very light and/or very highly charged" (Wikipedia); the ratio alone did not settle the mass. "Later in 1899 he measured the charge of the electron" (Wikipedia; Giunta: "In 1899, he measured the charge of the particles"). With the charge about the hydrogen ion's, the small ratio had to mean a small mass: in his Nobel lecture Thomson put the corpuscle's mass at "only about 1/1,700 of that of the hydrogen atom". Grokipedia gives the 1897 deflection toward the positive plate as showing the rays were negatively charged and calls the discovery "the first evidence of subatomic structure"; Wikipedia says he concluded "that atoms were divisible, and that the corpuscles were their building blocks".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/J._J._Thomson ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson ; https://web.lemoyne.edu/GIUNTA/EA/THOMSONann.HTML

F30. In Bohr's model the electron "is assumed to orbit the proton in a circular path" (it moves; it does not sit still), held by the "electrostatic attraction between the electron and proton" (the electric pull, not gravity). Classical physics said an orbiting electron "would continuously radiate electromagnetic energy" (electromagnetic radiation, i.e. light in the physicist's broad sense) and spiral inward in about 10^-8 seconds. Bohr's electrons occupy "discrete energy levels" n = 1, 2, 3 ... with no upper limit, and radiation is emitted or absorbed "only during transitions between stationary states". Bohr's 1913 paper also arranged the electrons of many-electron atoms in rings ("a ring of n electrons ...").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Bohr_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electron ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_model

F31. An alpha particle has a mass of "approximately 4 atomic mass units" and a charge of "+2 elementary charges"; a gold atom has an atomic mass of 196.97 u (gold-197 is 100 percent of natural gold), so a gold atom has nearly 50 times an alpha particle's mass. In the gold-foil experiment "most particles passed straight through", and the atom has "a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space" (Grokipedia, Alpha particle).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alpha_particle ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gold

M6. "A common misconception is that atoms share the properties of the bulk material" (RSC; "individual copper atoms have no colour"). Gold the metal is soft (F7), but softness is not a property of a single gold atom.
    https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/elements-and-compounds/3009350.article
