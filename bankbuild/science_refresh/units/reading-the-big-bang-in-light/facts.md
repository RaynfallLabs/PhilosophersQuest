## What a red shift measures

F1. Cosmological red shift is "commonly attributed to stretching of the wavelengths of photons due to the stretching of space"; "all wavelengths of the light are stretched by the expansion of space" (Wikipedia, Harrison). Grokipedia: "the observed wavelength is stretched by the factor by which the universe has expanded between emission and observation", with 1 + z = a(now)/a(then), where a is the scale factor, which "parameterizes the relative size of the universe at different times". So light whose every wave arrives twice as long as it set out (z = 1) left when the distances between galaxies were half what they are now.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Redshift ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redshift ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Expansion_of_the_universe

F2. The expansion is "the metric expansion of space itself", stretching space "rather than objects moving through a fixed space". Distant galaxies can recede faster than light, and "this superluminal recession does not violate special relativity", because no object or information travels faster than light through local space. Bound systems (atoms, planets, stars, galaxies) do not expand: gravity and electromagnetism "overpower the expansion". Wikipedia adds that the cosmological red shift "is very different from how Doppler redshift depends upon local velocity", though it "can be interpreted as an accumulation of infinitesimal Doppler shifts" (so no choice here pits stretching against Doppler).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Expansion_of_the_universe ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redshift

F3. Dust mimics a red shift only in colour: "Dust grains scatter and absorb blue light more efficiently than red light", which "tilts the continuum spectrum redward without shifting emission lines"; a true red shift moves the lines themselves, which is how astronomers measure it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Redshift

F4. The speed of light in empty space is "an invariant universal constant", fixed at 299,792,458 metres a second, "the same for all inertial observers", and "no particle or signal can exceed it". MISCONCEPTION: that red-shifted light has slowed down or "got tired".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_light

## The glow foretold, then found

F5. Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman first predicted the background glow in 1948, "in a correction they prepared for a paper by Alpher's PhD advisor George Gamow" (Wikipedia, CMB); they reasoned that the expansion would stretch the early universe's radiation into microwaves, "down to a temperature of about 5 K". Grokipedia: they "explicitly calculated its current temperature as approximately 5 K in their 1948 and 1949 publications", building on Gamow's big bang nucleosynthesis work. Their papers "failed to elicit any immediate response" (Linda Hall Library); their predictions were "more or less forgotten" until the early 1960s (Wikipedia, Alpher). Honest note: two years later they re-estimated the figure as 28 K (Wikipedia, Alpher), so only the 1948 figure is used.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_microwave_background ; https://lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/ralph-alpher ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Asher_Alpher ; https://physicsworld.com/a/ralph-alpher-1921-2007/

F6. Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson found the glow at Bell Labs in 1964 with the Holmdel horn, built "primarily for receiving signals from NASA's Project Echo passive communications satellites"; they were using it "to measure radio emissions from the Milky Way galaxy", and the find was "made without prior intent to search for cosmological signals" (Grokipedia). After subtracting the air and the instrument they had "3.5 K residual background noise" (Wikipedia). Robert Dicke, James Peebles and David Wilkinson at Princeton, who were preparing their own search, explained it; Penzias and Wilson shared the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics. Today's value is 2.72548 K.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Holmdel_Horn_Antenna ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_cosmic_microwave_background_radiation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_microwave_background ; https://physicsworld.com/a/ralph-alpher-1921-2007/

F7. The expansion itself was known long before the glow: Edwin Hubble's 1929 paper, with Milton Humason, showed galaxies "receding from Earth at speeds proportional to their distance" (T4 ground, used here only to make a wrong choice wrong).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hubble's_law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble%27s_law

F8. The steady-state model "had expected no uniform relic radiation"; any background in it would come from discrete sources, "lacking a uniform thermal spectrum" (Grokipedia). Hoyle proposed that the glow was starlight "thermalized by interstellar dust", later "iron whiskers"; the quasi-steady-state model of Hoyle, Burbidge and Narlikar attributes it to "thermalization of starlight scattered by microscopic carbon and iron particles" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: the model "attempted to explain the microwave background radiation as the result of light from ancient stars that has been scattered by galactic dust". Hoyle never accepted the Big Bang (Grokipedia, Fred Hoyle).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Steady-state_model ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady-state_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fred_Hoyle

F9. The glow was released about 380,000 years after the beginning, when the universe had cooled to about 3,000 K and became transparent; the expansion has since stretched it by a factor of about 1,100 (Wikipedia gives z = 1089, "a shift in average temperature from 3000 K down to 3 K"). The first stars formed "roughly 100-480 million years after the Big Bang" (Grokipedia, Stellar population), long after the glow set out.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_microwave_background ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redshift ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Stellar_population

## What COBE measured

F10. The Cosmic Background Explorer (COBE) was launched on 18 November 1989. Its FIRAS instrument, led by John Mather, found "a near-perfect blackbody with a temperature of 2.725 ± 0.002 K", with "deviations less than 50 parts per million" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: "the most perfect black body spectrum ever measured", and "it is impossible to distinguish the observed data from the blackbody spectrum for 2.725 K". The spectral result "ruled out alternative theories such as the steady-state model" (Grokipedia, COBE). Why the glow is a blackbody: "This spectrum arises from photons that were in thermal equilibrium with matter in the early universe", when they were "tightly coupled to the ionized plasma through frequent Thomson scattering" (Grokipedia, CMB).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_Background_Explorer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_Background_Explorer

F11. A blackbody's glow has "a continuous and smooth spectrum that depends solely on the temperature of the emitting body", whatever it is made of; the curve "peaks at a wavelength inversely proportional to the temperature" (Grokipedia). So one curve means one temperature.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Black-body_radiation

F12. Why the curve counted against dust: the glow's "spectrum is so close to that of an ideal black body that it could hardly be formed by the superposition of contributions from a multitude of dust clumps at different temperatures as well as at different redshifts" (Wikipedia, Steady-state model). Grokipedia: attempts to mimic it were "unable to match the blackbody form". The glow is also very even across the sky, which counts against many separate sources.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady-state_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Steady-state_model

F13. Dust does soak up starlight: grains absorb it and re-emit it "as thermal infrared radiation", "primarily as thermal emission in the infrared and submillimeter wavelengths", a "thermal continuum" following "a modified blackbody"; grains sit at temperatures that "generally fall between 10 and 100 K" (Grokipedia, Cosmic dust). Dust in the Milky Way glows at COBE's wavelengths and was subtracted from its maps as a foreground (Grokipedia, COBE). MISCONCEPTION: that every glowing thing shows bright lines (only thin gas does; solids and dust glow in a smooth band).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_dust ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_Background_Explorer

F14. COBE's DMR instrument, led by George Smoot, found "fluctuations of about 30 microkelvins on angular scales of 7 to 10 degrees", about "1 part in 100,000" (Grokipedia); the result was announced on 23 April 1992 at the American Physical Society's April meeting in Washington (Wikipedia). The fluctuations represent "primordial density variations that seeded galaxy formation" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: "the imprint of density contrast in the early universe". Mather and Smoot shared the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physics "for their discovery of the blackbody form and anisotropy of the cosmic microwave background radiation".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_Background_Explorer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_Background_Explorer

F15. Why ripples were expected: the even background "highlighted an unresolved question", how temperatures "identical in all directions" could go with the clumps that became galaxies; theorists answered that the early universe must have had density fluctuations, which gravity magnified over billions of years. Smoot: the ripples are "the primordial seeds that grew into present-day galaxies and clusters of galaxies"; they "had been predicted by theorists but, up until now, never had been detected", and scientists "have searched for these irregularities for 27 years" (Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory, 1 May 1992). The spots are "a hundred-thousandth of a degree" warmer or cooler.
    https://www2.lbl.gov/Science-Articles/Archive/smoot-and-wrinkles-in-time.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_Background_Explorer

## WMAP and Planck

F16. The Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe (WMAP) was launched on 30 June 2001 and made "the first high-resolution (0.2-degree) maps of CMB temperature fluctuations"; it gave the universe's age as about 13.77 billion years, "ordinary matter comprising about 4.6%", dark matter 24% and dark energy 71.4% (Grokipedia). The European Space Agency's Planck was launched on 14 May 2009, worked "with substantially improved performance over WMAP" and ended in October 2013 (Grokipedia). On 21 March 2013 the Planck team presented its first all-sky map: normal matter "only about 4.9%", "about 26.8% is dark matter", dark energy 68.3%, age 13.82 billion years (Max Planck Society). The shares come from fitting the standard model to how strong the fluctuations are at different angular sizes (Max Planck Society). Honest open point: Planck's expansion rate (about 67.4 km/s per megaparsec) disagrees with nearby measurements (about 74) by "over 5 sigma" (the Hubble tension).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wilkinson_Microwave_Anisotropy_Probe ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Planck_(spacecraft) ; https://www.mpg.de/7044245/Planck_cbm_universe

## Hydrogen and helium from the first minutes

F17. Big Bang nucleosynthesis ran "roughly 1 to 20 minutes after the Big Bang"; neutron decay brought the neutron-to-proton ratio to "about 1/7"; the result was "about 75% of hydrogen and 25% helium-4 by mass" (Wikipedia), with traces of deuterium, helium-3 and lithium-7 (Grokipedia). Heavier elements were not made: "The rapid expansion of the universe prevents significant production of heavier elements beyond lithium" (Grokipedia); "the temperature had dropped so low that the fusion of helium nuclei into still-heavier elements could not occur" (OpenStax). The first stars were made of "primordial hydrogen and helium", roughly three-quarters hydrogen and one-quarter helium by mass, with no heavy elements (Grokipedia, Stellar population).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Big_Bang_nucleosynthesis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang_nucleosynthesis ; https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/29-3-the-beginning-of-the-universe ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Stellar_population

F18. Too much helium for the stars: "There is just too much helium in the universe to be explained by what happens inside stars"; "even the oldest stars and the most distant galaxies show significant amounts of helium"; "We estimate that 10 times more helium was manufactured in the first 4 minutes of the universe than in all the generations of stars during the succeeding 10 to 15 billion years" (OpenStax Astronomy 2e). Fred Hoyle and Roger Tayler (Nature, 1964, "The Mystery of the Cosmic Helium Abundance") "made the case for a large primordial 4He abundance that could not be simply explained by stars" ("Understanding Big Bang Nucleosynthesis: the physics and its history", a review, arXiv 2111.14254).
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/29-3-the-beginning-of-the-universe ; https://arxiv.org/html/2111.14254v3 ; https://www.nature.com/articles/2031108a0

F19. How the prediction is tested: primordial helium is measured from "the helium and hydrogen emission (recombination) lines from low-metallicity, extragalactic H II regions" (glowing gas clouds in other galaxies, poor in heavy elements); gas that has passed through stars "has its hydrogen burned to helium, increasing the 4He abundance above its primordial value", so the lowest-metallicity regions give the cleanest value, and the helium-against-oxygen trend may be "extrapolated to zero metallicity" (Steigman 2003). Wikipedia: measured in "metal-poor dwarf galaxies". The observed value is 0.245 ± 0.003 (Particle Data Group 2024), "in excellent agreement" with the prediction (Grokipedia). Honest notes: in 2003 Steigman called the agreement "only at the ∼5% level", a mild tension; lithium-7 is still measured "a factor of 2–4 lower" than predicted (the cosmological lithium problem, open).
    https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/0307244 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang_nucleosynthesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Big_Bang_nucleosynthesis

F20. Astronomers call every element heavier than helium a "metal"; heavy elements are made in stars, and "supernova explosions and stellar winds from successive generations of stars enrich the interstellar medium", so later generations of stars are richer in them (Grokipedia, Metallicity). The Sun's helium share is about 0.242.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Metallicity

## Dark matter: an honest hole

F21. Fritz Zwicky studied the Coma Cluster in 1933: he "measured the radial velocities of its member galaxies using spectroscopic observations", found "a high velocity dispersion of roughly 1,000 km/s", applied the virial theorem, and found the cluster "required substantially more mass than accounted for by visible stars and gas"; he wrote of "dunkle Materie" (Grokipedia). His 1933 paper says the density would have to be "at least 400 times greater than that derived on the basis of observations of luminous matter", and that "dark matter is present in much greater amount than luminous matter" (Wikipedia). The idea was "overlooked for decades amid skepticism toward unseen components" (Grokipedia); "Back then, nobody listened" (Stephen Maurer, quoted by Wikipedia). The Coma Cluster has "over 1,000 confirmed members" about 320 million light-years away, and dark matter makes up "about 90% of the cluster's total mass"; "contemporary analyses confirm Zwicky's inference" (Grokipedia, Coma Cluster).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fritz_Zwicky ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Zwicky ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Coma_Cluster ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dark_matter

F22. Vera Rubin used the image-tube spectrograph built by W. Kent Ford Jr. to measure Doppler shifts in galaxies; their 1970 paper was "Rotation of the Andromeda Nebula from a Spectroscopic Survey of Emission Regions". The speeds of gas and stars in Andromeda "remained roughly constant at approximately 250 km/s" out to large radii, "rather than declining inversely with the square root of the radius as predicted by Keplerian dynamics" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: "the outermost components of the galaxy were moving as quickly as those close to the center", so "galaxies must contain at least five to ten times more mass than can be observed directly". A 1980 study (Rubin, Thonnard and Ford) of 21 spirals found the same flat curves. Rubin "did not rule out" alternatives to dark matter (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Vera_Rubin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vera_Rubin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Galaxy_rotation_curve

F23. The Solar System for comparison: "The Sun accounts for 99.86% of the system's total mass" (Grokipedia); Mercury orbits at 47.4 km/s and Neptune at 5.4 km/s (NASA planetary fact sheet). Grokipedia, Galaxy rotation curve: with visible mass alone, speeds should fall off "inversely with the square root of radius" as in Keplerian orbits; instead they "remain roughly constant", typically 200-300 km/s.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_System ; https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Galaxy_rotation_curve

F24. What dark matter is remains unknown: "Unlike ordinary matter, dark matter's composition remains unknown"; it makes up "approximately 27%" of the universe against "just 5%" for ordinary matter, "about five times more"; candidates include "weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs), axions, and sterile neutrinos"; "Null results from experimental searches continued to constrain dark matter candidates" (the LZ xenon detector set a "world-leading" upper limit); Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND) "proposes a fundamental alteration to gravity at low accelerations" instead (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dark_matter

## Dark energy: the expansion speeding up

F25. Two teams, the Supernova Cosmology Project (led by Saul Perlmutter) and the High-Z Supernova Search Team (Brian Schmidt, Adam Riess), found in 1998 that the expansion is speeding up. Type Ia supernovae are "standardizable candles": their peak brightness is consistent, so how bright one looks gives its distance. Distant ones looked "fainter than expected in a decelerating universe", so they were farther than expected. Astronomers had expected gravity to slow the expansion. Perlmutter, Schmidt and Riess shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics. The speeding-up began "approximately 5–6 billion years ago" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: Riess and others found the high-red-shift supernovae "10% to 15% further than expected" in a low-density universe without a cosmological constant; cosmologists had expected that the "recession velocity would always be decelerating".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Accelerating_expansion_of_the_universe ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerating_expansion_of_the_universe

F26. The checks: the 1998 results were "initially met with skepticism regarding systematic errors in distance measurements" and were "soon corroborated by additional supernova datasets", with baryon acoustic oscillations in galaxy surveys and the background glow (Planck) as independent evidence (Grokipedia). Dust is corrected for: "corrections account for interstellar dust extinction via color excess measurements" (dust reddens as it dims); on whether such supernovae were dimmer long ago, "current data show no strong evidence for such changes at z < 1" (Grokipedia). A 2006 review: the possibility of grey dust alone causing the dimming "has been now ruled out" (Corasaniti, MNRAS 372).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Accelerating_expansion_of_the_universe ; https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0603833

F27. Dark energy is the name for the cause: about 68% of the universe; "one of the greatest unsolved mysteries in modern physics"; the simplest model is the cosmological constant, the energy of empty space, but quantum theory predicts a vacuum energy "up to 120 orders of magnitude larger" than the value seen; DESI and Euclid results give tentative "hints that dark energy may be evolving, potentially weakening over cosmic time", not confirmed (Grokipedia). NASA: "The short answer is: We don't know."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dark_energy ; https://science.nasa.gov/dark-energy/

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- A red shift means light has slowed down or tired on its way (F4: light's speed never changes).
- Any reddening is a red shift (F3: dust reddens the colour but cannot move the lines).
- Galaxies fly through space faster than light (F2: space stretches; nothing outruns light through space).
- The Big Bang went off at one point, so the glow should be warmest toward a centre (T4: no centre).
- Every glowing thing shows bright lines (F11, F13: dust and solids glow in a smooth curve).
- A forecast counts only if its number comes out exactly right (F5, F6: 5 K forecast, about 3 K found).
- Gravity of visible matter is all there is, so the cluster must be flying apart (F21).
- Fainter supernovae mean the expansion is slowing harder (F25: fainter means farther, so faster).
