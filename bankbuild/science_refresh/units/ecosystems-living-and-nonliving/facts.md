# Ecosystems: Living and Nonliving Parts: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; National Geographic Education, USGS, state agencies and grade-5 standards to corroborate.
Neighbours not repeated here: food-chains-and-food-webs (producers, consumers, chains, webs, a link removed) and decomposers-and-recycling-matter (rot, compost, humus, nutrients back to soil). ## Facts

F1 An ecosystem is all the living things in an area interacting with each other and with the nonliving physical and chemical environment around them. https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecosystem ("all living organisms in a defined area interacting with each other", with the nonliving environment); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/ecosystem/ ("a geographic area where plants, animals and other organisms, as well as weather and landscape, work together"; "Every part of an ecosystem depends on every other part, directly or indirectly")

F2 The living parts of an ecosystem are its biotic factors: plants, animals, fungi and bacteria (microorganisms). https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecosystem ("Biotic components encompass all living organisms within an ecosystem": plants, animals, fungi, microorganisms); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/ecosystem/ ("biotic (living) factors"; "Biotic factors include plants, animals and other organisms"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12a-biotic-abiotic-interactions?hsLang=en (biotic: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria; its pond example counts the bacteria in the mud)

F3 The nonliving parts of an ecosystem are its abiotic factors: sunlight, water, air, soil, rocks and temperature. https://grokipedia.com/page/Abiotic_component ("the non-living chemical and physical parts of an ecosystem that affect living organisms", "such as rocks, water, air, soil, temperature, sunlight"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/abiotic-factors/ ("the nonliving physical and chemical aspects of an ecosystem"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12a-biotic-abiotic-interactions?hsLang=en (abiotic: water, soil, sunlight, air, temperature, rocks)

F4 Ecosystems can be very large or very small: a tide pool, the pond the sea leaves among the rocks when the tide goes out, is a complete tiny ecosystem. https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/ecosystem/ ("Ecosystems can be very large or very small"; "Tide pools, the ponds left by the ocean as the tide goes out, are complete, tiny ecosystems")

F5 Neighbouring words that are NOT an ecosystem: a community is only the living populations of an area (no nonliving part); a population is the members of one species in one area; a food web is only the network of who eats whom. https://grokipedia.com/page/Community_(ecology) ("an assemblage of interacting populations of two or more species"; strictly biotic, unlike an ecosystem, which "integrates both biotic components (living organisms) and abiotic factors"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Population_ecology ("assemblages of organisms of a single species occupying the same geographic area at the same time"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_web ("a complex network of feeding relationships among organisms in an ecosystem")

F6 MISCONCEPTION: children often judge life by movement, so they count moving nonliving things (clouds, wind, the Sun, fire) as alive, and may count still living things such as plants as not alive; even some ten-year-olds misclassify plants as not alive. https://www.education.vic.gov.au/school/teachers/teachingresources/discipline/science/continuum/pages/livingthings.aspx (things are "living" if they move or grow, so clouds, wind and lightning are counted as living; fire thought alive because it moves and "consumes"); https://www.spiegeloog.amsterdam/is-the-sun-alive-and-are-the-clouds-made-of-sugar/ (Wright, Poulin-Dubois and Kelley 2015, movement as the cue; Leddon, Waxman and Medin 2009, "even ten years old children misclassify plants as not alive"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12a-biotic-abiotic-interactions?hsLang=en (listed misconception: "Plants are biotic, but the soil they grow in is just dirt")

F7 Wind is moving air, and a cloud is a mass of tiny water droplets or ice crystals in the air: both are nonliving, though both move. https://grokipedia.com/page/Wind ("Wind is the movement of air relative to Earth's surface"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Cloud ("a visible aggregate of minute particles of water droplets or ice crystals suspended in the atmosphere")

F8 Moss is a small green land plant that makes its own food by photosynthesis; it grows on rocks, soil and tree bark. https://grokipedia.com/page/Moss ("Mosses are small, non-vascular land plants"; the green plant body "is photosynthetic"; habitats include "forest floors, rock surfaces, and exposed mineral soils")

F9 Living things depend on abiotic factors. Lizards, which take their body heat from their surroundings, perch on sun-exposed rocks in the morning to warm up; cold slows their muscles, and they become inactive in cold periods. https://grokipedia.com/page/Ectotherm ("many lizards perch on sun-exposed rocks in the morning"; reptiles "often become inactive during cold periods"; low temperatures slow muscle performance in lizards); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12a-biotic-abiotic-interactions?hsLang=en (a desert lizard needs warm sunlight, air and sand)

F10 Many interactions are with other living things (biotic): a bee drinks nectar, a sweet liquid that flowers make; a hawk catches a mouse; a rabbit eats a plant's leaves. The grade-5 line names these as food, predators and partners. https://grokipedia.com/page/Nectar ("a sweet, viscous liquid secreted by specialized glands called nectaries in plants", the raw material bees make honey from); https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecosystem (biotic interactions, including herbivory); curriculum map G5.L1 ("other living things: food, predators, partners")

F11 A habitat is the place where a living thing makes its home and finds everything it needs to survive: food, water, shelter and space. https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/habitat/ ("A habitat is a place where an organism makes its home"; it "meets all the environmental conditions an organism needs to survive"; its main components are shelter, water, food and space; the puma needs deer and other prey, water, shelter such as dens, and space); https://grokipedia.com/page/Habitat ("the specific set of resources and environmental conditions in a given area"; "a habitat is inherently organism-centered")

F12 The mountain lion (also called the puma or cougar) hunts mainly deer, raises its young in hidden dens, and needs a large home range. https://grokipedia.com/page/Cougar ("also known as the mountain lion, puma, panther, or catamount"; "primarily hunts large ungulates such as deer and elk"; "Cougars require large home ranges"; kittens "born in hidden dens or thick cover"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/habitat/ (the puma's habitat example)

F13 Words a habitat is not: a niche is a living thing's role or job in its ecosystem; climate is the long-term pattern of weather in a region; a shelter is only one part of a habitat. https://grokipedia.com/page/Habitat ("the ecological niche represents the multidimensional functional position of a species within its environment"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Climate ("Climate is the long-term pattern of weather conditions in a specific region"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/habitat/ (shelter as one of the four components)

F14 A healthy ecosystem usually has many different kinds of living things (biodiversity); ecosystems with more biodiversity are generally stronger and stand up better to disease and drought. When a disease killed most of the chestnut trees in some great forests, the forests survived because other kinds of trees grew there too. CAUTION: the evidence is for standing up to trouble (resistance); grassland experiments found no clear effect on how fast an ecosystem recovers afterwards, so the question says "stands up", not "recovers". https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/biodiversity/ ("Ecosystems with a lot of biodiversity are generally stronger and more resistant to disease than those with fewer species"; "The forest ecosystem was able to survive because other kinds of trees also grew there"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Biodiversity ("Higher biodiversity generally enhances ecosystem stability"; high-diversity grassland plots lost about half as much productivity in drought and other extremes; "no significant biodiversity effect on resilience"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12c-human-activities-ecosystems?hsLang=en (features of a healthy ecosystem: "Many different kinds of living things (biodiversity)", balanced food webs, clean air and water)

F15 Predators belong in a healthy ecosystem: taking them away can throw a whole food web out of balance. https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/ecosystem/ ("removing the wolves threw off the food web in the ecosystem"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12c-human-activities-ecosystems?hsLang=en ("Balanced food webs")

F16 When the environment changes (its physical features, temperature or resources), some organisms survive and reproduce, some move to new places, some new kinds move in, and some die. A drought kills plants, shrinks wetlands, causes mass die-offs of water-dwelling animals in drying wetlands, and favours drought-tolerant kinds over sensitive ones. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls4-4-biological-evolution-unity-and-diversity (LS2.C: "some organisms survive and reproduce, others move to new locations, yet others move into the transformed environment, and some die"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Drought ("widespread vegetation die-off"; "rivers and wetlands shrink"; "Aquatic species in drying wetlands experience mass die-offs"; drought "favors drought-tolerant or invasive species while eliminating sensitive ones"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/habitat/ ("If there were a drought, plants would become scarce")

F17 MISCONCEPTION: an individual animal can change its body to suit a change in its surroundings. In fact individuals do not evolve in one lifetime; inherited features change in populations over many generations. https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/ (misconception "Individual organisms can evolve during a single lifespan"; "Populations, not individual organisms, evolve"; natural selection "does not involve effort, trying, or wanting")

F18 Plant roots bind the soil together; cutting down a forest strips that cover away and exposes the soil, so rain washes it off (erosion) and the mud is carried into streams. https://grokipedia.com/page/Soil_erosion ("root systems mechanically bind soil aggregates"; "Deforestation exposes soil to erosive agents by eliminating vegetative cover"; eroded sediment "clogs habitats"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Deforestation ("removing vegetative cover that stabilizes surfaces"; on slopes "root removal compromises soil cohesion"; "sediment-laden discharge")

F19 Roots also help rain soak into the ground; bare ground soaks up less, so more water runs off over the top. https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/waters/shoreline-alterations-natural-buffers-lakescaping.html (deep native roots "hold soil, slow water, and help water soak into the ground"; "Vegetation is extremely important for controlling erosion")

F20 Cutting down forests destroys habitats; habitat loss and fragmentation are the main threat to the world's species. https://grokipedia.com/page/Deforestation (biodiversity loss by "eliminating habitats critical to species survival"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Habitat (loss and fragmentation of habitats "represent the predominant threat to global species, affecting over 88% of assessed threatened taxa"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/biodiversity/ ("Human activity altering and destroying natural habitats is a major reason for the loss of biodiversity")

F21 Fertilizer washed off farm fields carries nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus) into lakes; algae grow fast (an algal bloom) and turn the water green and cloudy; when the algae die, bacteria break them down and use up the oxygen dissolved in the water, and fish die from the lack of oxygen (fish kills, dead zones). https://grokipedia.com/page/Eutrophication ("Agricultural runoff ... the predominant anthropogenic driver"; dense blooms "obscure light penetration"; "Decomposition of algal biomass consumes dissolved oxygen"; "Fish kills result from these low-oxygen events"); https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/dissolved-oxygen-and-water (algal blooms "die and are eaten by bacteria, depleting the oxygen"); https://tpwd.texas.gov/education/resources/keep-texas-wild/freddie-the-fish/background-for-teachers (bacteria "decompose all of these dead algal cells"; dissolved oxygen falls "to concentrations not suitable for fish to respire properly"; written for 9- and 10-year-olds)

F22 Fish breathe oxygen dissolved in water, and need it to survive; cold water holds more dissolved oxygen than warm water. https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/dissolved-oxygen-and-water ("This dissolved oxygen is breathed by fish and zooplankton and is needed by them to survive"; "Cold water can hold more dissolved oxygen than warm water")

F23 Fertilizer runoff and algal blooms are taught in 5th grade: Mystery Science's grade 5 ecosystems unit (5-ESS3-1) defines fertilizer, runoff and algal blooms and asks why a lot of algae is bad for fish. https://mysteryscience.com/ecosystems/mystery-6/protecting-environments/954?t=student ("a material that contains nutrients to help plants grow"; "a quick increase in the amount of algae growing in an area")

F24 A strip of wild, deep-rooted plants along a lake or stream shore (a buffer) catches nutrients such as phosphorus and nitrogen and the soil in runoff, holds the bank against erosion, and protects wildlife habitat and fish. Lawn grass mowed to the water's edge does none of this: its roots are only 2-3 inches long, against about 2-3 feet for a native prairie grass. https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/waters/shoreline-alterations-natural-buffers-lakescaping.html ("intercepting nutrients and reducing runoff, erosion, and sedimentation"; "protects wildlife habitat, water quality, and fish"; functions "eliminated previously by sod, hard structures, or mowing"; "the roots of lawn grass are only 2-3 inches long"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Riparian_buffer (buffers "stabilize streambanks against erosion"; "intercepting and filtering surface runoff")

F25 Fallen trees in a lake give fish shelter and places to feed. https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/topic/fishing/outreach/fishsticks.html ("Fallen trees provide shelter and feeding areas for a diversity of fish species"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Coarse_woody_debris (woody debris makes "pools that enhance fish and invertebrate habitats")

F26 Feeding bread to ducks harms a pond: bread has little food value for the ducks, and uneaten soggy bread builds up nutrients in the water, feeding more algae and bringing disease and pests. https://canalrivertrust.org.uk/about-us/our-campaigns/keeping-our-ducks-healthy/why-is-bread-bad ("Bread doesn't have much nutritional value"; "Uneaten soggy bread can cause a build-up of bad nutrients"; "more algae growing around water, more diseases and more pests")

F27 People can harm an ecosystem (deforestation, pollution, overfishing) or help it (planting trees, restoring wetlands, cleaning up pollution, protecting land as preserves and parks). https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12c-human-activities-ecosystems?hsLang=en (TEKS 5.12C: "Describe a healthy ecosystem and how human activities can be beneficial or harmful to an ecosystem"; its lists of beneficial and harmful activities)

F28 The grade-5 standards: TEKS 5.12A, "observe and describe how a variety of organisms survive by interacting with biotic and abiotic factors in a healthy ecosystem"; TEKS 5.12C, as in F27. https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12a-biotic-abiotic-interactions?hsLang=en; https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12c-human-activities-ecosystems?hsLang=en

## Misconceptions used as wrong choices
- Moving things (wind, flowing water, clouds) are alive; still things (moss) are not (F6, F7, F8).
- A food web, a community or a population is the same as an ecosystem (F5).
- A habitat is just an animal's shelter or "home" (F11, F13).
- An ecosystem is healthiest with only the strongest kinds, with lots of animals of a few kinds, or with no predators (F14, F15).
- An animal changes its body to cope with a drought (F17).
- Pollution kills fish by poisoning them directly; dark or warm water is to blame (F21, F22).
- Tidy-looking or kind-looking acts help a lake: mowing to the water, clearing fallen logs, feeding the ducks bread (F24, F25, F26).

## Notes
- Lanes: no named places in the stems. The chestnut example (F14) is used in a context without its region; the puma example (F11, F12) is National Geographic's own habitat example, told as a mountain lion.
- F14 is worded with care: diversity helps an ecosystem stand up to a drought or disease; faster recovery is not established.

## Revision facts (2026-11-26, the one revision round)

F29 Turtles are ectotherms that warm their bodies mainly by basking in sunlight, often on rocks or logs. https://grokipedia.com/page/Turtle ("Turtles are ectotherms that primarily regulate body temperature through behavioral means, such as basking in sunlight to elevate core temperatures"; sliders pile "atop one another at logs or rocks for thermoregulation")

F30 Adult frogs are mostly meat-eaters that catch insects, flies among them, with a sticky tongue. https://grokipedia.com/page/Frog ("Adult frogs are predominantly carnivorous, capturing prey such as insects (including flies, moths, locusts, and spiders)"; "extensible, sticky tongues")

F31 Pondweeds are living water plants: dabbling ducks eat their leaves, stems and seeds, and dense beds of pondweed and other water plants give fish shelter from predators. https://grokipedia.com/page/Potamogeton ("dabbling ducks and coots feed on vegetative parts and seeds of various pondweeds"; "Dense beds offer refuge from predators, shelter for juvenile fish"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Aquatic_plant ("native macrophytes sustain fish populations by providing both forage and refuge")

F32 Some frogs live through a drought by burrowing into the soil or mud and aestivating: the body's metabolism slows (in aestivation to as little as 1-20% of normal), some shed skin layers into a waterproof cocoon, and they wait out the dry season and breed when the rains come. They slow the body they have; no animal grows a new kind of body because its home dried. https://grokipedia.com/page/Aestivation (frogs "burrow into soil, shed skin layers to form waterproof barriers"; "metabolic rate to as low as 1–20% of normal levels"; amphibians "aestivate through dry seasons and breed explosively during monsoons"; obligatory aestivation "is seasonally programmed"); with F17 (inherited features change only over generations)

F33 MISCONCEPTION (author's judgement, the judge's suggested lure): heavy trees press the soil down, so cleared soil is lighter and washes away. Cutting the trees does not change the soil's own weight; cleared soil washes off because no roots bind it and bare ground soaks up less rain (F18, F19).
