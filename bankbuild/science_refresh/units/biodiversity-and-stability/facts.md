## What biodiversity is

F1. Biodiversity is "the variability among living organisms from all sources", counted at "three hierarchical levels: genetic diversity, species diversity, and ecosystem diversity" (Grokipedia). The Convention on Biological Diversity's definition, which the education study below quotes, likewise covers ecosystem, species and genetic diversity.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Biodiversity ; https://mdpi.com/2071-1050/11/17/4517

F2. A plant grown from a cutting is "a genetic clone of the parent"; "cuttings produce genetically identical clones without such recombination" (seed-grown plants carry recombined genes). Seed tubers of a potato are likewise "planted to produce genetically identical daughter plants".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cutting_(plant) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Potato

F3. Students commonly reduce biodiversity to the number of species: in a study of 275 German 10th graders nearly all did so; other studies find students overlook genetic and ecosystem diversity, and an Indonesian grade-10 study found the most misconceptions where genetic, species and ecosystem diversity are compared. (See M1.)
    https://mdpi.com/2071-1050/11/17/4517 ; https://wiredspace.wits.ac.za/server/api/core/bitstreams/e383d88f-3808-4b2f-878a-0b6de5050ae5/content

## Cedar Creek: diversity and the drought (Tilman and Downing)

F4. Cedar Creek is "a 5,400-acre field station in east-central Minnesota managed by the University of Minnesota". From 1982 David Tilman ran "over 200 grassland plots across four fields" given nitrogen additions "ranging from 0 to 27.2 grams per square meter per year" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: the grassland experiment began in 1982 with "more than 200 grassland plots". Tilman's 1996 paper gives 207 plots.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/g_david_tilman ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._David_Tilman ; https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/biodiversity-population-versus-ecosystem-stability/

F5. David Tilman and John A. Downing, "Biodiversity and stability in grasslands", Nature 367:363-365 (1994). The 1987-1988 drought was the most severe of the past 50 years at the site (rainfall cut by about half). Abstract: "primary productivity in more diverse plant communities is more resistant to, and recovers more fully from, a major drought"; "each additional species lost from our grasslands had a progressively greater impact on drought resistance" (a curved, saturating relationship); the results support "the diversity-stability hypothesis but not the alternative hypothesis that most species are functionally redundant". Drought resistance is a share (the proportional change in plant mass); species-rich plots can lose more mass in absolute terms because they have more to lose (J. Plant Ecol. 2024, citing Tilman and Downing 1994 and Isbell 2015).
    https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/biodiversity-and-stability-in-grasslands/ ; https://www.nature.com/articles/367363a0 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/g_david_tilman ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3387045 ; https://academic.oup.com/jpe/article/17/4/rtae059/7701213

F6. The honest caveat. The plots differed in species partly because of the nitrogen added to them; Michael Huston (1997) called the nitrogen a hidden confound. Tilman's later review says the drought result held after controlling for nitrogen addition and species composition. A 2017 reanalysis (ESA meeting abstract) found no effect of richness on drought resistance once nitrogen treatment was accounted for. Forest Isbell's team (Nature 526:574-577, 2015), pooling 46 grassland experiments that sowed set numbers of species, found that diversity raised resistance to climate extremes (1-2 species: productivity shifted by about half; 16-32 species: by about a quarter), but found no clear diversity effect on recovery a year later.
    https://eco.confex.com/eco/2017/webprogram/Paper62885.html ; https://annualreviews.org/doi/pdf/10.1146/annurev-ecolsys-120213-091917 ; https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/biodiversity-increases-the-resistance-of-ecosystem-productivity-t/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Biodiversity ("no significant biodiversity effect on resilience")

## Why the total holds steady: compensation and insurance

F7. Tilman, "Biodiversity: population versus ecosystem stability", Ecology 77:350-363 (1996), 207 plots: "biodiversity stabilizes community and ecosystem processes, but not population processes". "When climatic variations harm some species, unharmed competitors increase. Such compensatory increases stabilize total community biomass, but cause species abundances to be more variable"; the difference "likely results from interspecific competition". Lecture summary of the paper: "Year to year variability in species abundance higher in species rich plots"; "Year to year variability in total biomass lower in species rich plots". The insurance hypothesis: biodiversity "buffers ecosystem functioning against environmental fluctuations"; "fluctuations in individual species' performances offset one another" (Grokipedia); mixtures have "a greater likelihood of tolerant species being present (insurance effect hypothesis)", "which maintains biomass in periods of drought" (J. Plant Ecol. 2024). Species that rose and fell together would make the total swing more, not less (follows from the same offsetting).
    https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/biodiversity-population-versus-ecosystem-stability/ ; https://science.umd.edu/classroom/BSCI363/inouye/Lectures/diversitystability2/tsld005.htm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_effects_of_biodiversity ; https://academic.oup.com/jpe/article/17/4/rtae059/7701213

## Losing species costs more over time (Reich)

F8. Peter Reich, David Tilman and colleagues, "Impacts of biodiversity loss escalate through time as redundancy fades", Science 336:589-592 (2012): two Cedar Creek experiments of at least 13 years, BioDIV (planted 1994-1995) and BioCON (planted 1997), with plots of one, four, nine or 16 species (ScienceDaily; BioDIV ran 1 to 16). Shorter studies, "most about two years long", had found that "having more than six or eight species in a plot gave no additional benefit". Over the long run "Every additional species in a plot contributed to a gradual increase in both soil fertility and biomass production"; in the richest plots all 16 species "contributed more and more each year"; "different species have different ways to acquire water, nutrients and carbon and maintain them in the ecosystem" (ScienceDaily). Scientific American: in the first few years plots with eight or more species "fared about as well as those with fewer species", but over more than a decade the plots with the most species produced the most.
    https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120503142638.htm ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-biodiversity-keeps-earth-alive/ ; https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/impacts-of-biodiversity-loss-escalate-through-time-as-redundancy-/

## Ecosystem services

F9. Ecosystem services are the benefits ecosystems' processes give people (Grokipedia: provisioning such as food and fresh water; regulating, such as pollination; supporting; cultural). Animal pollination underpins about 35 percent of global crop production, and over 75 percent of leading food crops depend on animal pollinators; wetlands remove up to 50-90 percent of nitrogen and phosphorus pollutants; soil microbes break down pollutants and recycle nutrients and improve soil fertility; root networks and litter cover reduce flood risk and soil erosion.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecosystem_service ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Biodiversity

F10. Taylor Ricketts, Gretchen Daily, Paul Ehrlich and Charles Michener, "Economic value of tropical forest to coffee production", PNAS 101:12579-12582 (2004). Finca Santa Fe, a 1,065-hectare coffee farm in the Valle General, Costa Rica, bordered by forest fragments of 46, 111 and 34 hectares. Sites near the forest (within 100 m), intermediate (700-800 m) and far (1,400-1,600 m). On each site some branches were hand-pollinated (whether on the same bushes was not read) ("augmented pollen") and control branches were left "unmanipulated to measure production under ambient pollination". Pollination was adequate near and at intermediate distances; at far sites hand-pollination raised production by 20.8 percent. "Forest-based pollinators increased coffee yields by 20% within ≈1 km of forest" and cut "peaberries" (small misshapen seeds) by 27 percent. Dominant visitors: feral honey bees (Apis mellifera) and 10 native stingless bee species. Coffea arabica "is able to self-pollinate, but bee visitation can increase yields 15-50%". Fragments A and B were worth about $60,000 (paper: about $62,000) a year to the farm.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC515099 ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/forest-boosts-nearby-coff/

F11. Lucas Garibaldi and colleagues, "Wild pollinators enhance fruit set of crops regardless of honey bee abundance", Science 339:1608-1611 (2013): "universally positive associations of fruit set with flower visitation by wild insects in 41 crop systems worldwide"; "fruit set increased significantly with visitation by honey bees in only 14% of the systems surveyed"; "an increase in wild insect visitation enhanced fruit set by twice as much as an equivalent increase in honey bee visitation"; the two "promoted fruit set independently, so high abundance of managed honey bees supplemented, rather than substituted for, pollination by wild insects".
    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23449997/ ; https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/wild-pollinators-enhance-fruit-set-of-crops-regardless-of-honey-b/

## One crop everywhere: rice, potatoes, corn

F12. Youyong Zhu and colleagues (with Christopher Mundt), "Genetic diversity and disease control in rice", Nature 406:718-722 (2000), Yunnan Province, China. Genetically diversified rice was planted in all the rice fields of five townships in 1998 and ten in 1999. "Crop heterogeneity is a possible solution to the vulnerability of monocultured crops to disease." "Disease-susceptible rice varieties planted in mixtures with resistant varieties had 89% greater yield and blast was 94% less severe than when they were grown in monoculture"; fungicide sprays were no longer needed by the end of the two-year programme. Design: "a repeating pattern of one row of glutinous rice inter-planted with four or six rows of indica rice"; the "highly susceptible glutinous varieties suffered less disease when interplanted with the generally resistant indica hybrid varieties" (AGRIS abstract of a companion paper). By 2001 the mixed planting had "expanded to 100,000 ha in Yunnan" (IRRI, Rice Today). Rice blast is caused by a fungus (Magnaporthe).
    https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v406y2000i6797d10.1038_35021046.html ; https://agris.fao.org/search/ru/records/64723a34e17b74d2224ef2c2 ; https://ricetoday.irri.org/?p=12582 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnaporthe_grisea

F13. How a mixed planting slows a disease (general): "The dilution effect occurs when non-host plants in the intercrop reduce the proportion of suitable hosts"; mixing "breaks [pathogen] life cycles by incorporating non-host crops that prevent transmission and spore dispersal", and "non-susceptible companions ... limit airborne spread" (Grokipedia, Intercropping). Resistance to a disease is inherited, carried in genes (F16); a plant does not catch it from a neighbour. (Zhu's own discussion of mechanism was not read; the question asks only for the best explanation and offers none of the other proposed ones.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Intercropping ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9110483

F14. Phytophthora infestans, the cause of potato late blight, is "a heterothallic oomycete pathogen in the phylum Oomycota", a water mould, not a true fungus: its cell walls hold cellulose, "notably absent chitin". Its sporangia "detach readily for wind or water dispersal". It reached Europe in 1845; in Ireland about a third of the 1845 crop was destroyed by harvest time, and the 1846 crop almost entirely, in cool, humid weather.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Phytophthora_infestans ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Great_Famine_(Ireland)

F15. The Lumper was "the prolific but genetically uniform" variety that dominated Irish potato growing by the early 19th century; "its genetic uniformity heightened vulnerability to disease", and varieties like it were "lacking genetic resistance" (Grokipedia, Great Famine). Wikipedia: the variety "whose widespread cultivation throughout Ireland, prior to the 1840s, is implicated in the Irish Great Famine". In Ireland "the lack of varietal diversity prevented natural resistance" (Grokipedia, Potato). Potatoes there were propagated from tubers and descended from a small founding group, so there was little variation (maize teacher's guide, summarising the Understanding Evolution account).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Great_Famine_(Ireland) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Lumper ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Potato ; https://maize.teacherfriendlyguide.org/index.php/genetic-diversity-and-evolution/consequences-of-low-diversity

F16. "Early breeding identified major resistance genes such as R1 through R11 from Solanum demissum" (Grokipedia). S. demissum is a wild relative of the potato from Mexico (Planta 2022, Table 1, origin of R1: Mexico); "Research was initiated to introduce the Rpi genes from Solanum demissum into potato cultivars", cultivars carrying them were grown on a large scale in Europe, and the genes "were quickly overcome by new virulent P. infestans strains" (all of R1 to R11). Breeding for resistance from wild relatives began about 100 years ago; breeders now stack several resistance genes, many from other wild Mexican species such as Solanum bulbocastanum. Spraying a plant does not change the genes it passes on (acquired traits are not inherited; see the T3 unit natural-and-artificial-selection and the T5 Lamarck line).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Phytophthora_infestans ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9110483

F17. The 1970 southern corn leaf blight: "approximately 85% of U.S. corn hybrids relied on Texas male-sterile cytoplasm" for "efficient hybrid seed production" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia gives "an estimated 90% prevalence", adopted in the early 1960s "to eliminate the previous need for hand detasseling". Race T "first appeared in the United States in Florida in 1969", producing a toxin that "specifically targeted T-cms corn". The epidemic "reduced corn yields by about 15%, equivalent to a loss of 710 million bushels", about $1 billion in 1970 dollars (Wikipedia: "about 15% of the corn belt's crop"). By 1971 about half of U.S. seed was in normal cytoplasm, "fully resolving the vulnerability by 1972"; Wikipedia credits the return to normal cytoplasm along with weather, buried residue and earlier planting. "The 1970 epidemic underscored the dangers of genetic uniformity in monoculture systems." Because the varieties were so similar, "none of them had genes that were resistant to this fungus" (maize guide).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Southern_corn_leaf_blight ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_corn_leaf_blight ; https://maize.teacherfriendlyguide.org/index.php/genetic-diversity-and-evolution/consequences-of-low-diversity

F18. Hybrid corn seed results "from a controlled cross-pollination between two distinct, highly homozygous parental inbred lines", made "often through manual or mechanical detasseling to remove pollen-producing tassels" or with "cytoplasmic male sterility systems"; farmers repurchase hybrid seed each year. Different hybrids come from different parent lines, so they are not copies of one plant.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hybrid_seed

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Biodiversity means the number of species only (F3); genetic variety within a species is overlooked.
M2. Many kinds of plants crowded together must compete harder for water, so a rich plot should suffer more in a drought (the opposite of F5).
M3. Species are spare parts: past a handful, extra species add nothing (the functional-redundancy hypothesis that Tilman and Downing rejected, F5; the short-study result that Reich's long study overturned, F8).
M4. Honeybees from hives do the pollinating, so wild insects can be replaced by more hives (the belief Garibaldi's study tested, F11).
M5. Resistance can be caught from a healthy neighbour, like a cold (the T2 ash-dieback lure; F13).
M6. A plant made to survive by spraying passes its survival on (inheritance of acquired traits; F16).
M7. Many differently named varieties mean much genetic variety; in 1970 most U.S. hybrids shared one trait (F17, F18).
M8. Phytophthora infestans is a fungus; it is a water mould, an oomycete (F14).

## Added at revision (2026-11-26)

F19. Zhu, Fang, Wang, Fan, Yang, Mew and Mundt, "Panicle blast and canopy moisture in rice cultivar mixtures", Phytopathology 95: 433-438 (2005), on the Yunnan mixtures: the glutinous (sticky) cultivars were "35 to 40 cm taller" than the hybrid; averaged over two seasons, glutinous rice in pure stands had 20.0 days of 100% humidity at 0800 h against 2.2 in mixtures, and dew covered 84% of its leaf area in pure stands against 36% in mixtures; "Although other mechanisms also were operative, reduced leaf wetness was likely a substantial contributor to panicle blast control in the mixtures."
    https://cgspace.cgiar.org/items/07c7c70e-db18-48e4-8aad-72089087b335

F20. The mechanisms studied in variety mixtures (Raboin and others, Plant Pathology 2012, as read by the fact-check): dilution of inoculum between susceptible hosts, "considered the most important mechanism in variety mixtures", the barrier effect, and induced resistance, "triggered by the presence of avirulent spores in the crop that induce the expression of the plant defence mechanisms". A root poison from the neighbouring variety is not among them. Induced resistance is a plant's own defence switched on, not resistance genes passed from a neighbour: a plant's genes come from its parents (F16).
    https://bsppjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-3059.2012.02602.x

F21. Corn hybrids in 1970: "Double-cross hybrids were primarily grown across the United States from the 1930s through the 1960s", and "By the early 1970s, the changeover from double-cross to single-cross corn hybrids was mostly complete" (Pioneer); so not every 1970 hybrid was a cross of two inbred lines (a double cross joins two single crosses of four inbreds). Race T: Wikipedia says the disease "first appeared in the U.S. in 1968"; Grokipedia and a USDA-era account give 1969; so "a year or two before" 1970.
    https://www.pioneer.com/us/agronomy/domestication-of-corn.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_corn_leaf_blight ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Southern_corn_leaf_blight

F22. Hubbard Brook, New Hampshire, Watershed 2: "All woody vegetation in W2 was cut between 18 November and 31 December 1965" and left in place; "Herbicides were applied during the following three growing seasons to suppress regrowth" (1966-1968); the aim was "to eliminate transpiring vegetation to evaluate its influence on streamflow and nutrient cycling"; "Annual streamflow increased by 40, 28 and 26 percent during the 3 years of devegetation" (Hubbard Brook Ecosystem Study). Likens, Bormann, Johnson, Fisher and Pierce, Ecological Monographs (1970): "Annual stream-flow was increased 39% the first year and 28% the second year"; "Nitrate concentrations were 41-fold higher than the undisturbed condition the first year and 56-fold higher the second"; the nitrate concentration "has exceeded, almost continuously, the health levels recommended for drinking water"; the loss "was due to an alteration of the nitrogen cycle within the ecosystem".

F23. Why the nitrate washes out after cutting: Bernhardt, Likens, Buso and Driscoll (PNAS 100: 10304-10308, 2003) give "reduced plant uptake" and increased nitrogen mineralization and nitrification in upland soils; SUNY ESF: with the trees gone, more sun warms the soil and "boosts the activity of soil microbes, which elevates the production of ammonium and nitrate", more than the few remaining plants take up, and rain carries it to the streams.
