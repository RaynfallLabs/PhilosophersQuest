# Food Chains and Food Webs: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-25. Grokipedia first; National Geographic Education, NOAA, NGSS, Wikipedia and teaching sources to corroborate. Curriculum line G5.L2 (NGSS 5-LS2-1, TEKS 5.12B).

Neighbours: how-plants-make-food keys photosynthesis and "the grass's energy came from sunlight"; energy-changes-form keys "the Sun is the main source of energy" and "food holds chemical energy". This unit does not key those facts (F2 is used in contexts only). decomposers-and-recycling-matter owns what decomposers do; here they are named as a role only (F7).

## Facts

F1 Producers (autotrophs) make their own food and do not depend on other living things for it. Plants and algae are producers; they catch the energy of sunlight by photosynthesis. Producers make up the first level of every food chain. https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_chain (the chain starts with "autotrophic plants or algae that harness sunlight through photosynthesis"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-chain/ ("Producers, also known as autotrophs, make their own food"; "They make up the first level of every food chain"; "Algae, whose larger forms are known as seaweed, are autotrophic"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/

F2 The energy that moves along a food chain enters it through the producers, which turn the Sun's energy into living matter. (Context only: the neighbours key the Sun as the source.) https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ ("Autotrophs, the producers in a food web, convert the sun's energy into biomass"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_web (producers convert solar energy into biomass through photosynthesis)

F3 Consumers (heterotrophs) cannot make their own food; they depend on other living things for it, and animals eat their food. https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_web ("Consumers, which are heterotrophs," depend on other organisms for food); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-chain/

F4 A herbivore is an animal that eats plants (and algae and other producers). Deer, rabbits, elephants and caterpillars are herbivores; rabbits are strict herbivores that live on grass, leafy plants, bark and twigs. https://grokipedia.com/page/Herbivore ("A herbivore is an animal that primarily consumes plant material"; "elephants, deer, rabbits, and caterpillars"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Rabbit ("Rabbits are strict herbivores"; "grasses, forbs, bark, and twigs"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/5th-grade/ ("Primary consumers are herbivores, which eat plants, algae, and other producers")

F5 A carnivore is an animal that eats other animals. Snakes eat only animals (obligate carnivores); hawks and owls are carnivorous birds; adult frogs are mostly carnivorous and eat insects. https://grokipedia.com/page/Carnivore (obtains most of its energy "by consuming the flesh, tissues, or products of other animals"; hawks, eagles, owls); https://grokipedia.com/page/Snake ("Snakes are obligate carnivores, relying exclusively on animal prey"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Frog ("Adult frogs are predominantly carnivorous, capturing prey such as insects"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-chain/ ("carnivores (animals that eat other animals)")

F6 An omnivore is an animal that eats both plants and animals. People and bears are omnivores. https://grokipedia.com/page/Omnivore ("regularly consumes a variety of food sources, including both plant and animal matter"; humans; the Kodiak bear eats "berries, fish, and mammals"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-chain/ ("omnivores (animals that eat both plants and animals)"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ ("Bears are omnivores, too")

F7 Decomposers, such as fungi and bacteria, are a third role in a food web, often drawn beside the main chain. (Named as a role only; their work belongs to decomposers-and-recycling-matter.) https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_chain (decomposers are "often depicted outside the main linear chain"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-chain/ ("Decomposers like fungi and bacteria complete the food chain"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ls2-1-ecosystems-interactions-energy-and-dynamics (LS2.A: "Some organisms, such as fungi and bacteria, break down dead organisms")

F8 Fungi, such as mushrooms, are not plants and are not producers: they have no chlorophyll, cannot carry out photosynthesis, and take in food from other matter. They are decomposers. https://grokipedia.com/page/Fungus ("Unlike plants, fungi lack chlorophyll and cannot perform photosynthesis"; "primary decomposers in terrestrial ecosystems"; "distinct from plants and animals"; Linnaeus once placed them among the plants)

F9 Mosses are small green land plants. Their green, leafy part carries out photosynthesis, and they grow in damp, shady places such as forest floors and rock surfaces. Students meet mosses as non-flowering plants from 3rd grade. https://grokipedia.com/page/Moss ("Mosses are small, non-vascular land plants"; "the dominant, photosynthetic phase that forms the visible green mats"; "forest floors, rock surfaces"); https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/Export/1660 (Florida SC.3.L.15.2: classify flowering and nonflowering plants, such as ferns and mosses that produce spores)

F10 A food chain shows who eats whom: one path along which energy and nutrients pass through an ecosystem. A simple chain: grass is eaten by rabbits, which are eaten by foxes. https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-chain/ ("The food chain describes who eats whom in the wild"; "a possible pathway that energy and nutrients can follow"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_chain ("a linear sequence of organisms in an ecosystem through which energy and nutrients are transferred"; "grass consumed by rabbits, which are then eaten by foxes")

F11 In a food chain or web the arrows point from the living thing that is eaten to the one that eats it: the direction the energy moves. https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_web (interactions "depicted as edges pointing from prey to predators"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_chain (arrows run "from producers at the base to top consumers"); https://www.generationgenius.com/blog/difference-between-food-webs-food-chains/ ("The arrows show the direction the energy is transferred")

F12 A food web is all the interconnected and overlapping food chains in one ecosystem. Food webs are drawn because most animals eat more than one thing. https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ ("A food web consists of all the food chains in a single ecosystem"; "All of the interconnected and overlapping food chains in an ecosystem make up a food web"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_web ("encompasses multiple overlapping chains"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_chain (a food web is "a more realistic network of multiple interlocking food chains"); https://www.generationgenius.com/blog/difference-between-food-webs-food-chains/ ("Most animals eat more than one thing"; "A food web is a model made of intersecting food chains")

F13 Other diagrams and words that are not a food web: an ecological pyramid (such as a pyramid of energy) shows the amounts of living things, living matter or energy at each feeding level (a 7th-grade topic); a habitat is the place and conditions where a living thing lives; a life cycle is the series of stages a living thing goes through as it grows and reproduces; a food pyramid, in nutrition, is a guide to how much of each food group a healthy diet holds. https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_pyramid_(nutrition) ("a visual guide depicting recommended proportions and servings of food groups"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_pyramid ("the relative amounts of organisms, their biomass, or the energy present at successive trophic levels"; "pyramid of energy"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Habitat ("the specific set of resources and environmental conditions in a given area"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Biological_life_cycle ("the sequence of developmental events that an organism undergoes")

F14 A change to one member of a food web spreads through it. If the frogs in a web die of a disease, the animals that ate frogs lose food, and the insects the frogs ate increase and then eat more plants. When one link is weakened, others are too: when sea otters decline, the sea urchins they eat multiply and wipe out the kelp. https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12b-predicting-changes-in-ecosystems (TEKS 5.12B examples: frogs die from disease, snakes lose food, insects increase and eat more plants); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ ("When one link in the food web is threatened, some or all of the links are weakened or stressed"; the sea otter, urchin and kelp example); https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_web ("The removal of top predators can cascade through the system")

F15 When plant-eaters grow in number, they eat the plants faster than the plants can grow back, and the plants dwindle (overgrazing). Food and eaters are linked both ways: "when there are fewer plants, the number of herbivores usually decreases." https://grokipedia.com/page/Overgrazing (grazing "removes plant cover faster than it can recover"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/5th-grade/ ("when there are fewer plants, the number of herbivores usually decreases"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ (urchins multiply and devastate the kelp)

F16 Ocean food chains also begin with producers. Most of the open ocean's producers are phytoplankton: microscopic algae that float in the sunlit upper water and form the base of ocean food webs. Tiny drifting animals (zooplankton) graze on them. https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/phyto.html ("Phytoplankton are microscopic marine algae"; they "contain chlorophyll and require sunlight"; they "float in the upper part of the ocean, where sunlight penetrates the water"; "the base of several aquatic food webs"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Phytoplankton ("Phytoplankton form the base of aquatic food webs as primary producers"; "largely confined to the euphotic zone"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Zooplankton (small, mostly heterotrophic drifting animals, primary consumers of phytoplankton); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ ("Algae and plankton are the main producers in marine ecosystems")

F17 The open ocean is miles deep: its average depth is about 3,682 metres (12,080 feet). Enough sunlight for photosynthesis reaches only the top 100 to 200 metres or so. Seaweed (large algae) needs sunlight and grows attached to rock in that sunlit zone, down to about 268 metres at the very deepest. https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/oceandepth.html ("The average depth of the ocean is about 3,682 meters (12,080 feet)"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Aphotic_zone (below the photic zone "photosynthesis negligible"; boundary "at depths of 100 to 200 meters in clear open-ocean waters"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Seaweed (seaweed "perform[s] photosynthesis using sunlight"; on "hard substrates such as bedrock, boulders"; records "up to 268 meters")

F18 The feeding links used in the stems are real: grasshoppers mostly eat grasses; frogs eat insects and are eaten by snakes and herons; deer browse leaves and twigs and eat acorns; most caterpillars eat leaves; deer mice eat seeds and arthropods, caterpillars among them; owls eat mice and birds; rabbits eat grass and are caught by hawks and foxes. https://grokipedia.com/page/Grasshopper ("primarily herbivorous insects"; "predominantly include grasses"; frogs and birds as predators); https://grokipedia.com/page/Frog (insects; "birds (such as herons)", snakes); https://grokipedia.com/page/White-tailed_deer ("twigs, leaves, buds, and bark"; "mast such as acorns"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Caterpillar ("Most caterpillars are herbivorous, voraciously consuming leaves"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peromyscus_maniculatus ("Deer mice are omnivorous"; seeds and arthropods including caterpillars); https://grokipedia.com/page/Owl ("small mammals like mice, voles, and rats"; "birds, insects"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Rabbit (red foxes; "hawks such as the red-tailed hawk")

F19 The grade's standards: NGSS 5-LS2-1 (LS2.A: "Organisms are related in food webs in which some animals eat plants for food and other animals eat the animals that eat plants"; fungi and bacteria break down dead organisms) and TEKS 5.12B ("Predict how changes in the ecosystem affect the cycling of matter and flow of energy in a food web"). https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ls2-1-ecosystems-interactions-energy-and-dynamics; https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12b-predicting-changes-in-ecosystems

## Misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Arrows drawn backwards: "Students often reverse the direction of the arrow to show that an animal is eating a plant or other animal rather than using the arrow to show the direction of energy flow." https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Research/lessons_CreatingChainsEducatorHandout.pdf (Snapshot Wisconsin educator handout, teaching tips)

M2 Mushrooms are plants: 5th graders say "Fungus is a type of plant" (Seyda 2021). https://wordpressua.uark.edu/scimap/2023/06/09/5th-grade-students-misunderstandings-and-misconceptions-about-fungi-2

M3 Changes stay local, or move all one way (Griffiths and Grant 1985, high-school food webs): "a change in one population will only affect another population if they are directly related"; "A change in the size of a prey population has no effect on its predator population"; "If the size of one population in a food web is altered, other populations in the web will be altered in the same way." https://wordpressua.uark.edu/scimap/?p=336; also https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.12b-predicting-changes-in-ecosystems ("If one species disappears, only the species that ate it are affected")

M4 Only the big animal at the top counts as the meat-eater. No study states this one in these words; the nearest is the Annenberg Learner list, where children fix on the top predator and see the links below it as there to serve it (items 8 and 9). Used as a lure because it is plainly wrong: the frog in grass, grasshopper, frog, snake eats an animal, so it is a carnivore too (F5). https://arboretum.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Energy-flow-misconceptions.pdf

M5 Plankton are all one kind: the drifting plankton include both producers (phytoplankton, algae) and consumers (zooplankton, animals), and children lump them together. Supported by F16's two kinds; no misconception study found, so used only as a lure that is plainly wrong (animals are consumers, F3).

M6 Plants are untouched by a change among the animals because they make their own food. No study states this one in these words; it is the plant-side form of M3 (a change reaches only some members). Used as a lure because F15 shows it is wrong: more plant-eaters leave fewer plants.

## Revision facts (2026-11-26, the one revision round)

F20 In ecology, "food pyramid" is sometimes another name for the ecological (energy) pyramid, which shows the biomass or bioproductivity at each feeding level; so the words have a nutrition meaning and a science meaning, and neither is a food web. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_pyramid ("also trophic pyramid, Eltonian pyramid, energy pyramid, or sometimes food pyramid"; "designed to show the biomass or bioproductivity at each trophic level in an ecosystem"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_pyramid

F21 Some seaweed floats free in the open ocean: pelagic Sargassum forms dense floating mats in places such as the Sargasso Sea and makes food by photosynthesis, but it is a minor producer next to phytoplankton, which do most of the ocean's primary production. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sargassum ("form dense, floating mats in regions like the Sargasso Sea"; "a local source of primary production in oligotrophic waters"; "their global sequestration impact remains minor compared to phytoplankton"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Phytoplankton (the majority of oceanic primary production)

F22 An eater is not always bigger than what it eats: gray wolves (adults about 27 to 65 kg) hunt in packs and kill moose, whose cows weigh about 200 to 490 kg and bulls about 380 to 700 kg. https://grokipedia.com/page/Moose ("Moose face predation primarily from large carnivores, with wolves (Canis lupus) and bears"; "Wolves typically hunt in packs"; "Bulls weigh 380-700 kg on average, while cows range from 200-490 kg"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Wolf ("males typically weighing 32-65 kg and females 27-45 kg"; they "hunt large ungulates such as deer, moose, and bison")

F23 Correction to the wording "eats only plants" for deer and caterpillars: rabbits are strict herbivores, but deer have been filmed deliberately eating songbird nestlings, and only most caterpillars (not all) eat leaves. Contexts say "a rabbit eats nothing else; deer and most caterpillars are herbivores too." https://www.usgs.gov/publications/white-tailed-deer-odocoileus-virginianus-predation-grassland-songbird-nestlings (Pietz and Granfors 2000, American Midland Naturalist: deer raided grassland songbird nests in North Dakota; "deer predations clearly were deliberate"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Rabbit ("Rabbits are strict herbivores"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Caterpillar ("Most caterpillars are herbivorous")
