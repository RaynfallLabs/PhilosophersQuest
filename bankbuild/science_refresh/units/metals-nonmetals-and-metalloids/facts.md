# Metals, Nonmetals and Metalloids (T2, 6th grade, physical strand)

Sources were read on 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; a second source is given
for anything a child could be taught wrong. Map line G6.P3 (TEKS 6.6C).

## The periodic table and its symbols

F1. The periodic table holds 118 confirmed elements.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Periodic_table ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_element

F2. Each element has a chemical symbol of one or two letters; the first letter is always a capital and any second letter is lowercase.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_symbol ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_symbol

F3. Many symbols come straight from the element's name (H hydrogen, C carbon, O oxygen); symbols are "generally based on the English or Latin name of the element".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_element ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_symbol

F4. Some symbols come from the element's Latin name, so they do not match the English one: Fe from ferrum (iron), Au from aurum (gold), Ag from argentum (silver), Pb from plumbum (lead, the root of the English word "plumbing"), Na from the Neo-Latin natrium (sodium), K from kalium (potassium), Sn from stannum (tin), Hg from hydrargyrum (mercury).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_symbol ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_symbol ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_chemical_element_name_etymologies

F5. Jöns Jacob Berzelius, a Swedish chemist, introduced the modern letter symbols (articles of 1813-1814; usually dated 1814), using the first letter of each element's Latin name and a second letter where needed. Grokipedia: the symbols were drawn from Latin names "to ensure consistency across languages", and Fe "remains unchanged in all contexts" though iron has other names in other languages.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_symbol ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_element ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_symbol

F6. Element names come from many sources: Greek words (hydrogen, "water-begetter"; helium, from the Greek word for the Sun), people (einsteinium for Albert Einstein; curium for Marie and Pierre Curie) and places (americium for the Americas; germanium for Germany; polonium for Poland). Of the 118 elements, 41 have names tied to places and 19 to people. The symbols of such elements still follow their names (Es for einsteinium, Am for americium).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_chemical_element_name_etymologies ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_element

## Metals

F7. Most elements are metals: Grokipedia gives "about 94" of the 118, "approximately 80%"; Wikipedia's Nonmetal article puts nonmetals at about 15-20% of the elements. "About four in five" is the safe plain figure.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Metal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonmetal

F8. Metals sit "predominantly on the left and center" of the periodic table; nonmetals on the "upper right side"; metalloids "lie along a diagonal 'staircase' line that roughly separates metals from nonmetals".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Periodic_table ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metalloid ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metal

F9. The lanthanides and actinides are printed in two separate rows below the main body of the table. Both are metals: the actinides are "15 metallic chemical elements", and the lanthanides are among the rare earths, all metallic.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Periodic_table ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Actinide ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rare-earth_element

F10. Hydrogen is traditionally placed at the top of group 1, above lithium, but it is classed as a nonmetal.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nonmetal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Periodic_table

F11. Metals are shiny (metallic lustre), malleable ("hammered into sheets"), ductile ("drawn into wires"), and good conductors of heat and electricity. Grokipedia: "Ductility enables metals to be drawn into wires, while malleability allows them to be hammered into sheets."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Metal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal

F12. At room temperature every metal is a solid except mercury, "the only metallic element that remains liquid at standard room temperature and pressure".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Metal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mercury_(element)

F13. Gold is the most malleable metal. One gram can be beaten into a sheet of about one square metre; gold leaf, made by goldbeaters hammering gold again and again, is about 0.1-0.2 micrometres thick, thin enough to be translucent ("semi-transparent"; the light that passes through looks greenish-blue). Grokipedia Gold_leaf credits gold's "ductility and malleability" together, but by F11's definitions the hammering into sheets is malleability.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gold ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gold_leaf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold

## Nonmetals

F14. There are 17 nonmetals. Eleven are gases at room temperature (hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine and the six noble gases, among them helium and neon); bromine is the one liquid; five are solids (carbon, phosphorus, sulfur, selenium, iodine).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nonmetal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonmetal

F15. So every element that is a gas at room temperature is a nonmetal: metals are solids except liquid mercury (F12), and metalloids are solids (F18). Not every nonmetal is a gas (F14).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nonmetal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metalloid

F16. Solid nonmetals are brittle or crumbly, not malleable or ductile, and they are poor conductors of heat and electricity. Most lack a metal's shine (carbon and sulfur "lack metallic sheen"), though there are exceptions a 6th grader is not asked about: iodine crystals have a metallic lustre, and graphite (a form of carbon) conducts electricity.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nonmetal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonmetal

F17. Sulfur is a pale (bright) yellow crystalline solid, brittle, a nonmetal with poor electrical conductivity; it is a chalcogen (group 16, on the right of the table).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sulfur ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nonmetal

## Metalloids

F18. The metalloids are usually six elements: boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium. They sit along the staircase between metals and nonmetals and have properties in between: they "generally appear metallic in luster but are brittle rather than malleable or ductile", and many are semiconductors. Wikipedia: "Metalloids usually look like metals but behave largely like nonmetals"; "shiny, brittle solids".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Metalloid ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metalloid

F19. Germanium is a grey-white metalloid that keeps a metallic lustre in air; pure germanium is crystalline and brittle. It sits in group 14, between silicon and tin, and the first transistor (Bell Laboratories, December 1947) was made with it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Germanium ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metalloid

F20. Silicon is "a hard, brittle solid with a blue-grey metallic lustre", a metalloid and a semiconductor. It is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust after oxygen, found in quartz, sand and clay.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Silicon ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metalloid

F21. A semiconductor has an electrical conductivity "between that of a conductor and an insulator". Adding small, controlled amounts of other elements (doping) changes how well it conducts, and its conduction "can be switched on or off".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Semiconductor ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor

F22. A transistor is an electronic switch (or amplifier) made of semiconductor, most often silicon; a single modern chip holds billions of them. Silicon "is the foundation of the semiconductor industry, enabling transistors, microchips".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Transistor ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metalloid ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metalloid

## Rare earth elements (map line G6.P3)

F23. The rare earth elements are 17 metals: the 15 lanthanides plus scandium and yttrium. Despite the name they are "not scarce in the Earth's crust"; they are "spread thinly as trace impurities" and seldom found concentrated.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rare-earth_element ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rare-earth_element

F24. Neodymium, iron and boron together make neodymium magnets, "the strongest type of permanent magnet available commercially"; they give strong fields in small volumes. They are used in the drive motors of electric and hybrid vehicles, the generators of wind turbines with permanent magnets, loudspeakers and headphones, computer hard disks and cordless tools.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Neodymium_magnet ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neodymium_magnet

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Every symbol comes from the English name, so iron should be I or Ir and lead L. Author's judgment (the usual surprise when the table is first met); wrong by F4.
M2. Shiny means metal, so a shiny grey metalloid should bend or flatten like one. Wikipedia Metalloid: they "usually look like metals but behave largely like nonmetals"; wrong by F18-F19.
M3. Metals fill the left half of the table and nonmetals the right half (from "metals on the left, nonmetals on the right"). Author's judgment; wrong by F7-F9: metals are about four in five and fill the middle too.
M4. Malleable and ductile mixed up (sheets against wire). Author's judgment; F11.
M5. Nonmetals are all gases. Wrong by F14: five are solids and bromine is a liquid.
M6. A metalloid is a metal and a nonmetal mixed together. Author's judgment; wrong by F18 (each metalloid is one element).
M7. Silicon, which looks like a metal, conducts like a metal; or, since sand and glass hold it, it blocks electricity like glass. Author's judgment; wrong by F20-F21.
M8. Rare earth elements are rare. Wrong by F23.

## Kept out
- The exact count of metals (94 or 95 by source) is never keyed: "about four in five" or "most" only.
- Iodine's lustre and graphite's conduction (F16) are real exceptions; no question rests on "nonmetals are never shiny" or "nonmetals never conduct".
- Who mines or trades rare earths (economics or history bank's).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F25. Silicon is not magnetic: Wikipedia's Silicon infobox gives its magnetic ordering as "diamagnetic" (molar magnetic susceptibility -3.9x10^-6 cm3/mol). Only iron, cobalt, nickel and alloys made from them, such as steel, are magnetic in normal conditions.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon ; https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/378

F26. Not every electric car motor holds a neodymium magnet: "In 2021, permanent magnet motors made up 84% of the electric car market", rising to 86% in the first half of 2022 (IDTechEx); the rest are magnet-free induction or wound-rotor motors. So "most electric car motors". Wikipedia: neodymium magnets serve as "Drive motors for hybrid and electric vehicles"; wind-turbine generators "(only those with permanent magnet excitation)", and "most wind turbines do not use permanent magnets", so "many wind turbines".
    https://www.electricvehiclesresearch.com/articles/28314/the-ev-market-doubles-down-on-permanent-magnets-despite-material-costs ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neodymium_magnet

F27. Headphones: neodymium-iron-boron magnets "are used in essentially all headphones these days" (Theodore Gray's periodic table); Wikipedia lists "Loudspeakers and headphones" among the magnets' uses. Electrostatic headphones, a small specialist kind, use no magnet, so "the speakers in most headphones".
    https://theodoregray.com/PeriodicTable/Samples/060.5/index.s6.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neodymium_magnet ; https://mynewmicrophone.com/why-how-do-headphones-use-magnets/
