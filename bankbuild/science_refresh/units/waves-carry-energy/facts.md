# Waves Carry Energy (T2, 6th grade, physical strand)

Curriculum line G6.P9 (TEKS 6.8C: how energy is transferred through
transverse and longitudinal waves). Sources were read on 2026-11-26.
Grokipedia first; a second source is given for anything a child could be
taught wrong. Not here: amplitude, frequency, wavelength and the
electromagnetic spectrum (8th grade).

## A wave carries energy, not matter

F1. A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from place to place, through a medium or through a vacuum, without the net transport of matter (Grokipedia: "transfers energy through a medium or vacuum without the net transport of matter"). OpenStax: mechanical waves "transfer energy and momentum, without transferring mass".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wave ; https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/16-1-traveling-waves

F2. Water waves carry energy, not water: as a wave passes, the water moves in a circular motion and its particles end up with no net displacement (NOAA: "Waves transmit energy, not water, across the ocean"; "water does not actually travel in waves"; Grokipedia Wind wave: "individual particles oscillate without net displacement"). In deep water the loops are nearly circular.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/wavesinocean.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Wind_wave

F3. A floating object rises and moves forward with a passing wave, then falls down and back, "ending up in the same position as before the wave came by" (NOAA Ocean Explorer). OpenStax: a water wave "causes a seagull to move up and down".
    https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/waves.html ; https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/16-1-traveling-waves

F4. The matter a wave travels through is its medium. Mechanical waves need a material medium, "such as air, water, or solids" (Grokipedia Wave); OpenStax: basic mechanical waves "require a medium".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wave ; https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/16-1-traveling-waves

## Transverse waves: crest and trough

F5. In a transverse wave the medium moves at right angles (perpendicular) to the direction the wave travels. On a stretched string or rope shaken at one end, the particles "move up and down as the wave travels along the string" (Grokipedia Transverse wave, which also describes "shaking the free end up and down to generate traveling transverse waves"). OpenStax: "the disturbance of the medium is perpendicular to the direction of propagation"; the medium moves up and down while the disturbance travels along.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Transverse_wave ; https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/16-1-traveling-waves

F6. A crest is the highest point of a wave; a trough is the lowest point (Wikipedia: "A crest point on a wave is the highest point of the wave"; "A trough is the opposite of a crest, so the minimum or lowest point of the wave"). Grokipedia: a trough marks "the lowest position"; on water, "crests appear as elevated ridges". Transverse waves show "crests and troughs" (Grokipedia Transverse wave).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Crest_and_trough ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crest_and_trough ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Transverse_wave

## Longitudinal waves: compression and rarefaction

F7. In a longitudinal (compression) wave the particles of the medium move back and forth parallel to the direction the wave travels (Grokipedia: they "oscillate parallel to the direction of wave propagation"; OpenStax: "the disturbance is parallel to the direction of propagation"). A coiled spring such as a Slinky pushed and pulled along its length carries one (Grokipedia: "coiled springs like a slinky").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Longitudinal_wave ; https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/16-1-traveling-waves

F8. A longitudinal wave is a train of compressions, "regions of increased particle density", and rarefactions, regions of "decreased density" where particles "are spaced farther apart than in their equilibrium state" (Grokipedia Longitudinal wave; Grokipedia Rarefaction: "the opposite of compression"). BSCS: "areas of highly compressed matter (compressions) and areas of uncompressed matter (rarefactions)".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Longitudinal_wave ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rarefaction ; https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_1st/sound/gr1.sound_common_student_ideas.pdf

F9. Sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave of alternating compressions and rarefactions; the particles vibrate back and forth and "return to their original positions after each cycle" while the wave moves on (Grokipedia Sound). BSCS: particles "travel a short distance with the wave and then return to their rest position"; "They don't travel the full distance of the wave."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sound ; https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_1st/sound/gr1.sound_common_student_ideas.pdf

F10. A spring can carry either kind: pushed and pulled along its length it carries a longitudinal wave (F7); moved up and down it carries a transverse one (OpenStax figure: "the spring moves vertically up and down, while the wave propagates horizontally to the right").
    https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/16-1-traveling-waves ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Longitudinal_wave

F11. Mechanical transverse waves "cannot travel through the bulk of fluids like gases or liquids" (Grokipedia Transverse wave), so sound in air cannot be a wave of air moving up and down across its path; it is longitudinal.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Transverse_wave ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sound

## Sound needs a medium; light does not

F12. Sound "requires a material medium" and "cannot propagate in a vacuum" (Grokipedia Sound). Smithsonian: "Sound does not travel in space"; "Sound requires molecules to travel through".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sound ; https://howthingsfly.si.edu/ask-an-explainer/how-does-sound-travel-space

F13. The bell-jar demonstration: a ringing bell (or a ticking watch) is put inside a glass jar and the air is pumped out; the sound grows faint and "eventually inaudible, even though the bell continues to vibrate visibly" (Grokipedia Bell jar). Robert Boyle did it in 1660 with a ticking watch in the receiver of his air pump. (Boyle is not named in any question: the grade's curriculum does not name him.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Bell_jar

F14. Light is a transverse wave: it consists of "oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to its direction of propagation" (Grokipedia Light). No matter moves in it. Grokipedia Transverse wave lists "electromagnetic waves, such as light and radio waves" as transverse.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Light ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Transverse_wave ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Wave

F15. Electromagnetic waves such as light "do not require a medium and can travel through vacuum" (Grokipedia Wave; OpenStax: they "do not require a medium"). Light travels through vacuum at 299,792,458 metres per second (Grokipedia Light). (Speed not used in a question.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wave ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Light ; https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/16-1-traveling-waves

## Sound through solids and liquids

F16. Longitudinal waves "can propagate through solids, liquids, and gases" (Grokipedia Longitudinal wave). Sound is "generally faster in liquids and solids than in gases": about 343 m/s in air at 20 degrees Celsius, about 1480 m/s in water, 5000-6000 m/s in steel (Grokipedia Sound).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Longitudinal_wave ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sound

F17. In water sound travels at around 1500 m/s, "roughly four times faster than in air", and "travels efficiently over long distances" (Grokipedia Underwater acoustics).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Underwater_acoustics

F18. A hydrophone is "an underwater microphone designed to detect and record sounds propagating through water"; hydrophones pick up "engine and propeller noises" and machinery vibrations of vessels (Grokipedia Hydrophone).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrophone

## Background only (not asked)

F19. Earthquakes send out P waves, which are longitudinal, and S waves, which are transverse (Grokipedia Wave).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wave

F20. A string telephone works because the can's bottom turns sound into "longitudinal mechanical vibrations" that travel along the taut string (Wikipedia Tin can telephone).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin_can_telephone

## Misconceptions students of this grade hold

M1. Matter moves along with the wave: students think the water (or air) travels with the wave to the far side (BSCS common student idea 5, "Matter moves along with the sound waves"; physics-education research reports a "particle model" in which sound "travels as a particle-like object", and a "particle pulses model", Hrepic, Zollman and Rebello 2003, citing Wittmann et al. 2002).
    https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_1st/sound/gr1.sound_common_student_ideas.pdf ; https://perg.phys.ksu.edu/papers/2003/SoundMental.pdf

M2. Sound moves between the particles of matter, through the empty space between them (BSCS common student idea 4).
    https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_1st/sound/gr1.sound_common_student_ideas.pdf

M3. Sound is a thing of its own, "a self-standing entity, different from the medium" (the "entity" model, Hrepic, Zollman and Rebello 2003), or a substance stored inside the object that makes it (BSCS common student idea 1). This feeds the belief that sound can cross empty space.
    https://perg.phys.ksu.edu/papers/2003/SoundMental.pdf ; https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_1st/sound/gr1.sound_common_student_ideas.pdf

M4. Mixing up the two kinds: thinking sound waves in air go up and down like a rope wave (the transverse picture drawn for every wave), or mixing up crests and troughs with compressions and rarefactions. This one is the author's reading of the unit's two vocabularies, not taken from a misconceptions study; the sources below are for the correction only (F9, F11: sound in air is longitudinal).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Transverse_wave ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sound
