## The old idea

F1. Spontaneous generation is the old, now-abandoned idea that living things can arise directly from lifeless matter, with no parents. Aristotle gave one of the earliest systematic accounts (On the Generation of Animals): some insects, worms and eels arise from decaying matter, mud or moisture. Maggots in dead flesh and frogs in mud after floods were stock examples. The idea was accepted for about two thousand years and lasted into the 19th century.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-1-spontaneous-generation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_generation

F2. Jan Baptist van Helmont (1579-1644), a Flemish chemist and physician, gave a recipe for making mice: a dirty shirt or rags with grains of wheat, left for 21 days (OpenStax: "rags and wheat kernels left in an open container for 3 weeks"). OpenStax notes that those conditions simply gave mice food and shelter. His book Ortus medicinae was printed after his death, in 1648.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Jan_Baptist_van_Helmont ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-1-spontaneous-generation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_generation

F3. Athanasius Kircher (1602-1680), a German Jesuit priest and scholar, affirmed spontaneous generation (Mundus Subterraneus, 1664-65). Redi reports Kircher's recipe: soak dead flies' bodies in honey water ("acqua melata"), set them on a copper plate over the warm heat of ashes, and tiny worms, visible only through a microscope, appear and grow wings into new flies. Redi wrote that the honey water serves only to invite the living flies more easily ("non serva ad altro che ad invitar più facilmente le viventi"); from dead bodies on which flies have left their young "nasceranno i vermi, e da' vermi le mosche" (worms will be born, and from the worms flies).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Athanasius_Kircher ; Redi 1668, djvu/33 and djvu/34

## Redi, his book, his question

F4. Francesco Redi (born 18 February 1626 in Arezzo, died 1 March 1697 in Pisa) was a physician, naturalist and poet, head physician to the Medici Grand Dukes of Tuscany (Ferdinando II, then Cosimo III) at their court in Florence, and a member of the Accademia del Cimento, one of the first scientific societies. (One reference title gives 1698 for his death; the body of each source gives 1697.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Francesco_Redi ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Redi ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/francesco-redi/ ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/francesco-redi-1626-1698

F5. Redi published his experiments in 1668 as Esperienze intorno alla generazione degl'insetti (Experiments on the Generation of Insects), written as a letter to Carlo Dati; an enlarged edition followed in 1688, and Mab Bigelow's English translation (1909) is of the 1688 edition. Redi's own rule, in the translation's words, was that "belief would be vain without the confirmation of experiment."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Redi ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/francesco-redi/ ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/francesco-redi-1626-1698 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Francesco_Redi

F6. Redi first watched rotting flesh closely. One June he shut dead snakes in a box: maggots appeared, ate the flesh to the bones, then lay still and took an egg-like shape (Redi called these cases "uova", eggs; modern accounts call them pupae), and about eight days later flies came out of them (others took fourteen days or more). Linda Hall: "he carefully followed the worms through their entire life cycles, from worms to pupae to flies." He also found eggs on meat and fish, like the clusters that flies leave on flesh, and saw maggots hatch from such eggs.
    Redi 1668, main text pp. 16-20 and djvu/27, djvu/29 ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/francesco-redi/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Francesco_Redi

F7. Redi's doubt, in his own account: in every trial, flies of the same kinds as those that later hatched had been seen settling on the meat before it bred maggots, so he asked whether all the maggots in meat come only from the seed of flies ("se per fortuna tutti i bachi delle carni dal seme delle sole mosche derivassero"), and he said the doubt would stay unresolved without an experiment. Linda Hall: "He surmised that perhaps flies laid the eggs that produced the worms." He noted that hunters, butchers and housewives already kept meat in fly-safes or under white cloths in summer, and he quoted Homer's Iliad, where Achilles fears flies will breed worms in the dead Patroclus's wounds.
    Redi 1668, djvu/29 and djvu/30 ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/francesco-redi/

## The flasks

F8. In mid-July Redi put a snake, some river fish, four small eels from the Arno ("quattro anguillette d'Arno") and a cut of milk-fed veal into four wide-mouthed flasks and closed the mouths very tightly with paper and string. He put the same things into four more flasks and left their mouths open. (Grokipedia and Wikipedia say "jars"; Redi wrote "fiaschi", flasks.)
    Redi 1668, djvu/30 and djvu/31 ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/francesco-redi/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Francesco_Redi

F9. Before long the fish and meat in the open flasks were full of maggots, and flies were seen going in and out of them at will; in the sealed flasks he never saw a maggot born ("ne fiaschi serrati non ho mai veduto nascer' un baco"), though many months passed ("ancorchè sieno scorsi molti mesi"). Now and then a small maggot was found on the outside of a sealed flask, at the lip of the neck, trying to find a way in.
    Redi 1668, djvu/31 ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/francesco-redi/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation

F10. The flesh in the sealed flasks still changed: the fish (all but the bones) turned to a thick, cloudy liquid that slowly cleared, with melted fat on top, and the eels swelled and bubbled; but the snake stayed almost whole, keeping its colours, as if sealed in the day before. CAUTION: so "all the sealed meat rotted" overstates it; no question says it. Grokipedia: the sealed jars "produced no maggots, though their contents still decayed".
    Redi 1668, djvu/31 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation

F11. Redi's flask experiments are noted as among the first to use a set of controls (Linda Hall). Sources differ on which jar to CALL the control: Linda Hall names the sealed flasks; many classroom accounts treat the open jars, left in the ordinary way, as the comparison or control group. What all agree on: the open and sealed flasks held the same meats at the same time, so the covering was the only difference, and the open flasks showed that this meat, under those conditions, did breed maggots.
    https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/francesco-redi/ ; https://www.mun.ca/biology/scarr/4270_Redi_experiment.html ; https://elephango.com/index.cfm/pg/k12learning/lcid/11779/Life_Comes_From_Life

## The gauze

F12. The objection: the sealed flasks had kept the air from getting in or out, so a critic could say the shut-out air, not the missing flies, stopped the maggots. Redi set out to remove that objection: he put meat and fish in a much larger vessel so the air could circulate, covered with a very fine Naples gauze ("sottilissimo velo di Napoli") so that air could get in, and set it inside a box also covered with gauze. Wikipedia: he covered jars with "fine gauze so that only air could get into them".
    Redi 1668, djvu/36 and djvu/37 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Redi ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Francesco_Redi

F13. Under the gauze no maggot ever appeared on the meat or fish. Flies buzzed round the vessel, drawn by the smell, and landed on the outer gauze, where some left maggots ("vi partorivano i bachi", gave birth to maggots there); a few maggots worked into the gauze's tiny holes and had to be picked off before they reached the vessel. OpenStax: maggots were found on the gauze; Grokipedia: flies gathered on the outside, but none reached the meat.
    Redi 1668, djvu/37 ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-1-spontaneous-generation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Francesco_Redi

F14. Redi's further checks: dead flies put in a sealed vessel bred nothing, while maggots appeared in an open one and grew into flies like the dead ones; maggots he had killed, shut in a closed vessel, bred nothing; pieces of meat buried in earth for many weeks never bred maggots. CAUTION: the sealed-vessel checks share the sealed flasks' air problem, so no question keys what they "ruled out".
    Redi 1668, djvu/32 and djvu/33 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Redi

F15. Redi's conclusion: no dead animal breeds worms ("Non invermina adunque... animale alcuno, che morto sia"); the maggots come from the seed that flies leave on the flesh. Wikipedia calls him the first to challenge spontaneous generation "by demonstrating that maggots come from eggs of flies." His view is often summed up as "omne vivum ex vivo" (all life comes from life); William Harvey's earlier motto (1651) was "omnia ex ovo" (everything from eggs).
    Redi 1668, main text p. 36 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Redi ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_generation

F16. A maggot is the larva of a fly. Female flies lay eggs in moist, rich places such as decaying flesh; in warm weather the eggs hatch in about 8 to 20 hours. Maggots later pupate, and the adult fly breaks out of the hard case (the puparium). Flesh flies (Sarcophaga) deposit live maggots instead of eggs, which fits what Redi saw on the gauze.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Maggot

## What the jars did not settle

F17. Redi's work covered only organisms big enough to see, so it left open whether microscopic life arises spontaneously (Grokipedia lists "assuming Redi ended the debate" as a misconception). Redi himself was open to the idea that the wasps and flies that come out of galls on oaks, willows and mulberries were produced by the living trees (Linda Hall; CAUTION: Grokipedia's Redi page says the opposite, so the galls are not used in any question).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/francesco-redi/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Francesco_Redi

F18. In the years after Redi's book, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek's microscopes revealed tiny organisms in water and infusions (broths), and some took them as evidence of spontaneous origin, so the debate went on (Leeuwenhoek himself held that microbes come from other life). (Leeuwenhoek is keyed in the T2 microscope unit; here he is not named or asked.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-1-spontaneous-generation

F19. John Needham, an English naturalist, microscopist and Roman Catholic priest, boiled mutton broth briefly in the 1740s (Grokipedia's Needham page: 1748; its spontaneous generation page: 1745), sealed it in flasks, and days later found tiny moving organisms in it under the microscope. Wikipedia: "Believing that boiling would kill all living things," he showed that sealed broths still clouded; OpenStax: he boiled the broth "hoping to kill all preexisting microbes". He credited a "vegetative force" (a life force) and argued for spontaneous generation. CAUTION: the sources differ on whether he corked the flasks hot or let the broth cool first, so no question gives the sealing detail.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/John_Needham ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Needham ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_generation ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-1-spontaneous-generation

F20. Lazzaro Spallanzani (1729-1799), an Italian Catholic priest and biologist, answered Needham in 1765: he boiled broths for up to an hour or more, sealed the flasks by melting their glass necks shut, and nothing grew; when he broke the seals, tiny life soon appeared. Needham replied that the long boiling had destroyed the vegetative force. OpenStax: Needham likely did not boil his broth long enough.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lazzaro_Spallanzani ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-1-spontaneous-generation

F21. Louis Pasteur's swan-neck flask experiments of the 1860s ended spontaneous generation's standing in science, nearly two hundred years after Redi's book. (Pasteur belongs to the T5 germ unit; mentioned here only in passing.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-1-spontaneous-generation

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. That rotting meat turns into maggots, and rags and wheat into mice: spontaneous generation itself, the respectable wrong theory of two thousand years (F1, F2).

M2. That the sealed flasks alone settled it. A sealed flask shuts out air as well as flies, which is why the gauze was needed (F12).

M3. That Redi's jars disproved spontaneous generation for all life. They tested only creatures big enough to see; microbes reopened the question (F17, F18; Grokipedia lists this misconception).

M4. That maggots are worms of their own kind, living in soil or inside bodies. A maggot is a young fly (F16); Redi's own word was "vermi", worms.

M5. That a control is the group "where nothing happens", or a spare set, or a repeat. Redi's open flasks were the comparison that showed this meat could breed maggots (F11).

M6. That sealing meat keeps it fresh. Most of Redi's sealed fish and eels broke down with no maggots (F10); only the snake stayed whole.

M7. Retelling caution: many accounts say flies "laid eggs on the gauze". Redi wrote that flies left maggots there (flesh flies bear live young, F16). No question says eggs were laid on the gauze.
