## Leeuwenhoek and his lenses

F1. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was a draper (a cloth merchant) in Delft, in the Netherlands, who learned to grind lenses and made his own simple microscopes. Robert Hooke's Micrographia (1665) inspired him, "a Dutch draper", to build them; he saw the book on a visit to London in 1668.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Micrographia ("inspiring Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch draper, to construct his own simple microscopes") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/leeuwenhoek.html ("He set himself up in business as a draper (a fabric merchant)")

F2. His microscope had one lens only, set in a tiny hole between two thin brass plates riveted together, about 5 cm by 2.5 cm (3 to 4 inches long); a pin held the specimen and fine screws moved and focused it, and the instrument was held close to the eye.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek ("about 5 cm by 2.5 cm, consisting of two thin brass plates riveted together") ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/leeuwenhoek.html ("using only one lens, mounted in a tiny hole in the brass plate"; "The entire instrument was only 3-4 inches long")

F3. His lenses were tiny: of the dozen that survive, most are smaller than 3 millimetres, and the best is less than 1.5 millimetres across. "For microscopes, the smaller the lens, the greater the magnification." The strongest surviving instrument magnifies 266 times, to a resolution of a little more than a micrometre; Wikipedia gives up to 275 times for the survivors, Grokipedia "up to 270 times".
    https://lensonleeuwenhoek.net/content/tiny-lenses ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek

F4. He made more than 500 lenses (Lens on Leeuwenhoek: at least 500 microscopes); fewer than ten of his microscopes survive.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek ("grinding over 500 optical lenses"; "fewer than 10 of which survive today") ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/leeuwenhoek.html

F5. The compound microscopes of his day had two or more lenses in a tube. Hooke's, the one behind Micrographia, magnified up to about 50 times; UCMP says early compound microscopes "were not practical for magnifying objects more than about twenty or thirty times natural size".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Micrographia ("achieved magnifications of up to about 50 times"; "The objective lens was plano-convex ... paired with a thinner eyepiece lens") ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/leeuwenhoek.html

F6. Why one lens beat two: the lenses of the time had flaws (aberrations) that blurred the image and fringed its edges with colour, and a compound microscope's combined lenses suffered from them, while a single lens avoided the aberrations of combined lenses. Hooke himself wrote that a single lens "will both magnifie and make some objects more distinct than any of the great Microscopes", because "the colours which do much disturb the clear vision in double Microscopes is clearly avoided".
    https://lensonleeuwenhoek.net/content/tiny-lenses (double-lens microscopes "suffered from inherent aberrations, both chromatic and, more importantly, spherical") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek ("far surpassing contemporary compound microscopes plagued by chromatic aberration") ; https://bitesizebio.com/43796/simple-and-compound-microscopes/ ("these simple microscopes didn't suffer from aberrations caused by combined lens designs") ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4360124/ (Lane 2015, quoting Hooke)

F7. Leeuwenhoek did not invent the microscope: the compound microscope is credited to Zacharias Janssen, a Dutch spectacle-maker, around 1590 (Wikipedia's cell theory page has compound microscopes appearing in Europe around 1620), and Hooke's Micrographia came out in 1665, before Leeuwenhoek's work.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Optical_microscope ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_theory

## The animalcules

F8. In a letter of 7 September 1674, Leeuwenhoek described water from a lake near Delft: green streaks "spirally wound serpent-wise" (the alga Spirogyra) and "very many little animalcules", whose "motion ... in the water was so swift, and so various upwards, downwards and round about". These were single-celled organisms (protozoa-like).
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4360124/ (Lane 2015) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek ("In 1674, van Leeuwenhoek first reported protozoan-like organisms in pond and lake water") ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-2-foundations-of-modern-cell-theory (timeline: "In 1674 van Leeuwenhoek observes single-celled organisms")

F9. He wrote in Dutch of "small animals"; his English translators made the word "animalcules". He took movement as the mark of life: the Embryo Project speaks of "his conviction that motility means life".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek (footnote: Dutch words meaning 'small animals', "translated into English as animalcules") ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/antoni-van-leeuwenhoek-1632-1723

F10. In 1676 he reported "upwards of 1 000 000 living creatures in one drop of pepper-water" (water with peppercorns steeped in it).
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4360124/

F11. In his letter of 17 September 1683 he described "a little white matter, about as thick as batter" between his teeth, which he "mixed ... several times with pure rain-water, in which there were no animalcules, and also with saliva", and saw "many very small living animalcules in the said matter, which moved very prettily".
    https://dbnl.org/tekst/leeu027alle04_01/leeu027alle04_01_0008.php (Alle de brieven, deel 4, English translation) ; https://lensonleeuwenhoek.net/content/wrote-letter-135-of-1683-09-17-francis-aston-bacteria-in-tartar ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/leeuwenhoek.html

F12. The tooth letter of 1683 "is often cited as the first unambiguous observation of bacteria"; Wikipedia lists his discovery of bacteria from the human mouth in 1683; OpenStax: "In the 1670s, van Leeuwenhoek discovered bacteria and protozoa."
    https://lensonleeuwenhoek.net/content/wrote-letter-135-of-1683-09-17-francis-aston-bacteria-in-tartar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-1-studying-cells

## Checking the claim

F13. The Royal Society's secretary, Henry Oldenburg, asked Leeuwenhoek to "acquaint us with his method of observing, that others may confirm such Observations as these"; Leeuwenhoek declined to describe his methods throughout his life.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4360124/

F14. Leeuwenhoek "sent eight testimonies from gentlemen of repute", among them "a Lutheran minister, a notary and a barrister", to back his claims; the Society read the testimonials from people in Delft in November 1677.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4360124/ ; https://www.lensonleeuwenhoek.net/node/6469 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek ("His credibility was questioned when he sent the Royal Society a copy of his first observations")

F15. The Society asked Robert Hooke in October 1677 to build a microscope strong enough to repeat the observations. Hooke failed on 1 and 8 November 1677; on 15 November, with pepper water steeped nine or ten days, he saw "great numbers of exceedingly small animals swimming to and fro" before Fellows including Christopher Wren, and the minutes record "there was no longer any doubt of Mr. LEEWENHOECK'S discovery". Leeuwenhoek was elected a Fellow in 1680.
    https://www.lensonleeuwenhoek.net/node/6469 ("Hooke's Three Tries") ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4360124/ (Hooke on his third attempt with pepper-water succeeded) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek (Hooke "replicated and confirmed Leeuwenhoek's observations") ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/leeuwenhoek.html ("In 1680 he was elected a full member of the Royal Society")

## Schleiden and Schwann

F33. Mitochondria were first described in 1857, by Albert von Kölliker, as "small granules in the muscle cells of insects", and named in 1898 by Carl Benda: Schwann in the 1830s could not have looked for them. Animal cells have no chloroplasts and no large central vacuole (the pilot cells unit's F17, F19).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mitochondrion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chloroplast ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Vacuole

F16. Matthias Schleiden, a German botanist (trained first in law), concluded in 1838 that all plant tissues are made of cells. Plant cells were "relatively easy" to see "because plant cells are clearly separated by their thick cell walls".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Matthias_Jakob_Schleiden ("all plant tissues consist of cells or derivatives thereof") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_theory ("all plant structures are composed of cells") ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-2-foundations-of-modern-cell-theory

F17. Schleiden held the nucleus (which he called the cytoblast) to be the most important part of the cell. Dining with Schwann, "their conversation turned on the nuclei of plant and animal cells"; Schwann "remembered seeing similar structures in the cells of the notochord", and the resemblance "was confirmed without delay by both observers". The Scientist dates the dinner to 1837 in Berlin; the details of the story vary between sources.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Schwann ; https://www.the-scientist.com/sketching-out-cell-theory-circa-1837-38927 ; http://cosmovisions.com/Williams040505.htm (Henry Smith Williams, A History of Science: Schleiden "came to believe that the nucleus is really the most important portion of the cell")

F18. Schwann reasoned that "the nucleus should also be found in the ultimate particles of animal tissues" if plant and animal tissues were alike; his microscope work, especially on embryonic tissue, found nuclei in abundance (Williams).
    http://cosmovisions.com/Williams040505.htm ; https://www.the-scientist.com/sketching-out-cell-theory-circa-1837-38927

F19. Theodor Schwann, a German physiologist, extended the idea to animals in his book of 1839 (Mikroskopische Untersuchungen ...), showing that animal tissues, such as cartilage and the notochord, consist of cells and cell products: "All living things are composed of cells and cell products." MBL: "Matthias Schleiden (with plants) and Theodor Schwann (with animals) declared that plants and animals are all made of cells."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Theodor_Schwann ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Schwann ; https://mbl.edu/research/research-centers/eugene-bell-center/featured-projects/seeing-cells-exhibit/theory-cells-basic-units-life

F20. Both men were wrong about where new cells come from. Schleiden "believed that cells formed through crystallization, rather than cell division" (free-cell formation, which "proved erroneous"); Schwann "hypothesized that living cells formed in ways similar to the formation of crystals", out of a precursor substance.
    https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-2-foundations-of-modern-cell-theory ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Matthias_Jakob_Schleiden ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Schwann ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Theodor_Schwann

## Remak and Virchow

F21. Robert Remak published in 1852 "convincing evidence that cells are derived from other cells as a result of cell division". He had observed red blood cells from chicken embryos in various stages of division, and the division of frog's eggs just after fertilisation.
    https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-2-foundations-of-modern-cell-theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Remak ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_theory ("Remak published observations in 1852 on cell division") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rudolf_Virchow ("Remak's 1852 documentation of cell division in embryonic and pathological tissues")

F22. Remak's work was questioned and "went largely unnoticed". Remak, a Jew, was repeatedly denied a full professorship.
    https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/rudolf-carl-virchow-1821-1902 ("Robert Remak ... had already come to this conclusion in 1852, though his publication went largely unnoticed") ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-2-foundations-of-modern-cell-theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Remak

F23. Rudolf Virchow, a German doctor (a pathologist), published the Latin aphorism omnis cellula e cellula, "every cell from a cell", in 1855 and made the idea famous (OpenStax prints his wording as omnis cellula a cellula). He had at first resisted the idea of direct cell division. His essay did not credit Remak: OpenStax says "By today's standards, Virchow's editorial piece would certainly be considered an act of plagiarism", and the historian Paul Weindling writes that Virchow plagiarised Remak's notion.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rudolf_Virchow ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/rudolf-carl-virchow-1821-1902 ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-2-foundations-of-modern-cell-theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Remak ; https://mbl.edu/research/research-centers/eugene-bell-center/featured-projects/seeing-cells-exhibit/theory-cells-basic-units-life ("Remak showed that cells arise by division of other cells, then German pathologist Rudolf Virchow popularized the idea")

## Better tools, more known

F24. "Better microscopes allowed observations and drawings that led to a Cell Theory for all of life" (MBL). Grokipedia ties cell theory's 19th-century form to "rapid progress in microscopy", including the achromatic lenses Joseph Jackson Lister introduced in 1830, which "dramatically enhanced resolution".
    https://mbl.edu/research/research-centers/eugene-bell-center/featured-projects/seeing-cells-exhibit/theory-cells-basic-units-life ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Jackson_Lister

F25. Light microscopes give sharp images up to about 1,000 times (a few up to 1,500 times); bacteria are visible at about 400 times, "but not smaller objects such as viruses". Light microscopes cannot resolve detail finer than about 200 nanometres, so "individual viruses ... cannot be resolved"; "Most virus species have virions too small to be seen with an optical microscope", and viruses are about one-hundredth the size of most bacteria.
    https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/2-3-instruments-of-microscopy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Optical_microscope ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus

F26. Ernst Ruska and Max Knoll built the first prototype (transmission) electron microscope at the Technical University of Berlin in 1931. It uses a beam of electrons instead of light; the electrons' much shorter wavelength lets it resolve far finer detail (0.1 nanometres against the light microscope's 200).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Electron_microscope ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-1-studying-cells ("electron microscopes use a beam of electrons instead of a beam of light"; "much higher magnification, 100,000x")

F27. Kausche, Pfankuch and Ruska used electron microscopy in 1939 to produce the first images of the tobacco mosaic virus; Wikipedia: "the invention of the transmission electron microscope in 1931 allowed their [viruses'] complex structures to be visualised". Electron microscopy also first showed parts inside cells such as the endoplasmic reticulum (Grokipedia, 1940s).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tobacco_mosaic_virus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_theory

F28. Bacteria, cell nuclei and chloroplasts were all seen with light microscopes long before the electron microscope: Leeuwenhoek saw bacteria (F12), and bacteria are visible at about 400 times (F25). The nucleus is 5 to 20 micrometres across; Robert Brown named it in 1831, studying orchid cells, after Leeuwenhoek's "globules" in salmon blood cells. Chloroplasts, 2 to 10 micrometres across, "can be observed using a light microscope as small green oval structures in plant cells"; Hugo von Mohl described them in 1837.
    https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/2-3-instruments-of-microscopy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_nucleus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chloroplast ; https://www.the-scientist.com/sketching-out-cell-theory-circa-1837-38927

## The light microscope's parts

F29. A compound light microscope has an eyepiece (ocular lens), typically 10 times; objective lenses of about 4, 10, 40 and 100 times on a rotating nosepiece; a stage with clips that hold the slide; an illuminator (a bulb or a mirror) below the stage; a condenser; a diaphragm; and coarse and fine focus knobs.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Optical_microscope ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/2-3-instruments-of-microscopy

F30. The diaphragm "adjusts the amount and quality of light passing through the specimen" (Grokipedia); OpenStax: the amount of light is adjusted by "opening or closing a diaphragm between the condenser and the specimen". Carolina: "Decreasing the aperture of the diaphragm will reduce glare and increase contrast in the image."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Optical_microscope ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/2-3-instruments-of-microscopy ; https://knowledge.carolina.com/uncategorized/how-to-use-a-student-compound-microscope/

F31. The coarse focus knob is for large movements with the 4x and 10x objectives; the fine focus knob for small movements, especially with 40x or 100x. Carolina: "Never use the coarse-focus adjustment at high power. That can damage both the slide and the objective." AmScope: "Since the higher power objectives are very close to the slide, using the coarse focus knob can scratch the lens and crack the slide." Start with the lowest-power objective; "increasing magnification decreases the field of view". Focusing moves the stage or the tube up and down; it does not change the magnification.
    https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/2-3-instruments-of-microscopy ; https://knowledge.carolina.com/uncategorized/how-to-use-a-student-compound-microscope/ ; https://eu.amscope.com/pages/how-to-use-a-properly-use-a-microscope ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Optical_microscope (coarse knob "Moves the stage or body tube up and down for initial rough focusing")

F32. "Total magnification is calculated as the product of the eyepiece magnification and the objective lens magnification (e.g., 10x x 40x = 400x)." OpenStax: "The ocular and objective lenses work together to create a magnified image"; "The total magnification is the product of the ocular magnification times the objective magnification".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Optical_microscope ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/2-3-instruments-of-microscopy

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. More lenses must make a better microscope, so two lenses should always beat one; and a bigger lens magnifies more (it is the reverse for microscopes, F3). Author's judgment from F3, F5, F6.

M2. Total magnification is found by adding the two lenses' powers (10 + 40 = 50). Pearson's microbiology exam-prep set offers "add the magnification of the ocular and the objective" as a wrong option; no study found. Author's judgment.
    https://pearson.com/channels/microbiology/exam-prep/set/default/9-microscopes-part-1-of-2/how-do-you-calculate-the-overall-magnification-of-a-compound-microscope

M3. Focusing is thought to zoom (change the magnification); it only moves the lens and slide closer or farther (F31). Author's judgment.

M4. An organism grows because its cells get bigger, not because they divide into more cells. STEM Learning: "A common misconception is that the cells are just 'bigger' in fully developed organisms"; Keeley's NSTA probe ("Sam's Puppy") finds most body cells stay about the same size and the number of cells increases.
    https://www.stem.org.uk/lx6dok ; https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/sams-puppy

M5. Virchow alone found that cells come from cells; Remak's earlier evidence is left out of many tellings (F21-F23). Schleiden and Schwann are often thought to have held the third claim of cell theory; they did not (F20).

M6. Testimony is evidence enough: eight respected witnesses settle a claim. The Royal Society wanted the observation repeated instead (F13-F15).

M7. Leeuwenhoek invented the microscope (F7); and his "little animals" were tiny animals like mites or worms rather than single cells and bacteria (F8, F12). Author's judgment.

M8. The dinner of Schleiden and Schwann is told in many forms, with dates of 1837 or 1838: it is used only as the conversation about nuclei that Wikipedia and The Scientist both report (F17).

## Revision round (2026-11-26): facts added and checked

F34. Leeuwenhoek wrote in Dutch: his words were mainly dierken, and often diertgens or diertjes ("Dier is animal and the -ken, -gen, or -je endings are the most common form of diminutive in Dutch"), so they mean little animals. Henry Oldenburg, who translated the early letters for the Royal Society, "was the one who first translated dierkens as 'animalcules'". Grokipedia calls animalcules "his term", but it is his translators' English word, not his own.
    https://lensonleeuwenhoek.net/content/animalcules ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek (Oldenburg the first to use "animalcules" to translate the Dutch) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antonie_van_Leeuwenhoek (Oldenburg "translated Leeuwenhoek's Dutch writings into English")

F35. Mites are animals: "Mites belong to the subclass Acari within the class Arachnida, phylum Arthropoda, kingdom Animalia", typically 0.08 to 1 mm long; and "Animals are multicellular, eukaryotic organisms", so mites and worms are many-celled animals, while a bacterium is a single cell (pilot cells unit).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mite ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Animal

F36. The cytoplasm "occupies the interior of a cell, enclosed by the plasma membrane and excluding the nucleus in eukaryotic cells": it fills all the space around the nucleus, so it is no single landmark to look for.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cytoplasm

F37. Louis Pasteur "conducted a series of experiments from 1860 to 1864 using specially designed swan-neck flasks filled with nutrient broth", on whether microbes arise on their own or come from the air; around 1855 he was at Lille working on fermentation. His flasks were about where microbes come from, not about cells dividing.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Louis_Pasteur

F38. In a compound microscope the objective makes an enlarged picture (the intermediate image) inside the tube, and "The eyepiece then acts as a magnifying glass to further enlarge this intermediate image"; that is why the total magnification is the product of the two powers (F32). HyperPhysics: the objective's image "is then viewed with a short focal length eyepiece used as a simple magnifier."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Optical_microscope ; https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/geoopt/micros.html

F39. Dates the questions avoid. Remak's first paper on how blood cells form came out in 1841 (Ueber die Entstehung der Blutkoerperchen); his 1852 paper (Ueber extracellulaere Entstehung thierischer Zellen und ueber Vermehrung derselben durch Theilung) is the one OpenStax cites for "convincing evidence that cells are derived from other cells as a result of cell division". For the electron microscope, Wikipedia says the 1933 machine of Ruska and Knoll was the first to beat the light microscope's resolution, while Grokipedia says the 1931 prototype already did, at 400 times: sources differ, so no question dates it.
    https://www.historyofmedicine.com/id/2042 ; https://zientzia.eus/artikuluak/robert-remak-itzalean-aritu-zen-judua/en/ ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-2-foundations-of-modern-cell-theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscope ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electron_microscope
