## Facts

F1 Weathering breaks rock down into smaller pieces where it lies, without carrying the pieces anywhere else. https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering ("without the transport of material to other locations"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Erosion ("weathering, which breaks down materials in place without transport")

F2 Mechanical (physical) weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing what it is made of; chemical weathering changes the minerals (rainwater's carbonic acid dissolves limestone; iron in rock rusts). Weathering happens on the sea floor too. https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering ("breaks rocks into smaller fragments without altering their chemical composition"; "reaction with carbonic acid from rainwater to dissolve limestone"; "rusting iron-bearing minerals"; "Submarine weathering refers to the breakdown and alteration of rocks on the ocean floor")

F3 Erosion is the moving of broken rock and soil from one place to another, carried by water, wind, ice and gravity. It differs from weathering, which breaks rock in place without moving it. https://grokipedia.com/page/Erosion ("transported by natural agents including water, wind, ice, and gravity"; "This process differs from weathering, which breaks down materials in place without transport"); wind erosion happens on dry land: https://grokipedia.com/page/Erosion ("Wind-driven erosion, or aeolian erosion, primarily occurs in arid and semi-arid environments")

F4 Deposition is the dropping of carried rock, sand and mud: it happens when the wind, water or ice carrying them slows down or loses energy. Deltas, dunes, glacier moraines and floodplains are built by deposition. https://grokipedia.com/page/Deposition_(geology) ("This occurs when the energy of the transporting agent decreases sufficiently to allow particles to come to rest"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sediment_transport ("Deposition occurs when the fluid's velocity or turbulence decreases, allowing particles to settle under gravity")

F5 Frost wedging: water seeps into a crack in rock, and when it freezes it expands by about 9 percent, pushing the crack wider. When it thaws, water seeps a little deeper; after many freezes and thaws the rock breaks apart. It works best where temperatures swing above and below freezing often, as in mountains. https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering ("frost wedging (where water expands upon freezing in cracks)"; "increases its volume by approximately 9%"; "leading to the widening of cracks"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Frost_weathering (repeated freezing and thawing "gradually enlarge these fractures"; "most prevalent in cold, temperate, and periglacial environments where freeze-thaw cycles are frequent"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice ("Upon freezing, water expands by about 9% in volume"). Honest caveat, not taught at this grade: Grokipedia's Frost weathering page says that in nature ice segregation (water drawn to growing ice in the rock) does more than the plain swelling; both are ice growing in the crack and pushing it apart, so "water swells as it freezes and pushes the crack wider" stays true at the 5th-grade level.

F6 Ice is less dense than liquid water (about 0.917 against 1 gram per cubic centimetre), which is why ice floats. Freezing does not make water heavier: in any change of state the total weight of matter stays the same. https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice ("having a lower density than liquid water"; "causing ice to float"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ps1-2-matter-and-its-interactions (5-PS1-2: "the total weight of matter is conserved", examples "could include phase changes")

F7 Ice is soft next to rock: at its melting point ice has a Mohs hardness of 2 or less, while feldspar is 6, quartz is 7 and granite rates about 6-7. So ice does not split rock by being harder than it. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice ("At its melting point, ice has a Mohs hardness of 2 or less"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Mohs_scale (orthoclase feldspar 6, quartz 7; granite "generally rates 6-7 on the scale due to its quartz content")

F8 Root wedging: plant roots grow into cracks in rock and, as they thicken, push the cracks wider until the rock breaks. (Roots also give off acids that slowly dissolve minerals, a chemical weathering.) https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering ("Plant roots contribute to physical weathering via root wedging"; "where expanding roots exert pressure on cracks in bedrock, fragmenting rock"; roots "exuding protons" create "acidic microenvironments that promote mineral dissolution")

F9 In SOIL, plant roots do the opposite job: they bind the soil and help it resist being washed away. (This is why "roots hold things together" is a tempting wrong idea about rock.) https://grokipedia.com/page/Soil_erosion ("root systems mechanically bind soil aggregates")

F10 Wind-blown sand wears rock away by abrasion, like sandblasting. Wind moves sand mostly by saltation: grains bounce along, lifted only a few centimetres above the ground. https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering ("strong winds contribute to physical abrasion by propelling abrasive particles like sand against exposed surfaces"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Dune ("particles bounce along the surface after being lifted to heights of a few centimeters")

F11 A canyon is a deep, narrow valley with steep sides, typically carved by a river. The sand and stones the water carries scrape the river's bed and walls like sandpaper, and a deep canyon takes a very long time: the largest examples took 5 to 6 million years or more. Canyons cut by rivers are usually V-shaped in cross-section. https://grokipedia.com/page/Canyon ("a deep, narrow valley with steep sides"; abrasion wears the channel "like sandpaper"; the process "can span 5 to 6 million years or more"; "Canyons typically exhibit a V-shaped cross-section"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Erosion ("incision forms valleys over millennia"; erosion forms "river valleys, canyons, and coastal cliffs")

F12 A delta is land built at a river's mouth, where it flows into a sea or a lake. The river slows sharply as it enters the still water and drops its sand, silt and mud, which pile up and build the land outward. The name comes from the Greek letter delta, a triangle, after its shape. https://grokipedia.com/page/River_delta ("a depositional landform that emerges at the mouth of a river"; "the river's velocity decreases dramatically upon entering the receiving water body"; "derives from the uppercase Greek letter delta"; "the delta progrades seaward, expanding the land area"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Deposition_(geology) ("the deposition of sand and silt at the mouth of a river, forming deltas"); TEKS 5.10C alignment note on deltas: https://teksguide.org/teks/s510c/alignments

F13 A sand dune is a hill or ridge of sand piled up by the wind, most often in deserts and also along coasts. Dunes move as the wind carries sand from one side to the other, up to 20-30 metres a year in active dune fields. https://grokipedia.com/page/Dune ("Dunes form when loose sediment, such as sand, is transported and deposited by aeolian (wind)" processes; "most commonly associated with arid desert environments"; "also occur along coastlines"; "Dunes migrate over time as wind erodes and redeposits sand, at rates up to 20-30 meters per year in active fields")

F14 A glacier is a huge mass of ice, built up on land from packed snow, that flows slowly downhill under its own weight, centimetres to metres a day. https://grokipedia.com/page/Glacier ("a large, perennial accumulation of crystalline ice, originating on land from the compaction of snow"; "movement at rates from centimeters to meters per day")

F15 Glaciers carve U-shaped valleys, with steep walls and a broad, flat or rounded floor; the ice grinds the rock with the stones frozen into it (abrasion) and tears out blocks (plucking). River-cut valleys are V-shaped instead. https://grokipedia.com/page/U-shaped_valley ("a broad, flat floor flanked by steep, near-vertical walls"; abrasion is "scraping and grinding of bedrock by rock fragments"; plucking); https://grokipedia.com/page/Valley ("Fluvial valleys often exhibit V-shaped profiles"; "U-shaped valleys result from glacial erosion"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Glacier (glaciers deposit "debris as moraines and shaping U-shaped valleys")

F16 A landslide is rock, soil and debris moving down a slope, pulled by gravity. Some move slowly, but the fastest are over in seconds to minutes. Heavy rain soaking a slope, earthquakes and fast snowmelt set them off. https://grokipedia.com/page/Landslide ("the movement of a mass of rock, debris, earth, or other materials down a slope under the influence of gravity"; "driven directly by gravity"; fastest class "Motion observable over seconds to minutes"; "prolonged heavy rainfall saturating slopes, earthquake shaking destabilizing materials"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass_wasting ("the downslope movement of rock, soil, regolith, and other earth materials under the direct influence of gravity"; debris avalanches "over 50 km/h")

F17 Faster-flowing water can pick up and carry bigger pieces of rock: larger gravel needs faster water to set it moving, and the size a stream can move grows roughly with the square of its speed. Floods carry far more and far coarser material than ordinary flow. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sediment_transport ("larger gravels demand progressively higher velocities to initiate motion"; floods "mobilize large volumes of both bedload and suspended material"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Hjulstr%C3%B6m_curve (competence "scales approximately with the square of the velocity"; high velocities promote "transport of coarse bedload")

F18 Floods change the land fast: a flash flood can rise within hours of the rain, a single flood can scour and widen a river's channel, the biggest floods have moved boulders weighing hundreds of tons, and as flood water spreads and slows it leaves sand and silt on the floodplain. https://grokipedia.com/page/Flood ("typically developing within six hours"; "can widen channels by 10-20% in a single event"; "erratic boulders weighing hundreds of tons transported 100 kilometers"; "Periodic flooding in river floodplains deposits sediments laden with nutrients"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Deposition_(geology) ("sediment-laden floodwaters spill onto the floodplain and deposit coarser silts and sands")

F19 Rock is far denser than water (common rocks 2.5-3.0 grams per cubic centimetre, granite about 2.6-2.7, against water's 1), so a boulder sinks; it never floats in a stream, however deep. https://grokipedia.com/page/Rock_(geology) ("typically ranging from 2.5 to 3.0 g/cm3 for common igneous and metamorphic rocks"; granite "2.63-2.75 g/cm3"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice (liquid water about 1 g/cm3)

F20 Earth events can be quick or slow: volcanic explosions, earthquakes, landslides and floods happen quickly, while weathering and erosion of rock usually work slowly, over thousands to millions of years. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/2-ess1-1-earths-place-universe ("volcanic explosions and earthquakes, which happen quickly" "and erosion of rocks, which occurs slowly"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering (denudation "typically ranging from 10 to 100 mm per millennium in temperate zones"); F16, F18

F21 The curriculum: TEKS 5.10C asks 5th graders to "model and identify how changes to Earth's surface by wind, water, or ice result in the formation of landforms, including deltas, canyons, and sand dunes"; NGSS 4-ESS2-1 asks for evidence of "the effects of weathering or the rate of erosion" by water, ice, wind or vegetation, with freezing and thawing, slope, vegetation and water flow as variables. https://teksguide.org/teks/s510c/alignments; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/4-ess2-1-earths-systems

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1 Weathering and erosion are the same thing, or the words are swapped (weathering = carried away). Dove, "Student ideas about weathering and erosion", International Journal of Science Education 19(8), 1997, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0950069970190809 ; Earth Learning Idea 207, https://www.earthlearningidea.com/PDF/207_Weathering_erosion.pdf (titled "Differences between weathering and erosion; addressing common misconceptions about weathering and erosion"; the body text could not be read, so only the title is relied on)

M2 Ice is heavier than the water it froze from, or ice splits rock because it is hard and sharp. False by F6 and F7: the same water weighs the same frozen, and ice is far softer than rock; it splits rock because it takes up more room (F5).

M3 Roots hold rock together, as they hold soil (F9). In rock cracks roots pry the rock apart (F8).

M4 A canyon is a crack opened by an earthquake, or has always been there, and the river only flows in it. Julie Sexton's interviews found students credit canyons to sudden events such as earthquakes: Journal of Geoscience Education, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.5408/11-249.1 and the 2008 abstract https://scisoc.confex.com/scisoc/2008am/webprogramschedule/Paper48993.html ; Cal Poly Pomona's Common Student Ideas lists "something else, like an earthquake, cracks the land open, and then a river starts to flow in a canyon", https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_2nd/ecs/gr2.ecs_common_student_ideas.pdf

M5 Floods move boulders because rocks float in deep water, or because the storm's wind pushes them. False by F19; it is the water's speed (F17).

M6 Weathering is slow and erosion is fast (or the reverse). Each can be slow or fast (F16, F18, F20).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F22 Water freezing in cracks splits rock fastest where it freezes and thaws often: frost weathering "is most prevalent in cold, temperate, and periglacial environments where freeze-thaw cycles are frequent" and "less pronounced in continuous permafrost zones lacking seasonal thaw periods", because water cannot move freely there; each thaw "releases the pressure, permitting additional water entry". The deepest cold is not the best for it either: ice segregation, the other way ice cracks rock, works best in a "frost cracking window" of -3 to -8 degrees Celsius. https://grokipedia.com/page/Frost_weathering

F23 Not every rock sinks: pumice, a rock full of gas bubbles, has a density "typically" of 0.25 to 0.75 g/cm3, "low enough for many specimens to float on water". Ordinary rock (2.5-3.0 g/cm3, F19) is two to three times as dense as water. https://grokipedia.com/page/Pumice

F24 A river delta builds up over thousands of years, not minutes: the Mississippi's delta has had "seven distinct channels active over the last 5000 years". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_delta
