## The tilt and its fixed direction

F1. Earth's seasons are caused mainly by the tilt of its axis. The tilt is about 23.5 degrees (23.44 degrees today), measured between the spin axis and a line standing straight up from the plane of Earth's orbit (equivalently, between the equator and the orbit's plane).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Season ("The primary astronomical cause of Earth's seasons is the planet's axial tilt"; "axial tilt of 23.5 degrees relative to its orbital plane") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Axial_tilt ("the current axial tilt is 23.44 degrees") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_tilt (tilt is "the angle between an object's rotational axis and its orbital axis") ; https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/seasons/en/ ("Earth's tilted axis causes the seasons")

F2. Measured from the flat plane of the orbit instead of from straight up, the axis stands at about 66.5 degrees (90 minus 23.5). The same number marks the Arctic Circle (66.5 degrees north), the edge of the region with whole days of light or dark.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_tilt (tilt measured from the orbital axis, which is perpendicular to the orbital plane) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Axial_tilt ("beyond latitude 90° minus the obliquity angle ε where extended periods of continuous daylight or darkness prevail") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solstice (Arctic Circle at 66.5° N)

F3. As Earth travels around the Sun, its axis keeps pointing the same way in space: it stays parallel to itself all year. Its northern end points almost exactly at Polaris, the North Star, which lies about 0.7 degrees from the north celestial pole and so seems to stand still in the northern sky. (Over about 26,000 years the axis slowly swings round, and Polaris will stop being the pole star, but within a year the direction does not change.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Season ("the direction of the axis remains parallel to itself throughout its orbit") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Season ("The tilt's fixed orientation in space, unchanged during Earth's 365.25-day orbit") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Polaris ("approximately 0.7 degrees from the north celestial pole"; "appear nearly stationary in the northern sky"; precession "over a 26,000-year cycle")

F4. Because the axis keeps its direction, for part of the year the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun and has summer; six months later, on the far side of the orbit, it leans away and has winter. The Southern Hemisphere does the opposite.
    https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/seasons/en/ (a pole leaning toward the Sun gives its hemisphere more direct sunlight and summer) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Season

F5. Classroom globes are mounted on their stands with the axis tilted about 23.5 degrees, to copy Earth's tilt.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Globe ("a rotatable axis mounted at an approximate 23.5-degree tilt to replicate Earth's axial inclination")

F6. The tilt slowly changes, between about 22.1 and 24.5 degrees, over a cycle of about 41,000 years; it is now about 23.4 degrees and slowly shrinking. A bigger tilt makes the seasons more extreme; a smaller tilt makes them milder.
    https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/earth-science/milankovitch-orbital-cycles-and-their-role-in-earths-climate/ ("The greater Earth's axial tilt angle, the more extreme our seasons are"; "As obliquity decreases, it gradually helps make our seasons milder") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Axial_tilt ("between 22.1 degrees and 24.5 degrees over a period of approximately 41,000 years")

F7. If Earth's axis had no tilt at all, there would be only minor changes in temperature through the year (from Earth's small change in distance from the Sun) and no real seasons.
    https://earthsky.org/earth/can-you-explain-why-earth-has-4-seasons/ (if Earth "orbited exactly upright", "minor variations in temperature throughout each year"; "we'd lack Earth's wonderful seasonal changes") ; https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/earth-science/milankovitch-orbital-cycles-and-their-role-in-earths-climate/ (smaller tilt, milder seasons)

## Direct and slanting light, and the length of the day

F8. The hemisphere leaning toward the Sun gets more direct sunlight: the Sun climbs higher in its sky. Light arriving at a low slant spreads the same energy over a larger area of ground, so each patch gets less (the way a flashlight beam tilted to a slant makes a bigger, dimmer spot).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Season (winter rays at a shallow angle "are spread over a larger area") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_irradiance ("This cosine projection reduces effective irradiance at oblique angles"; "spreads radiation over a larger area") ; https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/seasons/en/ (the hemisphere tilted away is "getting its rays from more of an angle")

F9. The hemisphere leaning toward the Sun also has longer days and shorter nights, so the Sun has more hours to warm it. At Sioux Falls, South Dakota, daylight lasts about 15 hours near the summer solstice and near 9 hours near the winter solstice.
    https://www.weather.gov/fsd/season ("the high sun angle produces long days"; "daylight ranges from about 15 hours around the summer solstice to near nine hours close to the winter solstice") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Season (tilt produces "changes in day length")

F10. Day length depends on latitude. At the equator the day lasts about 12 hours all year. Farther north, the June day is longer. North of the Arctic Circle (66.5 degrees north) the Sun does not set at all around the June solstice (24 hours of daylight, the polar day or midnight sun) and does not rise at all around the December solstice (polar night).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Daytime ("At the equator, daytime averages 12 hours year-round"; "the Arctic experiences continuous daytime (midnight sun)") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solstice ("the summer solstice brings 24 hours of continuous daylight (polar day)" above the Arctic Circle; polar night at the winter solstice)

F11. Earth spins at a steady rate all year: the mean solar day is 24 hours, and the length of the day varies by only about a millisecond.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_rotation (mean solar day "exactly 24 hours"; "LOD fluctuates by ±1 millisecond around the mean value")

F12. The Sun's own output hardly changes: about 0.1 percent over its 11-year cycle.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_constant ("variations typically ranging from 0.1% peak-to-trough")

## Opposite hemispheres

F13. The Northern and Southern Hemispheres always have opposite seasons: summer in one is winter in the other.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Season ("summer in one coincides with winter in the other") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Season (the hemispheres "always experience opposite seasons") ; https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/seasons/en/

F14. In Australia, Christmas falls in early summer, and families mark it with barbecues and trips to the beach.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Christmas_in_Australia ("In Australia, Christmas falls during the early summer season"; "outdoor barbecues, beach outings")

## Distance from the Sun: not the cause

F15. Earth's orbit is nearly a circle but slightly oval. Earth is closest to the Sun (perihelion) around January 3, at about 147.1 million km, and farthest (aphelion) in early July (July 6 in 2026), at about 152.1 million km: a difference of about 5 million km.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Apsis (perihelion January 3, 2026, about 147.1 million km; aphelion July 6, 2026, about 152.1 million km) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Season ("Earth is closest to the Sun in January and farthest in July"; perihelion "around January 3") ; https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/earth-science/milankovitch-orbital-cycles-and-their-role-in-earths-climate/ (perihelion "on or about January 3 each year")

F16. About 6.8 percent more sunlight reaches Earth in January than in July; this effect is small and secondary to the tilt.
    https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/earth-science/milankovitch-orbital-cycles-and-their-role-in-earths-climate/ ("each January, about 6.8 percent more incoming solar radiation reaches Earth than it does each July") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Apsis ("This effect is secondary to Earth's 23.5° axial tilt")

F17. The seasons are not caused by Earth's distance from the Sun. The Northern Hemisphere has winter while Earth is closest to the Sun, and the two hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same distance.
    https://www.weather.gov/fsd/season ("The seasons have nothing to do with how far the Earth is from the Sun") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Season ("The seasons are not the result of the variation in Earth's distance to the Sun") ; https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/seasons/en/ ("we are having winter when Earth is closest to the Sun")

## Solstices and equinoxes

F18. The June solstice (June 20, 21 or 22) brings the Northern Hemisphere its longest day and the Southern Hemisphere its shortest; the Sun is straight overhead at noon at the Tropic of Cancer (23.5 degrees north). The December solstice (December 21 or 22) brings the Northern Hemisphere its shortest day and the Southern its longest; the Sun is overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn (23.5 degrees south).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solstice ("typically falling on June 20, 21, or 22"; "usually on December 21 or 22"; "the Sun is directly overhead at the Tropic of Cancer") ; https://www.weather.gov/fsd/season (Sun "directly over the Tropic of Capricorn at 23.5 degrees south latitude")

F19. The word solstice comes from the Latin sol ("sun") and sistere ("to stand still").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solstice

F20. Stonehenge's main axis points to the sunrise at the summer solstice (and the sunset at the winter solstice). Thousands of people gather there to watch the sunrise at the solstice around June 21.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Stonehenge ("aligned to the summer solstice sunrise over the Heel Stone and the winter solstice sunset"; "opens for public gatherings at summer and winter solstices, drawing thousands for sunrise events")

F21. The equinoxes come around March 20 (or 21) and around September 22 (or 23). Earth's axis then leans neither toward the Sun nor away from it, the Sun is overhead at noon at the equator, and day and night last about 12 hours each everywhere on Earth. (Day is in fact slightly longer than night, because the air bends sunlight and the Sun is a disc, not a point.) In the Northern Hemisphere, the March equinox begins spring and the September one autumn; the Southern Hemisphere is the other way round.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Equinox ("tilting neither toward nor away from the Sun"; "directly above the equator"; "slightly longer than that of nighttime") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Daytime ("the equinoxes in March and September bring nearly equal 12-hour days worldwide") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Season (vernal "around March 20", autumnal "around September 22"; "equalizing day and night lengths worldwide")

F22. The word equinox comes from the Latin aequus ("equal") and nox ("night").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Equinox

## Other facts (context only)

F23. The Moon's pull, with the Sun's, raises the ocean tides; it does not make the seasons.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide (tides from "gravitational interactions between the Earth, Moon, and Sun")

F24. The hottest weeks come one to two months after the June solstice (July or August in the north), because land and oceans take time to warm (seasonal lag). Near the equator, temperatures change little through the year.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Season ("peak temperatures to occur 1-2 months after the summer solstice"; "Near the equator in tropical regions, seasonal temperature variations remain minimal") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Season ("seasonal lag due to thermal absorption and release by the oceans")

## Misconceptions students of this grade hold

M1. Seasons come from Earth's distance from the Sun (closer in summer). This is the great one: the documentary A Private Universe (1987) showed even Harvard graduates explaining summer this way. Wrong, by F15 and F17.
    https://scienceinschool.org/?p=69207 ("even Harvard graduates still think that it is hotter in summer because the Sun is nearer to the Earth than in winter") ; https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/seasons/en/ ("Many people believe that Earth is closer to the Sun in the summer")

M2. A low Sun (or the hemisphere leaning away) is farther from the Sun, and that is why its light is weaker. The real reason is the slant spreading the light (F8).

M3. The axis changes direction through the year (turning to face the Sun, or rocking back and forth), or stands straight up at the equinoxes. It keeps one direction in space all year (F3) and stays tilted at the equinoxes, leaning neither toward nor away from the Sun (F21).

M4. The equator, where the Sun is high, has the longest summer days. In June the day grows longer the farther north one goes, up to 24 hours in the Arctic (F10).

(M2-M4 are the mix-ups the lures model; their wrongness rests on the facts cited. I found no single survey to cite for their frequency, so no question claims how common they are.)
