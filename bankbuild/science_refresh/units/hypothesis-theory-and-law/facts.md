# Hypothesis, Theory and Law (T2, 6th grade, method strand)

Sources were read on 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; a second source is given
for anything a child could be taught wrong.

## The grade's standard

F1. Texas's 6th-grade science standard (TEKS 6.1H) expects the student to "distinguish between scientific hypotheses, theories, and laws". The same chapter's introduction says hypotheses are "tentative and testable statements that must be capable of being supported or not supported by observational evidence", and that theories are well established, highly reliable explanations that may still change as new science develops.
    https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-26 ; https://teksguide.org/teks/s61h/alignments

F2. The NGSS nature-of-science statements for grades 6-8 say: "Theories are explanations for observable phenomena"; "Science theories are based on a body of evidence developed over time"; "Laws are regularities or mathematical descriptions of natural phenomena"; "The term 'theory' as used in science is very different from the common use outside of science"; and "Science limits its explanations to systems that lend themselves to observation and empirical evidence."
    https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/18290/chapter/14 (NGSS Appendix H) ; https://www.commonplanner.com/standards/next-generation-science-standards-35ce4c68/appendix-h-understanding-the-scientific-enterprise-the-nature-of-science-in-ngss/grades-6-7-8-0661c517

## Hypotheses

F3. A hypothesis is a proposed explanation for a natural phenomenon or an observed pattern. It must be testable: it can be checked by observation or experiment.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hypothesis ; https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-26

F4. Hypotheses are commonly written in an "if... then..." form, linking a proposed cause to an expected effect. ("Commonly", not always: the if-then form is a habit of writing, not a requirement.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hypothesis

F5. A hypothesis is narrow and tentative, and still needs testing; a theory is broad and well corroborated. Berkeley's Understanding Science: hypotheses, theories and laws differ in breadth, and theories apply to a broader range of phenomena than hypotheses.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hypothesis ; https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/

F6. A testable idea is "one about which we could gather evidence to help determine whether or not the idea is accurate" (Berkeley). An unfalsifiable claim cannot be tested, because no observation could ever show it wrong; Karl Popper held that a scientific theory must be refutable.
    https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/The_Demon-Haunted_World

F7. In "The Dragon in My Garage", a chapter of The Demon-Haunted World (1995), the astronomer Carl Sagan claims a fire-breathing dragon lives in his garage. Each test a friend proposes is answered with an excuse: the dragon is invisible; flour on the floor would show no footprints because the dragon floats in the air; an infrared (heat) camera would show nothing because its fire gives off no heat. Sagan asks what the difference is between such a dragon and no dragon at all.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/The_Demon-Haunted_World ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Demon-Haunted_World

F8. Experiments are only one way to test an idea: "there are many ways to test almost any scientific idea; experimentation is only one approach", and many ideas are tested by detailed observations of the natural world. Scientific predictions often concern past events (Berkeley).
    https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/

F9. Sinosauropteryx, a small meat-eating dinosaur (one specimen has a lizard in its gut), was found in Liaoning Province, China, and described in 1996: the first definitive evidence of feathers in a non-avian dinosaur. Such feathers survive mainly as impressions in fine-grained rock (Currie: "siltstone mixed with volcanic ash"). Over 30 species of feathered dinosaurs are now known. Sinosauropteryx lived in the Early Cretaceous, many millions of years ago.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Feathered_dinosaur ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinosauropteryx

F10. Science does not prove ideas: "Science accepts or rejects ideas based on the evidence; it does not prove or disprove them" (Berkeley). Test results support or refute a hypothesis (Grokipedia); the TEKS say a hypothesis is "supported or not supported" by evidence.
    https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hypothesis ; https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-26

## Theories

F11. A scientific theory is "a well-substantiated explanation of some aspect of the natural world", based on "a body of facts that have been repeatedly confirmed through observation and experiment". Theories can be refined or expanded as new evidence emerges.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_theory ; https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/18290/chapter/14

F12. In everyday speech "theory" often means mere speculation or a hunch (Grokipedia; Berkeley: "a hunch with little evidential support"). In science it means a broad, well-tested explanation, very different from the everyday use (NGSS 6-8).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_theory ; https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/

F13. Examples of scientific theories: atomic theory (John Dalton, 1808), germ theory (Louis Pasteur from the 1860s), plate tectonics (solidified in the 1960s), the kinetic theory of gases, and cell theory (every living thing is made of cells; the cell is the basic unit of life; new cells come from existing cells).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_theory ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_theory

F14. Germ theory holds that specific microorganisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa), called pathogens or "germs", are the main causes of many infectious diseases. It gained definitive traction in the mid-to-late 19th century through Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch, and it remains central to infectious-disease control.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Germ_theory_of_disease

F15. Plate tectonics is the scientific theory that Earth's rigid outer shell (the lithosphere) is broken into plates that move; it accounts for earthquakes, volcanic eruptions and the building of mountain ranges. GPS measures the plates moving a few centimetres a year (1 to 10 cm).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_theory

## Laws

F16. A scientific law is a statement, based on repeated experiments or observations, that describes or predicts a range of natural phenomena; laws can often be written as one or more statements or equations.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_law

F17. Laws describe what happens without addressing its causes: they "do not posit a mechanism or explanation of phenomena" (Wikipedia); a theory explains why the patterns occur (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_law ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_theory

F18. Hooke's law: the force needed to stretch (or squeeze) a spring is directly proportional to how far it is stretched (F = kx), so each equal extra weight adds an equal extra stretch. It holds only up to the material's limit; past it, the spring deforms for good. Robert Hooke published it as an anagram in 1676 and decoded it in 1678 as "ut tensio, sic vis" ("as the extension, so the force").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hooke's_law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_law

F19. Isaac Newton's law of universal gravitation, published in the Principia in 1687, says every particle of matter attracts every other, with a force that rises with the product of their masses and falls off with the square of the distance between them.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_law

F20. In the General Scholium, added to the second edition of the Principia in 1713, Newton wrote (Motte's 1729 translation) "hitherto I have not been able to discover the cause of those properties of gravity from phænomena", and "hypotheses non fingo", "I frame no hypotheses". His law describes how gravity acts but does not explain its cause.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hypotheses_non_fingo ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation

F21. Albert Einstein's general relativity (1915-1916), a theory, explains gravity as the curving of spacetime by mass and energy, a deeper mechanism behind the attraction Newton's law describes.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_theory

F22. Boyle's law: for a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, pressure is inversely proportional to volume, so halving the volume doubles the pressure. Robert Boyle published it in 1662. It is an empirical (measured) relationship; real gases depart from it a little at high pressure or low temperature.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Boyle's_law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_law

F23. The kinetic (particle) theory of gases explains the gas laws: a gas's pressure comes from its moving particles striking the walls of the container, and in a smaller volume they strike the walls more often, so the pressure rises (OpenStax). The gas laws "do not tell us why gases follow these relationships"; the theory does. Daniel Bernoulli first linked pressure to particle impacts in 1738; Clausius (1850s), Maxwell (1860) and Boltzmann (1870s) built the theory out. It derives the ideal gas law from its own assumptions.
    https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/9-5-the-kinetic-molecular-theory ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kinetic_theory_of_gases ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_theory

F24. Theories do not "become" laws: the idea that a theory graduates into a law once it has enough evidence is a misconception, because the two do different jobs (a law describes, a theory explains). Berkeley: one "cannot grow into another, no matter how much fertilizer and water are offered".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_theory ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_law ; https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/

F27. Neither ranks above the other: laws and theories "occupy distinct but equally robust roles in science" (Grokipedia), and hypotheses, theories and laws differ "in breadth, not in level of support" (Berkeley). A law is not more certain than a theory.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_theory ; https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/

F25. Laws are not proven forever either: they hold under stated conditions and can be revised. Newton's laws of motion hold for speeds much less than the speed of light and for things larger than atoms; beyond those limits relativity and quantum mechanics take over.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_law

F26. Newton's three laws of motion, first published in the Principia in 1687, describe the relationship between the motion of a body and the forces acting on it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion

## Misconceptions students of this grade hold

M1. "A theory is just a guess." The everyday meaning (a hunch) is carried into science, where a theory is a well-tested explanation (F12). Berkeley lists it under vocabulary mix-ups; NGSS 6-8 names the difference.
    https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/ ; https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/18290/chapter/14

M2. "Hypothesis, then theory, then law": a ladder of certainty, an idea climbing it as evidence grows. Berkeley lists it as a misconception; Grokipedia rejects it (F24, F27).
    https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_theory

M3. "Science proves ideas" (and so a law is proven, a theory not yet). Berkeley lists it as a misconception (F10, F25).
    https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/

M4. "Only an experiment can test an idea", so ideas about the past cannot be tested. Berkeley corrects it (F8).
    https://undsci.berkeley.edu/for-educators/prepare-and-plan/correcting-misconceptions/

Note on a disagreement: Grokipedia's Hypothesis page says "a well-supported hypothesis may elevate to the status of a scientific theory", while Berkeley calls hypothesis-to-theory promotion a misconception. No question here keys anything on whether a hypothesis becomes a theory; the questions key only that a theory does not become a law (on which all the sources agree).
