## Uneven heating, pressure and wind

F1. Over a year the tropics take in more solar energy than they give off ("a surplus of incoming solar radiation in tropical regions and a deficit in polar areas"), and the atmosphere's circulation works to offset it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmospheric_circulation

F2. Wind blows from high pressure toward low pressure ("a horizontal pressure gradient that drives air movement", "winds flowing from high toward low pressure"). In a low, air flows inward and rises ("This convergence of air into the low-pressure center causes it to rise"), giving "cloud formation, precipitation, and unsettled weather conditions"; lows "drive much of the planet's precipitation and storm activity". Highs bring "clear skies and subsidence" (sinking air).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Low-pressure_area

F3. Without Earth's rotation (and tilt and surface water) "global circulation would be simple": one cell in each hemisphere, hot air rising at the equator, moving toward the poles high up, sinking there, and returning toward the equator near the ground as cold air.
    https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/global/global-atmospheric-circulations ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hadley_cell (Hadley's own single cell ran its sinking air "unrealistically toward the poles")

F4. Cold air is denser than warm air: "The heavier (more dense) cool air pushes under the lighter (less dense) warm air" (UCAR); "cold air is more dense (heavier) than warm air" (NWS).
    https://scied.ucar.edu/learning-zone/how-weather-works/weather-fronts ; https://weather.gov/lmk/basic-fronts

## Hadley, Halley and the trade winds

F5. George Hadley (1685-1768), born in London, was called to the bar in 1709 but "remained more interested in mechanical and physical studies than in legal work"; the Royal Society calls him a "lawyer-turned-scientist" and "amateur scientist". His paper "Concerning the cause of the general trade-winds" was read to the Royal Society on 22 May 1735 and printed in the Philosophical Transactions.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hadley ; https://royalsociety.org/blog/2015/07/debating-the-winds/ ; https://makingscience.royalsociety.org/items/clp_4ii_15/paper-concerning-the-causes-of-the-general-trade-winds-by-george-hadley

F6. Hadley's explanation: "the Sun's rays heat the equatorial region most intensely, causing the air there to expand"; air rises at the equator, cooler air flows in at the surface, and returns poleward aloft. "Surface air from slower-rotating higher latitudes lags westward relative to the faster-moving ground, which produces easterly winds" (Grokipedia). He "recognised that the Earth's rotation plays a role", which "had been missing in Halley's proposal" (Wikipedia). His error: he assumed linear momentum is conserved, where it is "in fact the angular momentum", and "the predicted effect is twice as large". The theory "remained relatively unknown and was independently created several times" (John Dalton, Heinrich Dove); it is now the Hadley circulation.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hadley_cell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hadley ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadley_cell

F7. Edmond Halley's earlier theory (presented 1685, trade-wind map 1686) had the air rise under the strongest solar heating and the heating's westward daily march drive "an easterly (westward) wind to follow on the path of most intense solar warming" (Royal Society). He later wrote to John Wallis that the questioning "makes me less confident of the truth thereof".
    https://royalsociety.org/blog/2015/07/debating-the-winds/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadley_cell ; https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.00740

F8. The trade winds are "prevailing easterly surface winds that reliably blow towards the equator", "from the northeast in the Northern Hemisphere and from the southeast in the Southern Hemisphere", between about 30 N and 30 S. Columbus used "the northeast trades to cross the Atlantic" in 1492. The northern and southern trades meet near the equator at the Intertropical Convergence Zone, the rising branch of the Hadley cell, with heavy rain.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Trade_winds ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmospheric_circulation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hadley_cell

## Coriolis

F9. Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis (1792-1843), a "French mathematician and engineer", derived the effect in 1835 in "Mémoire sur les équations du mouvement relatif des systèmes de corps", studying relative motion in rotating machinery such as waterwheels (Wikipedia: "a paper in 1835 on the energy yield of machines with rotating parts", "waterwheels"). "Early in the 20th century, the term Coriolis force began to be used in connection with meteorology."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Coriolis_force ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolis_force

F10. The deflection "acts to the right of the motion in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere" (Grokipedia); "deflected to the right (with respect to the direction of travel) in the Northern Hemisphere" and "to the left in the Southern Hemisphere"; the horizontal deflection "decreases to zero at the equator" (Wikipedia). It deflects "winds and ocean currents", producing the trade winds and the westerlies.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Coriolis_force ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolis_force

## The wind belts

F11. Three cells in each hemisphere: the Hadley cell (about 0-30 degrees), the Ferrel cell (30-60) and the Polar cell (60-90). "Sinking air at about 30° latitude creates subtropical high-pressure zones", under which lie deserts such as the Sahara; NOAA: "The high pressure band is located at about 30° N/S latitude."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmospheric_circulation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hadley_cell ; https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/global/global-atmospheric-circulations

F12. The westerlies "blow from west to east in the mid-latitudes", roughly 30-60 degrees, from the southwest in the Northern Hemisphere and the northwest in the Southern. They are the surface flow of the Ferrel cell, where "air near the surface flows poleward and eastward" (NOAA), turned by the Coriolis effect. Stronger in the south: the "Roaring Forties".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Westerlies ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmospheric_circulation ; https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/global/global-atmospheric-circulations

F13. The polar easterlies blow "from the east toward the west", about 60-90 degrees: cold, dense air sinks over the poles, making high pressure, spreads toward lower latitudes and is deflected into an easterly wind. They meet the westerlies at the polar front, about 50-60 degrees.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Polar_easterlies

## Jet streams

F14. A jet stream is a narrow band of strong winds in the upper troposphere, "typically flowing from west to east", about 9-16 km up; average about 110 mph (177 km/h), at times over 200 mph (320 km/h). Each hemisphere has a polar jet (50-60 degrees) and a subtropical jet (about 30 degrees). Jets are "driven by sharp temperature contrasts between polar and equatorial air masses", turned by the Coriolis effect; "The wind speeds are greatest where temperature differences between air masses are greatest" (Wikipedia); polar jets intensify "in winter due to greater meridional temperature gradients" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Jet_stream ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_stream

F15. The jet stream is "the principal upper-level steering current for extratropical cyclones", carrying storms and fronts usually eastward (Grokipedia); "The path of jet streams affects cyclonic storm systems at lower levels in the atmosphere" (Wikipedia). Westerlies page: "Extratropical cyclones are steered eastward along the axis of the westerly jet stream."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Jet_stream ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_stream ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Westerlies

F16. Wasaburo Ooishi (1874-1950), a Japanese meteorologist, worked at the Aerological Observatory at Tateno (founded 1920, by the JMA's account with Ooishi as founder). Pilot balloons are small hydrogen balloons tracked by a theodolite (JMA). From 1923 to 1925 he made "nearly 1,300 measurements" (Grokipedia; other counts given are 1,228 and 1,288). His 1926 report's winter profile "was the first known evidence of persistent strong westerlies over Japan, later known as the jet stream" (Wikipedia). He published in Esperanto "In an attempt to reach an unresponsive foreign audience" (Wikipedia), and the work drew little attention outside Japan (Grokipedia; Wikipedia, Jet stream: unnoticed because it "was published in Esperanto"). Others met the same winds later: Wiley Post in his high flights of the 1930s, and Heinrich Seilkopf named the "Strahlströmung" in 1939.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Jet_stream ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasaburo_Oishi ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_stream ; https://www.data.jma.go.jp/kousou/obs_second_div/history/history01-e.html

F17. On the B-29 raid on Tokyo of 24 November 1944 the bombers met "a high-speed wind coming out of the west at speeds as high as 200 miles per hour", "at precisely the altitudes at which the bombers were operating"; it "made accurate bombing impossible", and only 24 planes attacked the primary target (Wikipedia, XXI Bomber Command). Grokipedia: B-29 crews in 1944-1945 met winds "exceeding 200 mph". High-altitude daylight attacks were later replaced by low-altitude raids. 200 mph is about 320 km/h.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XXI_Bomber_Command ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Jet_stream ; https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/pacific-b-29s

## The Bergen school, air masses and fronts

F18. Vilhelm Bjerknes (1862-1951) founded the Bergen Geophysical Institute in 1917, with his son Jacob (born 1897) and Jacob's fellow student Halvor Solberg; Tor Bergeron joined in 1919 (NASA; Grokipedia). "With the Germans cutting off Norway and the rest of Scandinavia from continental European weather communications" in the First World War, Bjerknes persuaded the government to fund "a countrywide network of observation stations for practical forecasting" (Encyclopedia.com); the network grew "nearly tenfold" (Grokipedia).
    https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/vilhelm-bjerknes ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Vilhelm_Bjerknes ; https://encyclopedia.com/science/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bergen-school-dynamic-meteorology-and-its-dissemination

F19. The name: "They called these zones "fronts," an analogy with the First World War battlefronts" (NASA); boundaries called "fronts" "in analogy to the battlefronts of the war" (Grokipedia, Weather front); the team was "lifting the term 'front' from the recent world war" (Encyclopedia.com). Jacob Bjerknes's paper On the Structure of Moving Cyclones (1919) described the storm model; the "steering line" became the "warm front" and the "squall line" the "cold front".
    https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/vilhelm-bjerknes ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Weather_front ; https://encyclopedia.com/science/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bergen-school-dynamic-meteorology-and-its-dissemination

F20. The polar front is "a semipermanent, semicontinuous atmospheric boundary that separates cold polar air masses from warmer tropical air masses", near 50-60 N; the Bergen school's polar front theory explained mid-latitude storms as waves that form and grow along it (Grokipedia). NASA: the theory "described the interaction of warm and cold air masses" and "is the basis for much of current weather forecasting".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Polar_front ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/vilhelm-bjerknes

F21. A front is "a narrow transition zone, or boundary, separating two distinct air masses" differing in temperature, humidity and density (Grokipedia); "a boundary between two air masses that contain different temperature, wind, and moisture properties" (NWS). Jacob Bjerknes and Solberg classified air masses in 1921 (Grokipedia, Vilhelm Bjerknes).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Weather_front ; https://weather.gov/lmk/basic-fronts ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Vilhelm_Bjerknes

F22. Cold front: "denser cold air displaces warmer air"; the cold air lifts the warm air steeply, giving "brief but intense showers and thunderstorms", gusty winds and a sharp temperature drop behind it (Grokipedia). UCAR: "Lifted warm air ahead of the front produces cumulus or cumulonimbus clouds and thunderstorms", heavy rain, "a sudden drop in temperature"; cold fronts "move fast, up to twice as fast as a warm front". NOAA: after one passes, "winds shift to the northwest, temperatures fall ... and skies clear". (Wikipedia calls the plain "wedging" picture "too simplistic"; the questions say only that the cold air pushes under and lifts the warm air, as UCAR and NWS teach it.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Weather_front ; https://scied.ucar.edu/learning-zone/how-weather-works/weather-fronts ; https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/wxmaps ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_front

F23. Warm front: lighter warm air "gradually overrides cooler air along a gentler slope"; clouds build from high cirrus to layered stratus or nimbostratus, followed by long, steady, light-to-moderate rain over a wide area (Grokipedia). UCAR: "high clouds like cirrus, cirrostratus, and middle clouds like altostratus ahead of a warm front", lowering as it arrives, and warmer air after it. NOAA: clouds lower and thicken, "several hours of light to moderate rain". NWS (Louisville summary): warm fronts' "less steep slope" gives "a gradual rise of air", "more steady and widespread precipitation"; they move about half as fast as cold fronts. Wikipedia: warm-front rain is "relatively steady, as in light rain or drizzle".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Weather_front ; https://scied.ucar.edu/learning-zone/how-weather-works/weather-fronts ; https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/wxmaps ; https://weather.gov/lmk/basic-fronts ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_front

## Falling pressure

F24. "Falling pressure typically signals the approach of low-pressure systems associated with storms", "with rapid drops commonly preceding rain or thunderstorms in mid-latitudes"; "rising pressure often precedes high-pressure systems bringing clear skies and stable conditions" (Grokipedia, Barometer). Robert FitzRoy taught "a falling barometer signaling increased wind or rain, with rapid falls indicating gales" (Grokipedia, Robert FitzRoy); a barometer of his design was fixed at ports for crews "before setting out to sea" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Barometer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Robert_FitzRoy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_FitzRoy

F25. Humid air is lighter than dry air at the same temperature and pressure: water vapour's molecules (molar mass about 18) are lighter than nitrogen's (28) and oxygen's (32), so mixing in vapour lowers the air's weight; the "heavy" feel of a humid day is the body's (sweat evaporates poorly), not the air's weight.
    https://wxguys.ssec.wisc.edu/2021/07/12/air-humidity ; https://science.howstuffworks.com/nature/climate-weather/atmospheric/is-humid-air-heavier-than-dry-air.htm

## Heat carried toward the poles

F26. Ocean currents carry heat from the tropics toward the poles ("redistributing heat from tropics to poles"); at 30 N the ocean carries about 30-40 percent of the total poleward flux and the atmosphere the rest. Warm currents carry tropical heat poleward, "elevating coastal air temperatures and mitigating winter severity in downstream regions"; cold currents cool their coasts. Surface currents are "driven primarily by wind friction on the surface" (Grokipedia). NOAA Ocean Explorer: currents carry "warm water and precipitation from the equator toward the poles and cold water from the poles back to the tropics", "helping to counteract the uneven distribution of solar radiation reaching Earth's surface".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ocean_current ; https://coastal.ca.gov/coastalvoices/resources/NOAAOceanExplorerClimate.pdf

F27. Heat from Earth's interior reaches the surface at about 44-47 terawatts, about 0.09 W per square metre, some 0.03-0.04 percent of the roughly 240 W per square metre of sunlight the planet absorbs.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth%27s_internal_heat_budget

F28. Fresh snow reflects 80-95 percent of the sunlight that falls on it; sea ice 50-70 percent; open ocean only about 6 percent.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Albedo

F29. The ozone layer, "approximately from 15 to 35 kilometers above the surface" in the stratosphere, mostly above the jet streams (9-16 km, F14), "absorbs about 97-99% of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation" (taught at T3 in why-earth-supports-life). The jet stream does not shield the ground from ultraviolet light.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ozone_layer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Jet_stream

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Wind is caused by Earth's spin: students "rarely explain wind through pressure differences, and instead point to moving objects or the movement of the earth"; "over 54% of 6th grade students do not see any connection between the sun and wind" (research summaries, reached through a search: AAAS-based Curriculum Topic Study notes at Northern Arizona University and an AGU/SERC poster).
    https://jan.ucc.nau.edu/jclark/Education%20Mesonet/Research%20on%20Student%20Learning.htm ; https://serc.carleton.edu/earth_rendezvous/2024/program/posters/friday/283294.html
M2. The Coriolis effect sets the swirl of a draining sink or toilet: a misconception; at that scale it is "negligibly small compared to the many other influences".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Coriolis_force ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolis_force
M3. Humid air is "heavy" (F25).
M4. Halley's respectable wrong theory: the trade winds follow the Sun's heat westward (F7).
M5. Which air rides over which at a front: cold air is the denser and stays low (F4).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F30. Hadley was not the first to bring in Earth's spin: Galileo had "proposed that the trade winds resulted from the atmosphere lagging behind the Earth's faster tangential rotation speed in the low latitudes" (Wikipedia, Hadley cell); EBSCO: "There was a consensus that these winds arose as the Earth rotated from west to east." What Hadley added was the turn of air moving toward the equator from latitudes where the ground moves east more slowly, the element "missing in Halley's proposal" (Wikipedia, George Hadley).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadley_cell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hadley ; https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/earth-and-atmospheric-sciences/hadley-describes-atmospheric-circulation

F31. Earth's spin barely changes through the year: the length of day "fluctuates by ±1 millisecond around the mean value", with "atmospheric winds and pressure systems contributing the largest seasonal signals" (Grokipedia). Earth does not spin faster in winter in any way that could strengthen the winds.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_rotation

F32. At a front "the two air masses have different densities, based on temperature, and do not easily mix" (LibreTexts, Introduction to Earth Science); at a cold front the cold air is "undercutting the warmer air, forcing it to rise rapidly" (Grokipedia, Weather front).
    https://geo.libretexts.org/Courses/Fullerton_College/Introduction_to_Earth_Science_(Ikeda)/17%3A_Weather_Processes/17.03%3A_Air_Masses_and_Weather_Fronts ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Weather_front
