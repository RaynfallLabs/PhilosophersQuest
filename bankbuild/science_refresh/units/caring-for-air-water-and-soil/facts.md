## Air: where pollution comes from

F1. Burning fossil fuels and biomass (wood and other plant fuel) produces about 85 percent of the air pollution that people around the world breathe ("approximately 85% of global air pollution exposure"); human sources dominate where people live.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Air_pollution

F2. The World Health Organization (WHO) names household combustion devices, motor vehicles, industrial facilities and forest fires as common sources of air pollution, and particulate matter, carbon monoxide, ozone, nitrogen dioxide and sulfur dioxide as the pollutants of greatest concern.
    https://www.who.int/health-topics/air-pollution

F3. Diesel and gasoline vehicles are major emitters of nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide and ultrafine particles; coal- and gas-fired power plants contribute heavily to sulfur dioxide; homes that burn wood, coal or dung in inefficient stoves pollute too.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Air_pollution

F4. Natural sources of air pollution include volcanic eruptions and wildfires, which give off vast quantities of fine particles (PM2.5).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Air_pollution ; https://www.who.int/health-topics/air-pollution

## Air: smoke and the specks in it

F5. Smoke is a mixture of gases and particles: carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide, water vapour (Grokipedia: "a major non-pollutant gas"), soot (black carbon) and fragments of ash. Most of its particles are fine, under 2.5 micrometres across. EPA: wildfire smoke is "a mixture of gases and particles primarily from the burning of trees and plants".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Smoke ; https://www.epa.gov/wildfires/wildfire-smoke-and-health

F6. The United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) says that in wildfire smoke "fine particulate matter, also called particle pollution or PM2.5, is the biggest threat to your health".
    https://www.epa.gov/wildfires/wildfire-smoke-and-health

F7. Particles smaller than 10 micrometres can get deep into the lungs, and some may even get into the bloodstream; particles under 2.5 micrometres (PM2.5) pose the greatest risk to health. The average human hair is about 70 micrometres across, about 30 times wider than the largest fine particle. Grokipedia: "PM2.5 penetrates deep into lungs and bloodstream".
    https://www.epa.gov/pm-pollution/particulate-matter-pm-basics ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Air_pollution ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Smoke

F8. Larger particles (over 10 micrometres) "are generally of less concern because they usually do not enter the lungs", though they can irritate the eyes, nose and throat.
    https://19january2021snapshot.epa.gov/wildfire-smoke-course/why-wildfire-smoke-health-concern_.html

## Water: oil, sewage and the treatment plants

F9. The National Academies' report Oil in the Sea IV (2022) found that the top source of oil in North American waters is land-based runoff: oil carried by rain or snowmelt into rivers and then the sea, "primarily from cities and vehicles" (runoff from highways, parking lots, vehicle washing and vehicle fluid leaks). Natural seeps from the seabed are the second largest source and oil spills the third; discharges from commercial ships are relatively small. The report says exact amounts are hard to measure for lack of data.
    https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/2022/09/land-based-runoff-remains-top-source-of-oil-in-the-ocean-says-new-report ; https://www.ucdavis.edu/climate/news/most-oil-sea-comes-runoff-land-not-just-oil-spills

F10. Most oils float on the surface of seas, rivers and lakes and spread into a thin slick. Booms are floating barriers that hold spilled oil in, and skimmers remove the oil layer from the surface.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial-coastal/oil-spills/os03.html ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial-coastal/oil-spills/os06-sub-01.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Oil_spill

F11. Human waste that reaches water supplies spreads diseases such as cholera, typhoid and hepatitis A; WHO: water contaminated with faeces can transmit diarrhoea, cholera, dysentery, typhoid and polio. As the waste in sewage rots, microbes use up the oxygen dissolved in the water, "suffocating fish and invertebrates".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sewage_treatment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cholera ; https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/drinking-water

F12. At a sewage treatment plant, the first (primary) stage lets the sewage stand in settling tanks: the solids sink as sludge and are pumped out, while grease and oil float and are skimmed off.
    https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/a-visit-a-wastewater-treatment-plant ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sewage_treatment

F13. In the second (secondary) stage, microorganisms, chiefly bacteria, break down the dissolved and suspended waste. The sewage is mixed with air and with sludge "loaded with bacteria" in aeration tanks for several hours. Air is pumped through because, as USGS puts it, "As organic matter decays, it uses up oxygen. Aeration replenishes the oxygen." Some of the bacteria-rich sludge is sent back into the tanks to keep "a high population of microorganisms" there. The stage removes about 85 percent of the organic matter (EPA, 1998).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sewage_treatment ; https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/a-visit-a-wastewater-treatment-plant ; https://www3.epa.gov/npdes/pubs/bastre.pdf

F14. Treated wastewater is disinfected with chlorine to kill bacteria that could harm health before it is let out into a river or the sea.
    https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/a-visit-a-wastewater-treatment-plant

F15. A drinking-water plant cleans water in steps. Coagulation: chemicals such as alum (aluminium sulfate) make dirt and tiny particles bind together. Flocculation: gentle mixing grows them into larger, heavier clumps called flocs. Sedimentation: the flocs sink. Filtration: the clear water passes through filters of sand, gravel or charcoal, which remove particles and many germs (activated carbon removes bad smells).
    https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/how-water-treatment-works.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_treatment

F16. Disinfection, often the last step, uses chemicals such as chlorine to kill any germs that remain; plants leave a low level of disinfectant in the water so that it keeps killing germs in the pipes between the plant and the tap. Grokipedia: chlorine "inactivates remaining microorganisms", and the leftover chlorine prevents regrowth in the pipes.
    https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/how-water-treatment-works.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_treatment

F17. Softening (removing the calcium and magnesium that make water hard, by lime or by ion exchange) and corrosion control (raising the water's pH to protect pipes) are separate treatments from chlorine disinfection.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_treatment

## Soil: erosion and the ways to stop it

F18. Raindrops striking bare soil knock particles loose and splash them away (Iowa State: 3 to 5 feet; Grokipedia: up to 0.6 metres). The splashed particles clog the surface pores, so less water soaks in and more runs off, carrying soil with it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Soil_erosion ; https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/encyclopedia/how-reduce-potential-soil-erosion-early-spring

F19. A cover of plants, crop residue (straw and stalks left after harvest), fallen leaves or mulch takes the force of the falling raindrops ("Canopy layers intercept precipitation, dissipating raindrop impact energy"; mulch "absorbing kinetic energy from raindrops"); residue also slows the water running over the ground. Leaving residue on a field instead of tilling it bare is a standard way to stop erosion.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Soil_erosion ; https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/encyclopedia/how-reduce-potential-soil-erosion-early-spring

F20. Terraces turn a long slope into short, flat steps. They slow the water running downhill, give it time to soak into the soil, and let the soil it carries settle on the step instead of washing away; they can cut erosion by up to 11 times compared with untreated land.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Terrace_(earthworks) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Soil_erosion ; https://sera17.wordpress.ncsu.edu/terraces/

F21. Contour ploughing (rows run across a slope, not up and down it) makes small dams that slow runoff; it can cut erosion by up to 50 percent. (Context only.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Contour_plowing ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Soil_erosion

F22. Windbreaks are rows of trees or shrubs planted across the direction of the prevailing wind. Their main purpose is to slow the wind: they lower wind speeds in the fields by about 40 to 50 percent, for a distance of about 8 to 15 times the trees' height downwind, and so keep the wind from blowing soil away.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Soil_erosion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Windbreak ; https://www.fs.usda.gov/nac/practices/windbreaks.php

F23. A windbreak's tree roots reach only a strip of the field near the row: the trees hold back the crops only "within distances extending to 1.0-1.5 times the height of the windbreak", and "the bulk of the tree root system may not extend out to distances of 1.5H" (Hou and others, 2003, a USDA National Agroforestry Center study in Nebraska).
    https://fs.usda.gov/nac/assets/documents/research/publications/2003hou.pdf

F24. After the dust storms of the 1930s, the Great Plains Shelterbelt Project planted about 220 million trees in windbreaks across the American plains between 1935 and 1942.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Windbreak

## Energy: efficiency and the light bulb

F25. An incandescent bulb gives off over 90 percent of the energy it uses as infrared radiation and heat ("Over 90% of the energy is dissipated as infrared radiation and conductive/convective heat"), and only about 5 to 10 percent as visible light. It makes about 10 to 15 lumens of light per watt; modern light-emitting diodes (LEDs) make over 100.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Incandescent_light_bulb ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Light-emitting_diode

F26. Watts measure how much energy a bulb uses and lumens how bright it is. A traditional 60-watt incandescent bulb gives about 800 lumens; an LED bulb gives the same 800 lumens using only about 9 watts (United States Federal Trade Commission).
    https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/shopping-light-bulbs

F27. LEDs use at least 75 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs (newer Department of Energy pages say up to 90 percent) and last up to 25 times longer.
    https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/led-lighting ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Light-emitting_diode

F28. Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only change from one form to another.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Conservation_of_energy

## Misconceptions students of this grade hold

M1. Energy is "used up" and gone once a lamp or machine has used it; Harvard's MOSART list of student ideas includes "things use up energy" and that energy can be created or destroyed. (F28 says otherwise; the bulb's energy leaves as heat and light.)
    https://waps.cfa.harvard.edu/mosart/misconceptions.php

M2. Clear water is safe to drink; teaching lessons note that students assume clear water is safe and that "water that might look clean to drink may contain harmful bacteria or chemicals".
    https://www.unmc.edu/ubeatsgo/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/W8M4-Water-and-Health.pdf ; https://aws.sjrwmd.com/SJRWMD/education/Lesson_plan--water_pollution.pdf

M3. "Bacteria are germs so they are always bad", so a treatment plant must be killing bacteria rather than raising them (F13 says it raises them).
    https://aws.sjrwmd.com/SJRWMD/education/Lesson_plan--water_pollution.pdf

M4. Oil in the sea comes mostly from big tanker spills; the 2022 report ranks spills third, after runoff from land and natural seeps (F9).
    https://www.ucdavis.edu/climate/news/most-oil-sea-comes-runoff-land-not-just-oil-spills

M5. Big, visible flakes of ash must be the worst part of smoke; EPA says the reverse: the finest specks are the biggest threat, and larger particles usually do not reach the lungs (F6-F8). (No study found that measures how common this belief is; it is listed as a natural lure, not a documented survey result.)

M6. Trees always protect soil with their roots (the T1 lesson of the cleared hillside); a windbreak's roots reach only a strip near the row, and it protects the rest of the field by slowing the wind (F22-F23).
