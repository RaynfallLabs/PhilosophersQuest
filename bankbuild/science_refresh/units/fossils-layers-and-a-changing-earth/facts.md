## Sea fossils high in the mountains

F1. The rock at the summit of Mount Everest (8,848.86 m, almost 9 km above sea level) is fossil-bearing marine limestone of Ordovician age, about 450 million years old, laid down in a warm, shallow sea (the Tethys). It "still contain[s] the fossils of marine animals such as brachiopods, conodonts and crinoids". Grokipedia: "fossiliferous limestones and marbles derived from shallow marine sediments of the ancient Tethys Sea"; "marine sedimentary rocks thrust upward from ancient seabeds".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mount_Everest ; https://iugs-geoheritage.org/?p=3648

F2. The summit rocks were thrust and uplifted to their present height after India collided with Asia, a collision that began roughly 40-55 million years ago; uplift there still outpaces erosion. (The plate process itself is plates-at-work's; this unit says only that old sea floor was pushed up.)
    https://iugs-geoheritage.org/?p=3648 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mount_Everest

F3. Crinoids (sea lilies and feather stars) are "exclusively marine echinoderms"; their fossils are commonly found as pieces embedded in limestones, and they dominated shallow Paleozoic seas.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Crinoid

F4. Sea level has never stood anywhere near the height of the highest mountains. Its highest known stands (in the Cretaceous) were roughly 200-250 m above today's; Paleozoic swings were tens of metres to about 125 m; melting all of today's ice would add only about 69 m (Greenland 7.2 m, Antarctica about 61 m, other glaciers about 0.5 m). Everest's summit is almost 9,000 m up.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Past_sea_level ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Past_sea_level

F5. Older explanations of sea shells in mountain rock: medieval thinkers read them as remains of creatures killed in Noah's Flood; Albertus Magnus and many 17th-century scholars credited a "formative power" or "vital formative force" (vis plastica) that grew the shapes inside the rock. Leonardo da Vinci "rejected the Noachian Deluge as inadequate to explain widespread marine fossils in continental strata" and argued for slow deposition in ancient seas.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/History_of_paleontology ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Codex_Leicester

## The same land plants and animals on continents oceans apart

F6. Glossopteris was a woody seed plant of the Permian (about 299-252 million years ago), "commonly classified among the seed ferns", a shrub or tree with tongue-shaped leaves. Its fossils are found on the former southern continents: South America, Africa, India, Australia and Antarctica (also New Zealand and Madagascar).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Glossopteris ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossopteris

F7. Glossopteris seeds could not have crossed the oceans. Wikipedia: "Seeds, much too large to be wind-borne, could not have blown across thousands of miles of open sea", and floating across vast oceans is unlikely too. Grokipedia: "heavy seeds lacked the capacity for long-distance oceanic or wind dispersal" (it notes that small winged seeds spread locally). Its spread across the southern continents is "crucial evidence" that they were once connected.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossopteris ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Glossopteris

F8. The first birds appeared in the Late Jurassic, about 150 million years ago (Archaeopteryx), some 100 million years after Glossopteris died out, so no bird could have carried its seeds.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Origin_of_birds ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Glossopteris

F9. Mesosaurus, a small swimming reptile of the Early Permian (about 275 million years ago; adults mostly about 70 cm long), lived in shallow coastal waters and lagoons and was a slow swimmer. Its fossils are found only in southern Africa (South Africa, Namibia) and eastern South America (Brazil, Uruguay). Grokipedia: it "could not have crossed the modern South Atlantic"; the same animal on both sides is the core of the argument that the two were joined.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mesosaurus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesosaurus

F10. Lystrosaurus was a plant-eating dicynodont therapsid (one of the "mammal-like reptiles") of the Late Permian and Early Triassic; it was "probably fully terrestrial", a land animal. Its fossils are found in South Africa, India and Antarctica (also China, Mongolia and Russia). Edwin H. Colbert's team dug up Antarctic Lystrosaurus fossils in the Transantarctic Mountains in 1969-1970; the find "helped support" continental drift and plate tectonics.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lystrosaurus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lystrosaurus

F11. Things that cross oceans on their own leave no such clue: wind carries fern spores "over long distances, sometimes hundreds of kilometers", and ferns show "frequent transoceanic dispersal"; true ferns make spores, not seeds. Wandering albatrosses fly up to 15,000 km in a single trip and can "traverse ocean basins". Great white sharks migrate thousands of kilometres a year, out into the open ocean.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fern ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Albatross ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Great_white_shark

## Coal in Antarctica

F12. Coal forms from partly decayed plant material that piles up in waterlogged wetlands (swamps and peat mires), is buried, and is compacted by pressure and heat over millions of years. Much coal formed in warm, humid times.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Coal

F13. Coal is abundant in the Permian rocks (the Beacon Supergroup) of Antarctica's Transantarctic Mountains, where there are "fossil forests and coal deposits from the Permian-Triassic indicating a once-temperate climate". Glossopteris was "an important contributor to the vast Permian coal deposits of the Southern Hemisphere continents"; it grew in wet, swampy lowlands, and growth rings in Antarctic fossil wood suggest the trees shed their leaves for the dark winters.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Geology_of_Antarctica ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Transantarctic_Mountains ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossopteris

F14. CAUTION (a half-truth to avoid): many classroom texts say the coal shows Antarctica was once near the equator. In the Permian the southern landmass "straddl[ed] the South Pole", and Glossopteris grew at middle to high latitudes (roughly 30-90 degrees south), in a climate about 10-15 degrees Celsius warmer than polar regions today. What the coal truly shows is that Antarctica once had a climate mild and wet enough for swamp forests. No question keys the equator.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Geology_of_Antarctica ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Glossopteris

F15. Explorers of Shackleton's 1907-1909 expedition found coal near the head of the Beardmore Glacier: Frank Wild found seams of coal in sandstone cliffs on Buckley Island (Mount Buckley) in December 1908 (a 1909 newspaper report: "seven distinct seams of coal"; a 1962 report: hard coal has been found widely in Antarctica "ever since Shackleton discovered it near the head of the Beardmore glacier"). "First coal in Antarctica" is NOT claimed: no source read says so. Scott's party (1912) later hauled about 35 pounds (16 kg) of fossils, including the first Antarctic Glossopteris, and the fossils were found with their bodies. (Scene only; not keyed.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossopteris ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckley_Island_(Antarctica) ; https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19620118.2.147 (search summary only; the page was not opened)

## Matching rocks and mountain belts across oceans

F16. The Appalachian Mountains of North America and the Caledonian mountains of Scotland, Ireland, Norway, Sweden and East Greenland were built in the same mountain-building events (about 490-390 million years ago); their structural and rock-layer parallels indicate "a once-continuous orogenic belt", which rifting in the Mesozoic split when the central Atlantic opened. Wikipedia: these ranges "all originally belonged to the sprawling Central Pangean Mountains", and the Appalachians "formed part of the same mountain chain as the Little Atlas in Morocco".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Caledonian_orogeny ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Central_Pangean_Mountains ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachian_Mountains

F17. Matching stacks of rock: the Karoo rocks of South Africa and the Paraná Basin rocks of Brazil run in the same order, glacier deposits at the base (Dwyka; Itararé), then shales with Glossopteris (Ecca; post-glacial Itararé), up to thick basalt lavas on top (Drakensberg; Serra Geral, "chemically and temporally matching"). (Context only.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Karoo_Supergroup

F18. Neptunism, the late-18th-century theory of Abraham Gottlob Werner, held that all rocks formed by precipitation and sedimentation from a universal ocean, laid down in a fixed order everywhere as the ocean receded; field evidence for volcanic and intrusive rock (James Hutton and others) and Charles Lyell's work led to its decline by the mid-19th century.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Neptunism

## Reading a cliff

F19. Superposition: in undisturbed layers of sedimentary rock, "the oldest layer is at the bottom and the youngest layer is at the top", because each new layer is laid down on top of the ones before. It gives relative order only. (Steno as its author is reading-deep-time's.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Law_of_superposition ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Earth_Science/15%3A_Understanding_Earth's_History/15.05%3A_Principles_of_Relative_Dating

F20. Superposition holds only in undisturbed layers. Folds can turn layers past vertical so that "the apparent top becomes the bottom": an overturned fold has "inverted stratigraphy on the overturned side", with the oldest layer on top. Thrust faults can also push older rock over younger. Such stacks need other evidence before they are read.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Law_of_superposition ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fold_(geology)

F21. Original horizontality: "layers of sediment are originally deposited horizontally under the action of gravity", flat or nearly flat, so tilted or folded layers were deformed later, by forces in the crust, usually after the sediment had hardened into rock. Some layers are laid down on slopes (delta fronts, dune faces), but those dip only about 20-35 degrees, never near vertical. A middle-school text: "If sedimentary rock layers are tilted, they must have moved after they were deposited."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Principle_of_original_horizontality ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Earth_Science/15%3A_Understanding_Earth's_History/15.05%3A_Principles_of_Relative_Dating

F22. Folds form when originally flat layers are bent, usually by compression that squeezes and shortens the crust, so that the rock bends rather than breaks; an anticline arches up, a syncline sags down.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fold_(geology)

F23. Cross-cutting relationships: a feature that cuts across rock is younger than the rock it cuts. "Faults, dikes, or unconformities cutting through layers are younger than the affected rocks"; a fault that offsets sandstone layers is younger than the sandstone. A middle-school text: "The cut rock layers are older than the rock that cuts across them."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cross-cutting_relationships ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Law_of_superposition ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Earth_Science/15%3A_Understanding_Earth's_History/15.05%3A_Principles_of_Relative_Dating

F24. What a layer's rock says about the place it formed: limestone packed with seashells forms on a sea floor (F1, F3); coal forms from the plants of swamps (F12); sandstone with sloping cross-beds can be made of wind-blown dunes in an ancient desert, as the Navajo Sandstone was "formed from windblown sand dunes in one of the largest ancient deserts".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Navajo_Sandstone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Coal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Crinoid

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. That sea shells on a mountain mean the sea once rose over the mountain top (sea level has never been within kilometres of it; F4); and the historical wrong theories, a great flood that carried them up, and a formative force that grew the shapes in the rock (F5).

M2. That the same plant on far-apart lands spread there by wind, sea currents or birds (F7, F8).

M3. That any fossil found on two coasts proves they were joined, even one of a creature that can cross the ocean on its own (sharks, seabirds, wind-blown fern spores; F11).

M4. That coal could form under ice, in a desert or on a sea floor (coal needs swamp plants; F12); and the half-truth that the coal shows Antarctica sat at the equator (F14).

M5. That matching mountains on two sides of an ocean rose separately and look alike by chance, or (Werner's Neptunism) that one worldwide sea laid the same rock everywhere (F16, F18).

M6. Reading a cliff from the top down, as if the top layer came first (F19).

M7. That tilted layers were laid down tilted, or that erosion tipped them (F21).

M8. That a crack or fault was there before the layers it cuts (F23).

M9. That the bottom layer is always the oldest, whatever has happened to the stack; or that thickness, number of fossils or colour shows a layer's age (F19, F20).

F27. For the revised 23736dfd81. Lystrosaurus adults "typically measured 1 to 2.5 meters in length and weighed between 50 and 200 kilograms" (about a pig's size); "Lystrosaurus was a herbivore"; it "lacked specialized aquatic adaptations", which confirms "its primary terrestrial lifestyle". Ferns range from small plants to "tree ferns reaching up to 20 meters in height"; they "lack seeds, flowers, and fruits, instead reproducing primarily through spores", which the wind carries "sometimes hundreds of kilometers", with "frequent transoceanic dispersal" (F11). Sharks and seabirds cross oceans (F11). So a fern as tall as a man, a shark and a seabird can each cross an ocean, and a pig-sized plant-eating reptile of the land cannot.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lystrosaurus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fern ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Albatross ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Great_white_shark

F28. Coal is a "combustible black or brownish-black sedimentary rock formed primarily from the compressed remains of ancient vegetation": a black rock made of plants, not hardened lava (a wrong choice in 65d31b180e).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Coal

F29. The contracting-Earth hypothesis, "prevalent in the 19th and early 20th centuries", held that mountain building is caused by the contraction of the Earth as it cooled, like the wrinkles on a drying apple; mountains "mainly result from plate tectonic motions" (A Dictionary of Earth Sciences). Mountain building is slow: the Appalachian and Caledonian belts were built in a series of events about 490-390 million years ago (F16), not by one earthquake. (Wrong choices in a44c1dd82e.)
    https://encyclopedia.com/science/dictionaries-thesauruses-pictures-and-press-releases/contracting-earth-hypothesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Caledonian_orogeny

F30. Frost wedging: "water in cracks, pores, joints, or bedding planes freezes and expands to exert pressure that fractures the rock", ending in "granular disintegration or block detachment", and its effect "is generally limited to surface or near-surface layers". Ice splits rock; it does not stand whole layers on end (a wrong choice in 5d9485919c).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Frost_weathering

F31. A fault that moves while its layers are still being laid down (a growth fault) offsets them unevenly: "the lowermost layer has higher displacement than the uppermost layer". Layers that all show the same offset were all in place before the fault slipped, and layers lying flat and unbroken across a fault settled after it moved (cross-cutting, F23; superposition, F19). (The revised fb3f578248.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth_fault ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cross-cutting_relationships

F32. Superposition applies to "undisturbed layered sedimentary rocks or volcanic ash layers", and sediment can be "transported by water, wind, or ice and settle in layers", so layers laid on land obey it as well as those laid in the sea; "minor gaps known as unconformities may exist but do not invalidate the overall ordering if recognized"; folding can leave "older layers ... above younger ones", and thrust faults move "older blocks over younger". The rule gives the order, not how long each layer took (F19). (Wrong choices in a51b4b684c.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Law_of_superposition
