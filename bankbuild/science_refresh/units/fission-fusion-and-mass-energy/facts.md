## The four fundamental forces

F1. There are four fundamental interactions (forces): gravity, electromagnetism, the strong nuclear force and the weak nuclear force. The strong force is the most powerful and gravity "the weakest by far"; between two protons gravity is "approximately 10^-36 times weaker than the electromagnetic interaction" (a trillion trillion trillion times weaker).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fundamental_interaction ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction

F2. Why gravity rules large scales: gravity "has an infinite effective range, like electromagnetism", and it "always attracts and never repels"; astronomical bodies have close to zero net charge, so electric attraction and repulsion cancel, while "nothing 'cancels' gravity, since it is only attractive" (Wikipedia). Grokipedia: gravity has infinite range, "dominating large-scale structure". The electric (Coulomb) force varies as the inverse square of distance (Wikipedia, Nuclear force), as gravity does, so neither fades faster than the other.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fundamental_interaction ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_force

F3. The weak force "mediates processes like radioactive beta decay" (Grokipedia); "The weak interaction or weak nuclear force is responsible for some nuclear phenomena such as beta decay" (Wikipedia). In beta-minus decay "a neutron decays into a proton, emitting an energetic electron (the beta particle) and an electron antineutrino", as when carbon-14 becomes nitrogen-14. Its range is about 10^-18 m, smaller than a nucleus.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Beta_decay ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fundamental_interaction ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction

F4. The strong force holds the nucleus together: after the nucleus was found, a new force was needed to overcome "the electrostatic repulsion ... of the positively charged protons. Otherwise, the nucleus could not exist" (Wikipedia). The nuclear force is about 100 times the electromagnetic force between two protons in close proximity (Grokipedia), and acts almost identically on protons and neutrons (both sources). It is strongly attractive at about 0.8 femtometre and "rapidly decreases to insignificance at distances beyond about 2.5 fm" (Wikipedia); 2.5 fm is two and a half trillionths of a millimetre. Its influence is confined to nuclear scales, "preventing it from affecting larger structures like atoms or molecules" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nuclear_force ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_force ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction

F5. Each proton or neutron pulls with the strong force only on "the protons and neutrons immediately surrounding it", while the protons push each other away; neutrons "add to the overall strong attraction" without adding repulsion; "the larger the nucleus, the more likely the electric repulsion is to overcome the strong attraction" (Texas Gateway physics). The Coulomb force "has a much greater range" than the nuclear force (Wikipedia).
    https://jukebox.esc13.net/ipsideveloper/gateway/physics/g_pm2l11s1.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_force

## Fission found and explained (Berlin, Sweden, Copenhagen, 1934-1939)

F6. Enrico Fermi, firing neutrons at uranium in 1934, "tentatively concluded that his experiments had created new elements with 93 and 94 protons"; Ida Noddack suggested the nucleus might break into "several large fragments", but the Berlin work of Hahn, Meitner and Strassmann gave what was then read as "strong evidence that they were indeed transuranium elements" (heavier than uranium).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission

F7. Lise Meitner left Berlin on 13 July 1938; after the Anschluss (12 March 1938) ended her Austrian exemption she was "reclassified as a German Jew" under Nazi law. She reached Stockholm and worked at Manne Siegbahn's laboratory, an "exile from physics". Grokipedia calls her "Hahn's collaborator of nearly 30 years".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lise_Meitner ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lise_Meitner ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Otto_Hahn

F8. Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann, bombarding uranium with slow neutrons in Berlin, found barium among the products; Hahn wrote to Meitner on 19 December 1938 asking for a physical interpretation. Meitner answered at once that such a "thoroughgoing breakup seems very difficult to me", but would not call it impossible. Hahn and Strassmann's paper, submitted on 22 December, said that as chemists they should write Ba, La, Ce for Ra, Ac, Th, but as "nuclear chemists" they "cannot yet bring ourselves to take this step".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lise_Meitner

F9. Barium has atomic number 56 (atomic weight 137.3); uranium has atomic number 92. So barium's nucleus holds little more than half of uranium's protons, and its mass is a little over half of uranium-235's.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Barium ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Uranium-235

F10. Christmas 1938 at Kungälv, Sweden: Meitner and her nephew Otto Frisch discussed the result on a walk in the snowy woods, then "sat down on a tree trunk" and calculated "on scraps of paper" (Frisch). The uranium nucleus's charge was large enough to overcome surface tension almost completely, so it was like "a very wobbly unstable drop" that one neutron could divide; the two pieces, driven apart by their electric repulsion, would gain about 200 MeV. Meitner recalled the formula for nuclear masses and found the two pieces together lighter than the uranium nucleus by about one-fifth of a proton's mass; by E = mc², one-fifth of a proton mass "was just equivalent to 200 MeV"; "it all fitted!"
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lise_Meitner ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission

F11. Frisch asked the biologist William A. Arnold what biologists call the process by which a living cell divides in two, was told "fission", and borrowed the word (sources disagree whether Arnold was a biologist or a physicist; the borrowing from biology is agreed).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Robert_Frisch

F12. Frisch's test, Copenhagen, 13 January 1939 (Frisch, Nature 143, 1939): a uranium-lined ionization chamber fed a linear amplifier and a thyratron biased to count only pulses of at least 500,000 ion pairs; on an oscillograph "the large pulses could be seen very distinctly" against "much smaller pulses due to the alpha particles of uranium"; the largest pulses meant an energy loss of about 70 MeV in the chamber. "No pulses at all were recorded during repeated check runs of several hours total duration" when either the neutron source or the uranium lining was removed. The number of pulses rose in step (linearly) with the neutron source's strength, which ruled out small pulses adding up by chance. Surrounding the source with paraffin wax doubled the effect. Thorium gave similar results. Frisch called it "conclusive physical evidence for the breaking up of uranium nuclei into parts of comparable size."
    https://web.chemteam.info/Chem-History/Frisch-Fission-1939.html ; https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/confirmation-of-nuclear-fission ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission

F13. Frisch mailed both papers (the joint Meitner-Frisch explanation and his own experiment) to Nature on 16 January 1939; the joint note appeared on 11 February and the experimental letter on 18 February 1939.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Robert_Frisch

F14. The 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Otto Hahn alone, "for his discovery of the fission of heavy nuclei"; it was announced after the war, in November 1945 (Wikipedia: 15 November; Grokipedia's Hahn page: 16 November; Grokipedia's fission page wrongly gives 1944, so no exact day is used). Neither Meitner nor Strassmann was included. Crawford, Sime and Walker (Physics Today, 1997) summed up her exclusion as "a mixture of disciplinary bias, political obtuseness, ignorance, and haste." Element 109 was named meitnerium in 1997.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lise_Meitner ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Otto_Hahn ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lise_Meitner

## Fission, the chain reaction and the reactor

F15. Uranium-235 fission is "primarily triggered by the absorption of a low-energy, thermal neutron"; it releases "an average of 2.43 neutrons" (two or three) per fission and roughly 200 MeV, most of it (about 170 MeV) as the kinetic energy of the two fragments, which are unequal in size (mass numbers about 95 and 140).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Uranium-235 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nuclear_fission

F16. Chain reaction: in 1939 Halban, Joliot-Curie and Kowarski showed that uranium struck by neutrons "emitted more neutrons than it absorbed, suggesting the possibility of a nuclear chain reaction"; a reactor holds the ratio of neutrons in one generation to the last (the multiplication factor) at one for a steady reaction.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_nuclear_fission ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nuclear_fission

F17. Fission's energy density is "millions of times that of chemical reactions", whose energies are a few electron-volts per atom (Grokipedia). One uranium fuel pellet, about half an inch tall and wide, gives about as much energy as one ton of coal, 149 gallons of oil or 17,000 cubic feet of natural gas (NEI; University of Michigan Center for Sustainable Systems).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nuclear_fission ; https://www.nei.org/fundamentals/nuclear-fuel ; https://css.umich.edu/sites/default/files/2025-09/CSS11-15_0.pdf

F18. In a reactor, uranium in ceramic pellets is stacked in sealed metal fuel rods; "the fuel rods are immersed in water which acts as both a coolant and moderator"; "The heat created by fission turns the water into steam, which spins a turbine to produce electricity"; "Control rods can then be inserted into the reactor core to reduce the reaction rate or withdrawn to increase it" (energy.gov). A control rod is "a neutron-absorbing rod", made of materials "such as boron carbide, cadmium, hafnium, or alloys like silver-indium-cadmium"; "deeper insertion absorbs more neutrons to decrease power output"; a scram rapidly inserts all the rods to stop the chain reaction (Grokipedia).
    https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Control_rod

F19. A moderator slows fast neutrons by collisions; slow (thermal) neutrons are "immensely more susceptible than fast neutrons to propagate a nuclear chain reaction" in uranium-235, and "fission probability decreases as neutron energy (and speed) increases"; light (ordinary) water is the most common moderator, with heavy water and graphite; an ideal moderator absorbs few neutrons. Fermi's group found in October 1934 that neutrons slowed in paraffin wax were "more effective than faster ones in producing certain nuclear reactions."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_moderator ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Neutron_moderator ; https://chem.libretexts.org/Ancillary_Materials/Exemplars_and_Case_Studies/Case_Studies/Nuclear_Energy_for_Today's_World/03._Playing_with_Neutrons

## Mass and energy

F20. Albert Einstein introduced E = mc² in a paper of September 1905; c is the speed of light, and c² is about 9 x 10^16 joules per kilogram, so one gram of mass is worth about 9 x 10^13 joules (90 trillion joules: enough, by simple division, to run a 100-watt bulb for about 28,500 years). Fully fissioning 1 kg of uranium-235 turns roughly 1 gram of mass into energy (about 0.1 percent). Chemical reactions change mass too, by about a billionth (10^-9) or less, "far below measurable thresholds", so chemistry "effectively conserves mass". MISCONCEPTION: that mass is destroyed; mass-energy as a whole is conserved.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence

F21. The mass defect: "The mass of an atomic nucleus is less than the sum of the individual masses of the free constituent protons and neutrons"; the difference "represents the energy that was released when the nucleus was formed", found by E = mc². A helium atom "has a mass about 0.8% less than the total mass of four hydrogen atoms". Fusing light nuclei and splitting heavy ones both release energy because the products have less mass than what went in; iron-56 and nickel-62 are the most tightly bound.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_binding_energy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence

## Fusion

F22. Fusion: "two or more light atomic nuclei collide at extremely high speeds and fuse to form a heavier nucleus". The "electrostatic repulsion between positively charged nuclei" must be overcome so the nuclei come close enough for the strong force, "attractive and dominant at short ranges"; fusion plasmas need "temperatures exceed[ing] 100 million Kelvin", with deuterium-tritium fuel best at 100 to 200 million kelvin (about the same in degrees Celsius). Plasma is "ionized gas", nuclei stripped of electrons. Deuterium and tritium (heavy forms of hydrogen) fuse into helium-4 and a neutron, releasing 17.6 MeV from the mass defect. Plasma is confined by magnetic fields (tokamaks, stellarators) or by laser-driven implosion (inertial confinement). Quantum tunnelling helps nuclei through the barrier.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nuclear_fusion

F23. The National Ignition Facility (Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, California), 5 December 2022: 192 laser beams delivered 2.05 MJ to a frozen deuterium-tritium fuel pellet held in a gold cylinder (hohlraum) about the size of a pencil eraser, and the shot yielded 3.15 MJ of fusion energy: ignition, "also known as scientific energy breakeven", "meaning it produced more energy from fusion than the laser energy used to drive it" (LLNL). Powering the lasers took about 300 MJ from the grid (Physics Today; Scientific American gives 322 MJ), so the whole facility's energy budget fell far short of breakeven; the shot was "split-second". Later shots gave 3.88 MJ (30 July 2023), 5.0 MJ (February 2025) and 8.6 MJ (7 April 2025). Fusion "remains pre-commercial, with no device yet producing more electricity than consumed". NIF's main mission supports the US nuclear stockpile.
    https://www.llnl.gov/article/49306/lawrence-livermore-national-laboratory-achieves-fusion-ignition ; https://grokipedia.com/page/National_Ignition_Facility ; https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/national-ignition-facility-surpasses-long-awaited-fusion-milestone ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/nuclear-fusion-lab-achieves-ignition-what-does-it-mean/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nuclear_fusion

## Nuclear power: benefits and waste

F24. Nuclear power supplies "about 9% of the world's electricity" (Grokipedia). US nuclear plants produced maximum power "more than 92% of the time" in 2020, about twice the rate of gas and coal plants and three times that of wind and solar, which are limited by "a lack of fuel (i.e. wind, sun, or water)"; plants refuel "typically every 1.5 or 2 years" (energy.gov).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nuclear_power ; https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-power-most-reliable-energy-source-and-its-not-even-close

F25. Life-cycle greenhouse emissions, IPCC 2014 medians, in grams of CO2-equivalent per kilowatt-hour: coal 820, natural gas 490, solar (utility) 48, hydro 24, nuclear 12, onshore wind 11, offshore wind 12. The fission process itself produces no carbon dioxide.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life-cycle_greenhouse_gas_emissions_of_energy_sources ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nuclear_power

F26. Used (spent) fuel: "The fuel is a solid when it goes into the reactor and a solid when it comes out"; it is first stored in steel-lined concrete pools of water, which cool it and shield its radiation, then in dry casks of steel and concrete. The US makes about 2,000 metric tons a year; all of it since the 1950s (about 90,000 t) "could fit on a single football field at a depth of less than 10 yards"; more than 2,500 cask shipments have crossed the US "without any radiological releases to the environment or harm to the public" (energy.gov). Pools are used "to cool it and provide shielding from its radioactivity" (Wikipedia).
    https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/5-fast-facts-about-spent-nuclear-fuel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spent_nuclear_fuel

F27. Used fuel "stays a radiation hazard for extended periods of time", and a repository must keep it away from people "for thousands of years" (Wikipedia); its radioactivity falls to that of the original ore only after about 200,000-300,000 years (Grokipedia). Finland's Onkalo is "the world's first operational deep geological facility for spent fuel"; the US has no licensed repository (Yucca Mountain halted since 2010).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spent_nuclear_fuel

## Misconceptions a 9th grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- The strongest force must reach farthest (F4: the strong force reaches only its nearest neighbours).
- Gravity rules space because the electric force fades faster or cannot cross space (F2: both fall off the same way; charges cancel).
- The "weak" force is the weakest (F1: gravity is far weaker).
- A neutron chips small pieces off a nucleus, or builds it up heavier (F6, F9: Fermi's reading; barium is about half uranium).
- A chain reaction spreads by heat, like a fire, or by the flying pieces (F15, F16: by the neutrons).
- Control rods slow neutrons, or cool the core (F18, F19: they absorb neutrons; slowing them is the moderator's job and makes fission likelier).
- A nucleus weighs less because its parts squeeze into less room, or because gravity is weaker inside (F21: the missing mass left as energy).
- Fusion is burning, like a fire in oxygen (F22).
- "Ignition" meant the laboratory made more energy than it used (F23: only the fuel did).
- Nuclear waste is a glowing green liquid, or a vast volume (F26: solid; one football field).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F28. Electrons do not feel the strong force at all, at any distance: a lepton is a fermion "that does not participate in the strong nuclear force"; leptons "lack color charge and therefore do not participate in the strong nuclear interaction" (Grokipedia, Lepton). The strong force's short reach (F4) is why it never reaches the next atom, not why electrons escape it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lepton

F29. Meitner's partnership with Hahn was "initiated by the end of 1907" and "would endure for over three decades" (Grokipedia, Lise Meitner, which elsewhere says "nearly 30 years"); their first joint papers came out in 1908 and 1909 (Wikipedia); she left Berlin on 13 July 1938 (F7). Late 1907 to July 1938 is some thirty years.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lise_Meitner ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lise_Meitner

F30. Two kinds of water reactor (energy.gov): "More than 65% of the commercial reactors in the United States are pressurized-water reactors or PWRs", which "pump water into the reactor core under high pressure to prevent the water from boiling"; through a heat exchanger "Those tubes heat a separate water source to create steam." Boiling water reactors "heat water and produce steam directly inside the reactor vessel." Either way the water round the fuel carries the heat away to make the steam.
    https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work

F31. At NIF the laser light does not strike the pellet: "the 2.05 MJ of laser light that reached the small cylindrical chamber known as a hohlraum, which converts the UV to x rays"; "The hohlraum then emits x rays, which compress the fuel inside"; "the amount of energy deposited on the fuel capsule" was "around 250 kilojoules" (Physics Today). The capsule sits "within a cylindrical gold hohlraum approximately the size of a pencil eraser" (Grokipedia, National Ignition Facility).
    https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/national-ignition-facility-surpasses-long-awaited-fusion-milestone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/National_Ignition_Facility

F32. Yucca Mountain: the Obama administration "proposed eliminating funding for the Yucca Mountain repository in the fiscal year 2010 budget", and in January 2010 "Energy Secretary Steven Chu announced the DOE's intent to withdraw the license application"; the licensing process "remains suspended", "with no high-level waste emplaced as of 2025" (Grokipedia, Yucca Mountain nuclear waste repository). The United States has no permanent repository for used fuel (F27: Yucca Mountain halted since 2010). Finland's Onkalo is built and was licensed to operate on 22 October 2026, with disposal still to begin (fact-check note), so "has built" is the right wording.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Yucca_Mountain_nuclear_waste_repository ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spent_nuclear_fuel ; https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/23/finland-nearing-opening-of-worlds-first-permanent-nuclear-waste-repository

F33. Cask shipments: DOE says more than 2,500 have crossed the US "without any radiological releases to the environment or harm to the public" (F26), meaning no radioactive material got out of a cask. So a choice saying used fuel escapes its casks whenever it is moved is wrong.
    https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/5-fast-facts-about-spent-nuclear-fuel
