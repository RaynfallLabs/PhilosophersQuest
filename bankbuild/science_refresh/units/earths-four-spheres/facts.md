## The four spheres and their parts

F1. Earth's major systems are the geosphere (solid and molten rock, soil and sediments), the hydrosphere (water and ice), the atmosphere (air) and the biosphere (living things, including humans). Each is a system, and they act on one another.
    https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ess2-1-earths-systems (NGSS 5-ESS2-1 and DCI ESS2.A) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth_system_science

F2. The geosphere is the solid, inorganic part of Earth, "encompassing all rocks, minerals, sediments, soils", and Earth's interior.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Geosphere ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ess2-1-earths-systems

F3. Soil is made mostly of mineral grains from weathered rock, with organic matter, water and air: typically about 45 percent minerals, 5 percent organic matter, 25 percent water and 25 percent air by volume. Grokipedia calls soil a meeting place ("interfaces") of the lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere and biosphere; sphere models for schools put it in the geosphere (F1, F2).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Soil

F4. The hydrosphere is all the water on Earth in its liquid, solid and gas forms: oceans, seas, lakes, rivers, streams, groundwater, glaciers, ice caps and the moisture in the air (humidity and clouds). Ice is part of it; some scientists name the frozen part separately as the cryosphere, and Grokipedia's Hydrosphere page treats that as a subset.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrosphere ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth_system_science

F5. Groundwater is water underground that fills the pore spaces, cracks and fractures in soil, sediment and rock; it is part of the water cycle, and it is about 30 percent of Earth's fresh water.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Groundwater ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrosphere

F6. The atmosphere is the layer (envelope) of gases around Earth. Dry air is 78.08 percent nitrogen, 20.95 percent oxygen, 0.934 percent argon and about 0.04 percent carbon dioxide; water vapour, the most variable gas, is 0 to 4 percent of the air near the ground.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Earth

F7. The air's gases are well mixed: from the ground up to about 80-100 km, stirring keeps the main gases in "constant relative proportions that are independent of altitude". The share of oxygen, about 21 percent, "remains practically unchanged up to 21,350 metres".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Homosphere ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude_sickness

F8. Air pressure and density fall with height; in the lower atmosphere the pressure halves about every 5.5 km. At about 8,000 m the pressure is roughly a third of that at sea level; at the summit of the highest mountain (about 8,848 m) it is about 253 mmHg, against 760 mmHg at sea level. Less oxygen reaches a climber because the whole air pressure is lower.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Earth ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Effects_of_high_altitude_on_humans ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude_sickness

F9. Above about 8,000 m (the "death zone") the oxygen is too little for sustained human life without supplemental oxygen, and bottled (open-circuit) oxygen is the standard supply in high-altitude mountaineering.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Death_zone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Effects_of_high_altitude_on_humans

F10. In the lowest layer of the atmosphere, temperature falls with height, on average by about 6.5 degrees Celsius per kilometre.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Earth

## How the spheres act on one another

F11. Water acting on rock (hydrosphere on geosphere): "precipitation and river flows break down rocks", and weathering by water and wind breaks rock into sediments that help form soil. Carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in rainwater as a weak acid that dissolves limestone.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth_system_science ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Geosphere ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering

F12. Living things acting on rock (biosphere on geosphere): plant roots grow into cracks in rock and pry it apart (root wedging); "root growth and burrowing" also alter soils.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Geosphere

F13. Living things acting on the air (biosphere on atmosphere): plants take in carbon dioxide from the air by photosynthesis and release oxygen.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth_system_science

F14. A volcano acting on the air (geosphere on atmosphere): "Volcanism represents a direct geospheric input to the atmosphere." Water vapour is the main volcanic gas, typically 60-90 percent of it; carbon dioxide is next (10-40 percent), then sulfur dioxide (1-10 percent). USGS: "By far the most abundant volcanic gas is water vapor." Oxygen is not among a volcano's main gases.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Geosphere ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Volcanic_gas ; https://www.usgs.gov/programs/VHP/volcanic-gases-can-be-harmful-health-vegetation-and-infrastructure

F15. Volcanic gases come from water, carbon dioxide and other gases dissolved in magma under high pressure; as the magma rises and the pressure falls, they come out of the melt as bubbles.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Volcanic_gas

F16. Volcanic ash is "tiny fragments of jagged rock, minerals, and volcanic glass", each less than 2 mm across. It is hard and rocky, not like the soft ash left by burning wood.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Volcanic_ash

F17. A volcano acting on living things (geosphere on biosphere): over time, weathered volcanic ash and rock make some of the most fertile soils on Earth (Andisols, "among the most productive soils globally for agriculture"), adding minerals and holding water well; many prime farming regions have soils of volcanic origin. In the short term a thick ash fall buries crops and pasture.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Volcanic_ash ; https://www.usgs.gov/news/volcano-watch-volcanic-explosions-provide-foundation-agriculture ; https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130213203807/http://vulcan.wr.usgs.gov/LivingWith/PlusSide/fertile_soils.html

F18. The ocean acting on the air (hydrosphere on atmosphere): the ocean takes in most of the solar energy reaching Earth, stores it and gives heat back to the atmosphere ("air-sea exchanges of momentum, heat, and freshwater"); it warms and cools more slowly than the atmosphere, and evaporation from the ocean makes most rain clouds.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth_system_science ; https://wmo.int/content/how-ocean-shapes-weather-and-climate

F19. The air acting on the sea (atmosphere on hydrosphere): "Waves are most commonly caused by wind"; wind-driven waves are made by friction between the wind and the water's surface. Waves raised by the pull of the Moon and the Sun are the tides, and tsunamis are very long waves set off by underwater disturbances, such as earthquakes, that shove a great deal of water quickly.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/wavesinocean.html

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Air is mostly oxygen. NASA's eClips Spotlite on the atmosphere and Oak National Academy's lessons both name it as a common student belief; nitrogen is about 78 percent (F6).
    https://science.nasa.gov/?p=1029102 ; https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/combined-science-secondary-ks4-foundation-edexcel/units/atmosphere-and-changing-climate/lessons/earths-atmosphere

M2. High air holds a smaller share of oxygen, or oxygen sinks to the bottom of the air. In fact the share stays about 21 percent; the air is thinner (F7, F8). Oak National Academy lists the belief that the atmosphere is the same at all heights.
    https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/combined-science-secondary-ks4-foundation-edexcel/units/atmosphere-and-changing-climate/lessons/earths-atmosphere

M3. Water underground or inside rock is part of the geosphere, and solid ice or snow is "rock". In the four-sphere model all water, in any form or place, is hydrosphere (F1, F4, F5). (Teaching note.)

M4. Volcanic ash is burnt material like the ash of a fire; it is broken rock and glass (F16).

M5. Higher ground is nearer the Sun and so warmer; the air in fact cools with height (F10). (Teaching note.)

M6. The Moon makes the waves that break on a beach; the Moon makes the tides, and wind makes most waves (F19). (Teaching note; the T2 `the-tides` unit teaches the Moon and the tides.)
