## Nicolas Steno and the tongue stones

F1. Nicolas Steno (Niels Stensen, 1638-1686) was a Danish anatomist, geologist and later a Catholic bishop; in the 1660s he worked in Florence for the Medici court.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nicolas_Steno ; https://evolution.berkeley.edu/the-history-of-evolutionary-thought/pre-1800/fossils-and-the-birth-of-paleontology-nicholas-steno

F2. "Tongue stones" (glossopetrae) are triangular stones found in rock, known since ancient times. Pliny the Elder said they fell from the sky (during lunar eclipses); in Malta a legend tied them to Saint Paul and the island's snakes; others held that fossils grew naturally inside the rock (Athanasius Kircher's "lapidifying virtue"). People wore them as charms against poison.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossopetrae ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/steno.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/nicolaus-steno

F3. In October 1666 fishermen caught a huge shark (NASA: a great white) near Livorno, and Grand Duke Ferdinando II de' Medici had its head sent to Steno, who dissected it. He saw that its teeth matched the tongue stones and concluded the stones "were shark teeth" from once-living sharks. (He was not the first: Fabio Colonna had argued earlier that the stones were organic.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Steno ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/nicolaus-steno ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/steno.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nicolas_Steno

F4. The tooth led Steno to ask how one solid comes to lie inside another. His answer, in his own words: "If a solid body is enclosed on all sides by another solid body, of the two bodies that one first became hard which, in the mutual contact, expresses on its own surface the properties of the other surface." So a tongue stone "must have been buried in soft sediments which hardened later": fossils hardened before the rock that holds them.
    https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/steno.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nicolas_Steno

F5. In his Prodromus (Florence, 1669) Steno set out that layers of rock settle from water, at first flat, one on another: "When the lowest stratum was being formed, none of the upper strata existed." So in undisturbed layers the oldest lies at the bottom and the youngest on top: the law of superposition, the foundation of relative dating. He is called the father of stratigraphy.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Law_of_superposition ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/nicolaus-steno ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/steno.html

F6. Steno himself accepted the biblical timeline and saw "no difficulty" in giving most rock layers to the Flood; near Florence he found lower layers without fossils under fossil-rich ones. After 1667 he left science for the Church (priest 1675, bishop 1677).
    https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/steno.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/nicolaus-steno ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nicolas_Steno

## Relative age and absolute age

F7. Relative age is the order of rocks and events (which is older, which younger) with no number of years; absolute age is a numerical age in years. Superposition gives only relative age. NGSS (ESS1.C): "Analyses of rock strata and the fossil record provide only relative dates, not an absolute scale."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Law_of_superposition ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ess1-4-earths-place-universe

## William Smith and index fossils

F8. William Smith (1769-1839), son of a village blacksmith and mostly self-taught, was an English land surveyor and canal engineer (he surveyed for the Somerset Coal Canal around 1794). In coal mines and canal cuts he found the rock layers always in the same order, and that "each stratum contained organized fossils peculiar to itself", so a layer could be identified and matched from place to place by its fossils, even where the rock itself looked different. NASA: layers could look alike ("a sandy deposit in one place and a similar sandy deposit elsewhere"), and Smith "found the key to telling the layers apart: their fossils were different"; as surveyor for the Somerset Coal Canal he "was able to track rock layers across the landscape". (Wikipedia: he went to Somerset in 1791 and was the canal company's surveyor until 1799.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/William_Smith_(geologist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Smith_(geologist) ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/william-smith/

F9. Smith's 1815 map, "A Delineation of the Strata of England and Wales, with part of Scotland", hand-coloured and about 8 by 6 feet, was the first national geological map (Wikipedia: "the first detailed, nationwide geological map of any country"; William Maclure's rougher map of the United States came out in 1809). He was nicknamed "Strata Smith"; in 1831 he received the Geological Society's first Wollaston Medal, and Adam Sedgwick called him "the Father of English Geology".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/William_Smith_(geologist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Smith_(geologist)

F10. Faunal succession: fossils lie in rock layers in a consistent, predictable order, so shared fossils let geologists match layers in distant places (correlation) and say which layer is older or younger, without ages in years.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Principle_of_faunal_succession ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_fossil

F11. A good index fossil has a wide geographic spread, a short span in time, abundance and features easy to recognise (Wikipedia: "a limited vertical time range, wide geographic distribution"). Layers far apart that hold the same index fossil formed while that species lived, so they are about the same age, even when the rock looks different. Ammonites, trilobites and graptolites are classic index fossils.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Principle_of_faunal_succession ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_fossil

## James Hutton at Siccar Point

F12. James Hutton (1726-1797) of Edinburgh landed at Siccar Point on the Berwickshire coast of Scotland by boat in 1788 (Grokipedia: June), with John Playfair and James Hall. There nearly vertical layers of grey Silurian greywacke (about 440 million years old) lie under gently sloping layers of red Devonian sandstone (about 375 million years old). The grey layers were laid down, buried, hardened, folded and lifted up, worn flat, and then buried under the new sediment.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Hutton ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Siccar_Point ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siccar_Point

F13. Such a junction, tilted older layers under flat younger ones, is an angular unconformity. An unconformity is a gap in the rock record: time is missing there (about 65 million years at Siccar Point).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Siccar_Point ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Law_of_superposition ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siccar_Point

F14. Hutton reasoned that erosion and deposition work very slowly today, so what the rocks showed needed enormous spans of time; he rejected the view that Earth was only a few thousand years old. He closed his 1788 Theory of the Earth: "we find no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end." He rejected Abraham Werner's Neptunism (all rocks settled out of one early ocean) and catastrophism such as a worldwide flood. He is called the father of modern geology.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Hutton ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hutton ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Deep_time

## Natural laws worked the same in the past (uniformitarianism)

F15. Uniformitarianism holds that the natural processes seen today worked throughout Earth's history, so the past is read through the present; its core assumption is that the laws of nature stay the same through time and space. Hutton first set it out; Charles Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830-1833) won it wide acceptance; William Whewell coined the word in 1832. Modern geology keeps the same laws but lets rates vary and allows rare great events, such as the asteroid impact at the end of the Cretaceous. NGSS Nature of Science 6-8: scientific knowledge assumes an order and consistency in natural systems (the curriculum map's G7.M2 standard).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Uniformitarianism ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hutton

F16. Ripple marks are small ridges in sand made by moving water or wind, and they can be preserved in sandstone. Geologists interpret fossil ripples "by comparing them with modern examples forming in known settings" (symmetrical ripples from waves, lopsided ones from one-way currents).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ripple_marks

## The geologic time scale

F17. The geologic time scale divides Earth's history into eons (the largest units), eras, periods, epochs and ages. The four eons are the Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic and Phanerozoic; the Phanerozoic's three eras are the Paleozoic ("old life"), Mesozoic ("middle life") and Cenozoic ("new life"). It was built in the 1800s from the order of rock layers (superposition) and their fossils (Smith's faunal succession); ages in years came only in the 1900s, with radiometric dating (Arthur Holmes, 1911).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Geologic_time_scale ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_time_scale ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ess1-4-earths-place-universe

F18. John Phillips, William Smith's nephew, published in 1841 the first global geologic time scale, ordering strata by the fossils found in them; he coined "Mesozoic".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Phillips_(geologist) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mesozoic

F19. The lines between the eras fall at mass extinctions. The Paleozoic (about 538.8 to 251.9 million years ago) ended with the Permian-Triassic extinction, about 252 million years ago, "the Great Dying", which killed about 90% of marine species and about 70% of land vertebrate species. The Mesozoic (about 252 to 66 million years ago) ended with the Cretaceous-Paleogene extinction about 66 million years ago, which killed roughly 76% of species and all the non-bird dinosaurs, and began the Cenozoic. The eras are not equal in length (about 287, 186 and 66 million years).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Paleozoic ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mesozoic ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Permian%E2%80%93Triassic_extinction_event ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cretaceous%E2%80%93Paleogene_extinction_event

## Earth's age

F20. Earth is about 4.6 billion years old: NGSS MS-ESS1-4 speaks of "Earth's 4.6-billion-year-old history" and the US National Park Service gives "~4.6 billion years"; the precise modern figure is 4.54 plus or minus 0.05 billion years, and the solar system's oldest solids are about 4.57 billion years old.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Age_of_Earth ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ess1-4-earths-place-universe ; https://home.nps.gov/subjects/geology/age-of-the-earth.htm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Geologic_time_scale

F21. Estimates of the 1800s fell far short: Lord Kelvin (1862), from how fast Earth cools, gave 20 to 400 million years and later (1897) 20 to 40 million; John Phillips, from how fast sediment piles up, about 96 million. The universe is about 13.8 billion years old.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Age_of_Earth ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Earth ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Age_of_the_universe

## Earth's history as one day

F22. Modern humans (Homo sapiens) first appeared in Africa about 300,000 years ago (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, about 315,000); early Homo about 2.8 million years ago; the human line split from the chimpanzees' about 7 million years ago.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Human

F23. Compressing Earth's 4.6 billion years into a single day is a standard teaching model (Wikipedia's Geologic Calendar gives the 24-hour version: dinosaurs appear about 10 pm, and modern humans "would not appear until the last two seconds of 11:59 pm"). Author's arithmetic, from F19, F20 and F22: one second of the day stands for about 53,000 years (4.6 billion / 86,400); the end of the dinosaurs, 66 million years ago, falls about 21 minutes before midnight; the first modern humans, about 300,000 years ago, about 6 seconds before midnight (2 to 6 seconds, by the date used); the human line's split, about 7 million years ago, about 2 minutes before midnight.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_Calendar ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Human ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cretaceous%E2%80%93Paleogene_extinction_event

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. That humans and dinosaurs lived at the same time: 26% of students in one introductory geology study held it before instruction; an instructor lists it among the commonest misconceptions, with "humans and the Earth are roughly the same age" and "the Earth is only several thousand years old".
    https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/ees/63 ; https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/time/workshop2012/essays/kapp.html

M2. Students usually get the order of Earth's events right but the scale of the time between them badly wrong.
    https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/38940.html

M3. The older explanations of tongue stones (F2): stones that grew inside the rock, or fell from the sky.

M4. Respectable wrong theories of Hutton's day: Werner's Neptunism (every rock settled out of one early ocean) and a worldwide flood laying down the rocks (F6, F14).

M5. That uniformitarianism means Earth always looked as it does now. It means the same laws, not the same landscape (F15).

M6. That layers and fossils alone give ages in years (F7: they give only relative dates).

M7. Author's notes, not a documented study: confusing Earth's age with the universe's (13.8 billion, F21), and millions with billions.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26; read on the web that day)

F25. Steno's answer to the growth view: UCMP: a tongue stone that grew inside rock would have been distorted by the surrounding rock; instead it "must have been buried in soft sediments which hardened later", the teeth having been buried "in mud or sand that was now dry land". Grokipedia: fossils were "buried within sedimentary solids", and Steno rejected the view that they were "inorganic formations or products of nature's 'sports'".
    https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/steno.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nicolas_Steno

F26. Rock that forms from melted rock (lava at the surface, magma underground) almost never holds fossils: "the molten and partially molten material that make igneous rocks tend to wipe away the traces of ancient organisms" (Discover); fossils in volcanic ash are a rare exception. (The T1 unit rock-layers-and-fossils sources the same fact, its F10; here it is only a lure's explanation.)
    https://www.discovermagazine.com/why-are-fossils-only-found-in-sedimentary-rocks-44759 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Magma

F27. Smith's 1815 map was not the first geological map of a country: Wikipedia credits Smith with "the first detailed, nationwide geological map of any country", says "this was not the world's first geological map", and that "a map of the United States by William Maclure was published six years earlier" (1809); Smith's was "the first geological map covering such a large area in detail". (Grokipedia says "the first national geological map" and does not mention Maclure; the question follows Wikipedia's narrower "first detailed".)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Smith_(geologist) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/William_Smith_(geologist)

F28. Ages for Earth were given in Hutton's day but none was known or measured: Buffon concluded in 1778, from cooling iron spheres, that Earth needed about 75,000 years to cool from a molten state; James Ussher's 1650 reckoning from scripture put creation in 4004 BC; geologists of the 1800s argued for deep time "without precise figures" (Grokipedia). The modern value is 4.54 plus or minus 0.05 billion years, from radiometric dating.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Age_of_Earth

F29. The Sun has brightened only slowly: it gave out about 70-75% of its present light about 4 billion years ago, and its brightness "has increased gradually over its lifetime", a "monotonic brightening" (Grokipedia). So no sudden bright spell of the Sun marks the era lines, which fall at mass extinctions (F19).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Faint_young_Sun_paradox

M8. Lures added in the revision: a tooth that fell into a crack in solid rock (a naive account; F25's print and sediment reasoning answers it), and Earth about 5 million years old (the millions-for-billions mix-up of M7).
