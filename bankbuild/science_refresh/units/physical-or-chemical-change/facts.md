## The two kinds of change, and how to tell them apart

F1. A physical change alters properties such as shape, size, density or state of matter but not the chemical composition: the substance stays the same substance. Examples: melting ice, boiling water, evaporation, cutting or crushing a solid, dissolving sugar in water. National Geographic: physical changes "change the form of a substance, but not its molecular structure"; sanding wood, melting ice and heating water into steam are its examples.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/changes-matter-physical-vs-chemical-changes/

F2. A chemical change (a chemical reaction) transforms one or more substances into "different substances" (products) as bonds break and form, producing "entirely new substances with different properties". Rusting (iron with oxygen and water gives iron(III) oxide) and burning wood are examples. Some processes, such as a burning candle, involve both physical and chemical changes.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/changes-matter-physical-vs-chemical-changes/

F3. Seventh grade: TEKS 7.6C asks students to "distinguish between physical and chemical changes in matter" (the signs of change are 6th grade); NGSS MS-PS1-2 has students compare properties before and after substances interact to decide whether a reaction happened.
    https://teksguide.org/teks/s76c/alignments ; https://tea.texas.gov/academics/subject-areas/science/tif-physical-and-chemical-changes-may-24-0.pdf ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps1-2-matter-and-its-interactions

F4. A physical change can often be undone by physical means, such as evaporating the water to recover a dissolved solid; for salt, National Geographic notes the salt "remains after all the water has evaporated". Salt works make salt this way: sun and wind evaporate seawater or brine until sodium chloride crystallizes. (Nuance: National Geographic calls dissolving salt one of the cases that are hard to classify; school science, Grokipedia and the TEKS teach it as physical.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/changes-matter-physical-vs-chemical-changes/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Salt_evaporation_pond

F5. Mass is conserved in a chemical reaction too: "the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products, and no atoms are created or destroyed". So a mass that stays the same does not show that a change was physical.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction

F6. The test is whether a new substance forms, not whether the change can be undone. The Royal Society of Chemistry: "The key point required is that a chemical reaction involves making a new substance." Students' own definitions show the belief that a physical change "Can be easily reversed" and a chemical change "Is not easily reversed" (Schollum 1981). National Geographic calls the idea that chemical changes cannot be reversed "not true". Some physical changes, such as shattering glass, are hard or impossible to undo in practice but make no new substance.
    https://edu.rsc.org/resources/introducing-chemical-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017789.article ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/changes-matter-physical-vs-chemical-changes/ ; https://sciencenotes.org/chemical-and-physical-changes-of-matter/

## Breaking glass

F7. Broken glass is crushed into cullet and remelted into new glass; glass "can be remelted repeatedly without altering its fundamental chemical structure". Breaking glass is a physical change: the shards are still glass.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Glass_recycling ; https://sciencenotes.org/chemical-and-physical-changes-of-matter/

F8. Ordinary (soda-lime) glass is made by cooling a molten mixture of silica (sand), soda ash and lime: it is not an element. Glass is chemically resistant, yet hydrofluoric acid etches (corrodes) its surface, so a hard material can still react. Fast changes can be chemical too: a firework's burst charge reacts in a flash (F15).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Glass

## Tarnishing silver

F9. Tarnish on pure silver is the compound silver sulfide, formed when silver reacts with sulfur-containing gases in the air; the most common is hydrogen sulfide (from wool, rubber, eggs, people and pollution). Thick tarnish is black, "the true colour of silver sulfide". Silver sulfide (Ag2S) holds no oxygen.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tarnish ; https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/preventive-conservation/guidelines-collections/metal-objects/understanding-silver-tarnish.html

F10. Each time a silver object tarnishes and is cleaned, some silver is lost; polishing "abrades the tarnish but thins the metal over time". Pure gold does not tarnish.
    https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/preventive-conservation/guidelines-collections/metal-objects/understanding-silver-tarnish.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tarnish

## A burning candle (Faraday)

F11. Michael Faraday gave the Royal Institution's Christmas Lectures on the chemistry of a candle to a young audience in London, first in 1848-49 and again in 1860-61; they were published as The Chemical History of a Candle.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/The_Chemical_History_of_a_Candle ; https://edu.rsc.org/feature/the-chemistry-of-candles/3007566.article

F12. Faraday blew a candle out and lit the vapour rising from it: "If you blow a candle out cleverly, you will see the vapour rise from it," and a light held in it sends "a train of fire going through the air till it reaches the candle". The vapour is the wax: "the vapour into which this solid matter is transformed". The RSC: bring a flame to the wick from above, and the candle relights before the flame touches the wick, showing that the cloud of gaseous wax is what burns, not liquid wax.
    https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Chemical_History_of_a_Candle/Lecture_I ; https://edu.rsc.org/feature/the-chemistry-of-candles/3007566.article ; https://grokipedia.com/page/The_Chemical_History_of_a_Candle

F13. In a burning candle, heat from the flame melts the wax next to the wick, the liquid wax is drawn up the wick by capillary action, and the heat vaporizes it; the wax vapour burns with oxygen, producing carbon dioxide, water vapour, heat and light. Melting and wicking are physical; the burning is a chemical change. Carbon dioxide and water vapour do not burn.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Candle ; https://grokipedia.com/page/The_Chemical_History_of_a_Candle

## Burning a match, and burning iron

F14. A match's wooden splint burns after the head lights. Burning wood reacts with oxygen "to form ash, carbon dioxide, and water vapor", substances different from the wood. The black, charred stick left is charcoal: "a lightweight, black, porous carbonaceous solid", mostly carbon, formed when wood is heated with little oxygen. Wood does not melt.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Match ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Charcoal

F15. Steel wool is mainly iron (0.1-0.3% carbon); fine grades "ignite readily when sparked with a ferrocerium rod or by touching the terminals of a 9-volt battery". Burning iron joins it with oxygen: 4Fe + 3O2 gives 2Fe2O3, iron(III) oxide. Rusting also joins iron with oxygen to make iron(III) oxide (rust is "primarily hydrated iron(III) oxide"); it happens "in the presence of oxygen and moisture", "dry iron does not rust", and it runs at ordinary temperatures (fastest around 20-40 degrees Celsius), with no flame to start it and no light given off. Rusting and burning are both oxidation, one slow and one fast.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Steel_wool ; https://chem.washington.edu/lecture-demos/combustion-steel-wool ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rust ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction

## A firework, and some physical lookalikes

F16. A firework's lift and burst charges are black powder (gunpowder), a mechanical mixture of saltpetre (potassium nitrate, about 75%), charcoal and sulfur; the nitrate supplies the oxygen, so it burns "without external oxygen". It "rapidly decomposes into gases, primarily nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and water vapor", with potassium sulfide and smoke; in a shell, this "rapid combustion and analogous gas expansion ... ruptures the shell casing".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gunpowder ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fireworks

F17. Dry ice is solid carbon dioxide. At normal pressure it sublimes, going straight from solid to gas at -78.5 degrees Celsius with no liquid; its white fog is water vapour from the surrounding air condensed into droplets by the cold gas, not carbon dioxide. No new substance forms.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dry_ice

F18. Sweat is about 99% water, with salts (mainly sodium and chloride); it cools the body as it evaporates from the skin. Evaporation is a physical change.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Perspiration ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change

F19. A geode is a hollow, roughly spherical rock lined with crystals, often quartz, which are revealed only when the rind is broken open. Cutting or crushing a solid is a physical change (F1).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Geode ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change

## Rocks: weathering

F20. Physical weathering "breaks rocks into smaller fragments without altering their chemical composition"; in frost wedging, water freezing in cracks expands by about 9% and pries rock apart. Chemical weathering "involves reactions that change the mineral composition of rocks", producing new minerals such as clays. Carbon dioxide dissolves in rainwater to form carbonic acid.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering ; https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/ks3/gsl/education/resources/rockcycle/page3659.html

F21. Feldspar, a main mineral of granite, is chemically weathered (by hydrolysis) into the clay mineral kaolinite; "most silicate minerals - apart from quartz (SiO2) - are broken down to form clay minerals and soluble salts". Carbonic acid in rainwater attacks granite. Kaolinite is a soft (Mohs 2 to 2.5), fine-grained, earthy, white mineral that is plastic when wet; weathered granite shows chalky white patches where feldspar has become kaolinite. Quartz resists this weathering.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kaolinite ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering ; https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/ks3/gsl/education/resources/rockcycle/page3659.html ; https://geo.libretexts.org/Courses/City_Colleges_of_Chicago/PHY_SCI_101%3A_General_Course_Physical_Science/04%3A_Weathering_Erosion_and_Sedimentary_Rocks/4.02%3A_Investigation_-_Chemical_Weathering_of_Feldspar_at_Atomic_Scale

## The body: digestion

F22. Mechanical digestion "physically fragments larger food particles through actions like chewing and muscular contractions" (the stomach's churning); chemical digestion uses enzymes to split large molecules into smaller ones. Saliva contains amylase, "which initiates the chemical digestion of starches into simpler sugars" (maltose and short chains called dextrins).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Digestion

## A glow that is not a reaction

F23. In an incandescent bulb, an electric current heats a tungsten filament until it glows, at roughly 2,200-2,700 degrees Celsius; over 90% of the energy leaves as heat and under 10% as visible light. The filament sits "within a vacuum or inert gas atmosphere to prevent oxidation". It fails by sublimation: tungsten atoms escape from its surface as a gas, thinning it and blackening the glass, until it breaks. The wire stays tungsten while it glows.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Incandescent_light_bulb

## A solid that vanishes in a chemical change

F24. Zinc added to dilute hydrochloric acid gives off hydrogen gas and forms "a clear zinc chloride solution" (Zn + 2HCl gives ZnCl2 + H2); zinc chloride is a white crystalline solid, highly soluble in water. So a solid can vanish into a clear liquid in a chemical change too: vanishing and a clear liquid do not prove a change was physical.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Zinc_chloride

F25. Tungsten, like iron and magnesium, is a metal, and metals do react: iron rusts (F15), and magnesium "appears as a shiny, silvery-white solid" that is highly flammable, "burning with a bright white light". A hot tungsten wire must be kept from oxygen "to prevent oxidation" (F23).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnesium ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Incandescent_light_bulb

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Reversible means physical and irreversible means chemical (F6). A shattered beaker cannot be put back, yet its pieces are still glass.
    https://edu.rsc.org/resources/introducing-chemical-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017789.article

M2. Signs are proof: light and heat (a glowing bulb), fog and bubbles (dry ice) feel like a reaction. The signs are clues only (T2's lesson).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction

M3. Burning is melting: students think only the wick burns while the wax melts or evaporates away (RSC concept cartoon, 11-14; BouJaoude 1991 found 14-year-olds saying a candle shrinks because its wax evaporates). Students often do not see combustion as a reaction with oxygen (Driver et al.).
    https://edu.rsc.org/lesson-plans/simple-chemical-reactions-how-do-candles-work-11-14-years/63.article ; https://www.nsta.org/science-scope/science-scope-aprilmay-2020/combustion-and-jumping-flame

M4. Tarnish is an oxide, like rust; for silver it is a sulfide (F9).

M5. Mass kept proves a change was physical; mass is kept in chemical changes too (F5).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F26. Black powder's products, from one study cited by Wikipedia: 55.91% solids (potassium carbonate, potassium sulfate and potassium sulfide first, then sulfur, potassium nitrate and others), 42.98% gases (carbon dioxide, nitrogen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, hydrogen, methane, in descending amount) and 1.11% water; "most of it turns into particulate matter" (the smoke). So its gases are mostly carbon dioxide and nitrogen, and water vapour is a minor product (this corrects F16's "primarily nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and water vapor", which describes only the gases).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder

F27. Iodine is "a dense, bluish-black solid" that "readily sublimes upon gentle heating", "to form a distinctive violet-colored vapor". Sublimation is a change of state, as with dry ice (F17), so no new substance forms.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Iodine ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change

F28. "A balloon pops when the material that makes up its surface tears or shreds, creating a hole", and the high-pressure air escapes. Tearing and stretching are physical changes (Grokipedia's own examples include "tearing a sheet of paper" and stretching rubber): the rubber stays rubber.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balloon_popping ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change

F29. Mixtures can react. The RSC's 11-14 demonstration heats a mixture of iron and sulfur powders, from which a magnet can first pull out the iron: "the sulfur melts and reacts with the iron exothermically to form iron(II) sulfide", and the glow and a new black substance that a magnet cannot separate are the signs of reaction.
    https://edu.rsc.org/experiment/iron-and-sulfur-reaction/713.article

F30. Silver tarnish comes from sulfur gases such as hydrogen sulfide and carbonyl sulfide: "hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and carbonyl sulfide (COS) are important because they form silver sulfide on the surface of silver objects causing them to tarnish"; in a museum study "carbonyl sulfide was present in greater concentration than hydrogen sulfide at all of the locations monitored". So hydrogen sulfide is one such gas, not surely the main one.
    https://cool.culturalheritage.org/jaic/articles/jaic44-03-002_4.html

F31. Heated particles do not shrink or grow: a heated substance expands because of the "greater average separation" of its particles (the T2 particles-in-motion lesson).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_expansion

F32. Physical weathering also includes thermal stress (insolation) weathering, "the physical disintegration of rocks due to repeated cycles of expansion and contraction caused by diurnal or seasonal temperature fluctuations", and abrasion, as "strong winds contribute to physical abrasion by propelling abrasive particles like sand against exposed surfaces". Physical weathering "breaks rocks into smaller fragments" (F20).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Weathering

F33. Electricity can drive a chemical change: electrolysis "decomposes liquid water into hydrogen and oxygen gases".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Electrolysis_of_water

F34. Magnetizing iron is a physical change. Grokipedia lists among physical changes "the temporary magnetization of ferromagnetic materials, such as iron filings exposed to an external magnetic field". The Ontario Science Centre: stroking a nail "in one direction with one end (pole) of the magnet" magnetizes it, until it "will pick up one then several paperclips"; iron holds "tiny groups of atoms called domains", whose directions "are all jumbled up" before, and stroking "causes the atom domains to arrange themselves in a neat pattern of straight rows". The iron stays iron.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change ; https://www.ontariosciencecentre.ca/science-at-home/diy-science-fun/make-a-magnet

M6. A new property means a new substance (author's judgment): students take a gained property, such as a nail's magnetism, for a sign of a chemical change.
