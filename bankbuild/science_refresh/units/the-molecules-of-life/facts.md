## The four kinds of large molecule

F1. There are four major classes of biological large molecule: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids. Most are polymers, long molecules built by joining many small building blocks (monomers).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-1-synthesis-of-biological-macromolecules

## Carbohydrates

F2. Glucose is an important source of energy. Plants store extra glucose as starch, "in different plant parts, including roots and seeds" (OpenStax); starch is "synthesized from glucose produced during photosynthesis" and is "a key reserve for storing energy" (Grokipedia). Glycogen is "the animal equivalent of starch", a highly branched chain of glucose "usually stored in liver and muscle cells" (OpenStax).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-2-carbohydrates ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Starch

F3. Why the store is starch, not glucose: starch packs into "insoluble granules that minimize osmotic pressure", and its "osmotically inactive form efficiently sequesters large glucose quantities without disrupting cellular water balance or volume" (Grokipedia). Starch "is insoluble in water, which makes it an ideal storage compound"; glucose stored as such would draw water into the cells by osmosis and could make them burst; the compact structure stores much glucose in a small space (TutorChase, GCSE biology).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Starch ; https://www.tutorchase.com/answers/gcse/biology/how-do-plants-store-excess-glucose

F4. Starch must be broken back down to glucose before its energy is used: "starch's alpha-linked glucose units enable enzymatic breakdown for energy mobilization" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Starch

F5. Sugar travels through a plant's phloem mainly as sucrose, "the main transport form of carbon", from sources such as leaves to sinks such as tubers and seeds; there "unloaded sucrose is often converted to starch", trapping the carbon in polymer form. Starch is the stay-put store, not the travelling form.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Phloem

F6. Cellulose is "the primary structural component of plant cell walls" (Grokipedia); "Cellulose mostly comprises a plant's cell wall" (OpenStax). Like starch it is a chain of glucose, but its units are joined by beta links, while starch and glycogen use alpha links; the beta links give straight chains that lie side by side, held by hydrogen bonds into microfibrils of "high tensile strength". It is insoluble in water and "the most abundant organic polymer on Earth" (Wikipedia). Anselme Payen discovered it in 1838.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cellulose ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-2-carbohydrates

## Lipids

F7. Lipids are "generally insoluble in water but soluble in nonpolar organic solvents" (Grokipedia); non-polar molecules are "hydrophobic ('water fearing'), or insoluble in water" (OpenStax). Because they do not dissolve in water, lipids are carried in the blood packaged as lipoproteins (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lipid ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-3-lipids

F8. Fats are the long-term energy store: "Cells store energy for long-term use in the form of fats" (OpenStax). Triglycerides "serve as the primary form of energy storage in animals", with about 9 kilocalories per gram against about 4 for carbohydrates, more than twice as much; and "the anhydrous nature of triglycerides" allows "compact storage in adipose tissue depots without the water retention associated with glycogen" (Grokipedia). In adipose (fat) cells, triglycerides make up the bulk of the stored lipid.
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-3-lipids ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lipid ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Glycogen

F9. Glycogen is the short-term store: it is stored mainly in the liver and skeletal muscle, it "can be quickly mobilized to meet a sudden need for glucose" but is "less compact than the energy reserves of triglycerides", and it is "stored in a hydrated form, composed of three or four parts of water per part of glycogen" (Wikipedia); Grokipedia: "rapid release of glucose for energy production", it "binds 3-4 g of water per gram", about 4 calories per gram against 9 for fat, and liver glycogen can supply glucose for 12 to 24 hours.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycogen ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Glycogen

F10. A phospholipid has "a hydrophilic polar head group and two hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails"; the head holds a phosphate group (Grokipedia). In water phospholipids "spontaneously self-assemble into bilayers that form the structural basis of cell membranes" (Grokipedia, Lipid), with "hydrophilic polar headgroups facing the aqueous environments and hydrophobic acyl tails sequestered in the interior" (Grokipedia, Lipid bilayer). "The phospholipid bilayer is the major component of all cellular membranes"; cholesterol is also a membrane component in animal cells (OpenStax). Proteins make up "typically around 50% of membrane volume" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lipid_bilayer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lipid ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-3-lipids ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane

F11. What crosses the bilayer unaided: "Small, nonpolar molecules such as oxygen (O2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) diffuse freely across the membrane"; ions such as sodium and potassium face major barriers ("charged species are repelled by the nonpolar interior"); glucose's passive permeability is very low; water crosses at a modest rate, raised by channel proteins called aquaporins (Grokipedia, Cell membrane; Lipid bilayer).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_membrane ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lipid_bilayer

## Ernest Overton, 1890s

F12. Ernest Overton (1895-1899) "immersed cells in solutions of over 500 substances at equal concentrations and measured how quickly each entered"; nonpolar molecules that dissolve in lipids "entered far more readily than polar, water-soluble ones, regardless of size", and he concluded that cell membranes "must be composed primarily of [lipid] material" (Grokipedia, Lipid bilayer). Wikipedia: he "studied the permeability of a range of biological materials to around 500 chemical compounds", his work allowed "the distinction to be drawn between the cell wall of plants and their cytoplasmic membrane", and he proposed that biomembranes "are made up of lipids"; he suggested the membrane "might be made of lecithin" (a phospholipid) and cholesterol (Wikipedia, History of cell membrane theory). The finding that "substances that dissolve in lipids pass more easily into the cell than those that dissolve in water" is called Overton's Rule (Gross, University of Tennessee).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lipid_bilayer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_membrane ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Ernest_Overton ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cell_membrane_theory ; https://lgross.utk.edu/bioed/webmodules/permeability.htm

## Gorter and Grendel, 1925

F13. Evert Gorter and François Grendel (1925) extracted the lipids from the red blood cells of humans and several other mammals (rabbits, dogs, sheep, goats) with acetone, spread them as a film one molecule thick (a monolayer) on water in a Langmuir trough, and found "the total lipid monolayer area was consistently about twice the measured surface area" of the cells; they read the 2:1 ratio as a membrane "two-molecule-thick", tails facing inward and heads facing the water on both sides (Grokipedia). Red blood cells suited the test because a mature mammal's red blood cell has no nucleus and no organelles, so "the plasma membrane is the only lipid-containing structure in the cell" (Wikipedia, Cell membrane). The cells' surface area was estimated from stained blood smears (Middlebury).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lipid_bilayer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Membrane_models ; https://cr.middlebury.edu/biology/SCB2/A/A5.html

F14. Honest caveat: their acetone "removes only about 75% of RBC lipid" (chloroform-methanol removes all of it), and their smears gave "an average value of 99.4 square microns per cell" against about 138 in later measurements, so both numbers came out low (Middlebury); later analyses found "an incorrect monolayer pressure, incomplete lipid extraction and a miscalculation of cell surface area", yet "the fundamental conclusion- that the cell membrane is a lipid bilayer- was correct" (Wikipedia). Middlebury: the "extra" lipid from better extraction "just about accounts for all the 'additional' cell surface", "two compensatory errors".
    https://cr.middlebury.edu/biology/SCB2/A/A5.html ; https://cr.middlebury.edu/biology/labbook/diffusion/frap/membranes/rawpages/MembStruct4aside2aside1.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cell_membrane_theory

F15. Later models: Davson and Danielli (1935) put a lipid bilayer between two thin protein layers, a "sandwich" (protein on both faces); Singer and Nicolson (1972) proposed the fluid mosaic model, proteins embedded in a fluid lipid bilayer.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Membrane_models ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lipid_bilayer

## Proteins

F16. Proteins are chains of amino acids; "there are 20 common amino acids present in proteins". Their jobs include enzymes ("catalysts in biochemical reactions"), structure (keratin), muscle contraction (actin and myosin), transport (haemoglobin carries substances in the blood), defence (antibodies, which "protect the body from foreign pathogens") and hormones (insulin). A chain's "unique sequence" of amino acids is its primary structure, and "Protein shape is critical to its function" (OpenStax). Keratin is a structural protein of "skin, hair, nails, horns, and feathers" (Grokipedia).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-4-proteins ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Keratin

F17. Frederick Sanger worked on bovine (cattle) insulin "the 12 years from 1943 to 1955" at Cambridge. He labelled chain ends with his reagent (DNFB), broke the chains into "overlapping peptides" with acids and enzymes, separated the pieces by paper chromatography and electrophoresis, and fitted them together; the B chain's 30 amino acids were worked out in 1951, the A chain (21) by 1952, and the whole order of both chains was confirmed by 1955 (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: B 1951, A 1952). Insulin has two chains, A (21 amino acids) and B (30), joined by disulfide bonds (OpenStax). It was "the first complete sequencing of any polypeptide" and showed "that proteins possess precise, genetically determined sequences rather than variable compositions" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: "Prior to this it was widely assumed that proteins were somewhat amorphous"; Sanger "proved that proteins have a defined chemical composition" and, "by extension, that every protein had a unique sequence". Nobel Prize 1958 "for his work on the structure of proteins, especially that of insulin" (a second in 1980).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Frederick_Sanger ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Sanger ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-4-proteins

## Nucleic acids

F18. "The two main types of nucleic acids are deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA)." Nucleic acids "carry the cell's genetic blueprint and carry instructions for its functioning"; RNA "is mostly involved in protein synthesis", and messenger RNA "carries the message from DNA". Each nucleotide is a base, a five-carbon sugar and a phosphate group; "DNA contains A, T, G, and C; whereas, RNA contains A, U, G, and C" (OpenStax).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-5-nucleic-acids

F19. "In the 1860s, Friedrich Miescher" isolated "phosphate-rich chemicals from white blood cells (leukocytes)" (OpenStax), the substance now called DNA.
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/14-1-historical-basis-of-modern-understanding

F20. Phoebus Levene identified the parts of a nucleotide (base, sugar, phosphate) and showed that phosphate links join the nucleotides into a chain (Grokipedia). His tetranucleotide hypothesis held that DNA "consisted of a repeating tetramer of the four nucleotides" (Grokipedia), in "equal amounts" of A, G, C and T, and "it was widely thought that DNA was organized into repeating tetranucleotides in a way that could not carry genetic information"; Levene "declared that it could not store the genetic code because it was chemically far too simple", and "The protein component of chromosomes was thought to be the basis of heredity" (Wikipedia). OpenStax: "DNA was still considered to be too simple a molecule to carry biological information. Proteins, with their 20 different amino acids, were regarded as more likely candidates." Before 1944 "it had been widely believed that it was proteins that served the function of carrying genetic information"; Avery, MacLeod and McCarty reported in 1944 that DNA is the substance that causes bacterial transformation, and "There was considerable reluctance to accept the conclusion" (Wikipedia, Avery). Erwin Chargaff's base-ratio findings "disproved the equal-base tenet" (Grokipedia); the 1952 Hershey-Chase experiment was "decisive" (OpenStax). Sources date the hypothesis variously (1909 to the 1920s), so no date is used for it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Phoebus_Levene ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoebus_Levene ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/14-1-historical-basis-of-modern-understanding ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avery%E2%80%93MacLeod%E2%80%93McCarty_experiment

## Diffusion, osmosis and active transport

F21. Diffusion: "Diffusion occurs when small molecules and ions move freely from high concentration to low concentration", and it is passive because "it does not require energy" (Wikipedia, Cell membrane); small nonpolar molecules move down their concentration gradients without energy input (Grokipedia, Cell membrane).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_membrane

F22. Osmosis is "the net movement of solvent molecules, typically water, across a semipermeable membrane", flowing "from a region of lower solute concentration to a region of higher solute concentration" (Grokipedia); "Osmosis is the movement of a solvent across a semipermeable membrane toward a higher concentration of solute" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Osmosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmosis

F23. Jean-Antoine Nollet first documented osmosis in 1748 (Wikipedia). He used a pig's bladder: he "filled the bladder with alcohol and submerged it in water", and "water passed through the membrane into the bladder, causing it to swell" (Grokipedia). EBSCO: he enclosed a vessel of alcohol ("spirits of wine") in pig's bladder and set it in water; "while the water traversed the bladder wall, the alcohol would remain trapped within the bladder", and "In some experiments, the bladder would expand until it burst."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Osmosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmosis ; https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/chemistry/nollet-discovers-osmosis

F24. "The word 'osmosis' descends from the words 'endosmose' and 'exosmose', which were coined by French physician Rene Joachim Henri Dutrochet (1776-1847)" (Wikipedia); Grokipedia credits him with coining the term in 1826; his works include Nouvelles observations sur l'endosmose et l'exosmose (1827) and Research in Endosmosis and Exosmosis (1828), and he studied fluid movement in plants and animals. He called water moving in "endosmosis" and the reverse "exosmosis" (EBSCO). So: a French doctor named the process in the 1820s.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmosis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Osmosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Dutrochet ; https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/chemistry/nollet-discovers-osmosis

F25. Cells in salt water and pure water: "Red blood cells placed in hypertonic saline solutions undergo crenation, shrinking as water exits"; in a hypotonic solution "water influx causes swelling and potential rupture, known as hemolysis"; in isotonic solutions "no net movement occurs" (Grokipedia). A cell in a more concentrated solution "will shrivel", and in a more dilute one "the cell will swell and may even burst" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Osmosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmosis

F26. Plant cells: plasmolysis is "the shrinkage of the protoplast away from the cell wall due to loss of turgor pressure" (Grokipedia); "the cell membrane disengages with the cell wall" (Wikipedia). Water entering by osmosis presses against the wall (turgor).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Osmosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmosis

F27. A raisin swells in water: when dried raisins are placed in fresh water, "water enters the raisin through its semipermeable skin, causing expansion" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Osmosis

F28. A slug in salt: Dr Gordon Port, senior lecturer at Newcastle University, says salt works by dehydration, not a chemical reaction: "Salt essentially draws the water out of their skin", and slugs and snails "die within minutes of dehydration"; touched by salt, a slug produces much mucus, "exuding a lot of mucus" (BBC Science Focus). Mental Floss: salt "mixes with the water in the mucus that the slug secretes", that solution "has a higher salt concentration than the inside of the slug, so osmosis occurs"; "the cells that make up its skin have highly permeable membranes"; "Humans can handle salt without the same thing happening because our skin isn't as permeable as a slug's."
    https://www.sciencefocus.com/nature/why-does-salt-kill-slugs ; https://www.mentalfloss.com/science/biology/why-does-salt-kill-slugs

F29. Active transport "counters concentration gradients using energy derived from ATP hydrolysis or ion gradients" (Grokipedia, Cell membrane); it requires "the cell to expend energy" (Wikipedia, Cell membrane).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_membrane ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane

F30. The sodium-potassium pump was "Discovered in 1957 by Danish biochemist Jens Christian Skou through studies on crab nerve membranes", and it earned Skou the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1997; it carries three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in for each ATP used (Grokipedia; Wikipedia agrees). Inside a typical mammalian cell potassium is 120-150 mM against 4-5 mM outside (Grokipedia; Wikipedia 100-140 against 3.5-5), roughly 30 times as much inside. The pump takes about 20-40 percent of a resting mammalian cell's ATP, up to 70 percent in neurons (Grokipedia; Wikipedia "typically 30%"). Inhibitors such as cyanide "halted transport", linking it to the cell's respiration, and "Without continuous pump activity, these gradients would rundown due to passive leaks" (Grokipedia). "Failure of the Na+-K+ pumps can result in swelling of the cell" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium%E2%80%93potassium_pump ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium%E2%80%93potassium_pump

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- In osmosis the salt or sugar moves, not the water; or the water moves toward the side with less dissolved in it (F22). The Diffusion and Osmosis Diagnostic Test of Odom and Barrow was built from 20 such documented misconceptions, on concentration and tonicity, "life forces", the membrane and the processes themselves (https://lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.11-04-0038).
- Salt kills a slug by burning or poisoning it (F28: it dries the slug out).
- Starch builds the cell wall (F6: the wall is cellulose; starch is the store).
- Stored starch is ready to use as it is, or travels round the plant (F4, F5).
- Fat gives up its energy faster than glycogen (F9: glycogen is the quick store).
- Fats dissolve in blood (F7: they are carried as lipoproteins).
- The membrane is the cell wall, or a sieve that sorts only by size (F12).
- A protein is a loose, shapeless mixture (F17, the view Sanger overturned).
- Diffusion needs energy; active transport and diffusion confused (F21, F29).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-27)

F31. Fat cannot become glucose, apart from a small part of it: "it is widely held that fatty acids cannot be converted to glucose in humans directly" (Wikipedia, Gluconeogenesis); glycerol, "a part of the triglyceride molecule", can (Wikipedia), and in fasting humans glycerol "contributes approximately 5-20% to hepatic glucose output" (Grokipedia, Gluconeogenesis). Glycogen is itself a chain of glucose, "quickly mobilized to meet a sudden need for glucose" (F9).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluconeogenesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gluconeogenesis

F32. Nollet's set-up, corrected (fact-check): "He covered with pig bladder the mouth of a flask of ethanol, then submerged it in water" (Wikipedia, Jean-Antoine Nollet, 1748); after about five to six hours "the pig bladder became convex", and when he pricked it "liquid shot out rising more than 1 foot in height". EBSCO: he filled a vessel with alcohol and covered it with pig's bladder; water traversed the bladder wall while "the alcohol would remain trapped", and in some experiments "the bladder would expand until it burst." So the bulge came from water passing in, not from the alcohol warming, expanding, turning to vapour or soaking the wall. (F23's Grokipedia line, alcohol inside the bladder, is the outlier.)
