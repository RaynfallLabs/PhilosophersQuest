## Meiosis halves the number

F1. Meiosis is the cell division that makes the sex cells (gametes: eggs and sperm). It halves the chromosome number from two sets (diploid) to one set (haploid). Human body cells hold 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs; human sex cells hold one set of 23.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Meiosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiosis

F2. One round of DNA copying is followed by two divisions, meiosis I and meiosis II, so one cell yields four cells, each with half the chromosome number. Meiosis I parts the two partners (homologous chromosomes) of each pair; meiosis II parts the two copies (sister chromatids) of each chromosome.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Meiosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiosis

F3. Why the halving is needed: fertilisation joins two sets in one cell; without a reduction, "the number of chromosome sets will double with every future round of fertilization" (OpenStax). OpenStax calls meiosis I "a reductional division."
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/11-1-the-process-of-meiosis

F4. August Weismann, a German biologist, proposed a "Reductionstheilung" (reduction division) in 1887, arguing that the chromosome number halves in forming the sex cells to offset the doubling at fertilisation (Grokipedia). Wikipedia dates his account to 1890: "two cell divisions were necessary to transform one diploid cell into four haploid cells" if the chromosome number was to be kept. The questions say only "in the late 1800s".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/August_Weismann ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Weismann ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiosis

F5. Earlier observations: Oscar Hertwig described meiosis in sea urchin eggs in 1876; in 1883 Edouard Van Beneden described two successive divisions in the eggs of the roundworm Ascaris megalocephala that halve the chromosome number. (Van Beneden's chromosome counts are given differently by different sources, so none is used.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Meiosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiosis

## Why siblings differ: independent assortment and crossing over

F6. Independent assortment: at metaphase I the pairs line up across the middle of the cell, each pair facing either way at random and independently of the others, so each sex cell gets a random mix of the chromosomes a person got from his or her mother and father. In humans this gives 2 to the 23rd power, about 8.4 million, possible combinations in one sex cell (Grokipedia); OpenStax: "over eight million" genetically distinct gametes.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Meiosis ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/11-1-the-process-of-meiosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiosis

F7. Crossing over: in prophase I the partners of each pair lie side by side and exchange matching segments of DNA (between non-sister chromatids); the X-shaped links where this has happened are chiasmata. It makes recombinant chromosomes "with new combinations of maternal and paternal genes" (OpenStax); there is at least one crossover per chromosome pair, so sister chromatids are no longer identical afterwards.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chromosomal_crossover ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/11-1-the-process-of-meiosis

F8. Frans Alfons Janssens proposed in 1909 ("chiasmatypy", the chiasmatype theory) that chiasmata mark points where partner chromosomes break and rejoin.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chromosomal_crossover ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hunt_Morgan

## Thomas Hunt Morgan's flies (Columbia University, 1910-1911)

F9. Morgan's "Fly Room" (Room 613 of Schermerhorn Hall at Columbia) was filled with milk bottles of fruit-fly (Drosophila) cultures; his students included Alfred Sturtevant, Calvin Bridges and Hermann Muller. In 1910 he found a white-eyed male among red-eyed flies; crossed with a red-eyed female, all the offspring were red-eyed, and white eyes came back in the next generation in males only. He concluded the eye-colour gene rides on a sex chromosome, the X: the first clear case of sex-linked inheritance in animals. Morgan had at first been sceptical of Mendel's laws.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thomas_Hunt_Morgan ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hunt_Morgan ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-2-characteristics-and-traits

F10. A miniature-wing mutation, also on the X chromosome, sometimes sorted apart from the white-eye mutation; this led Morgan in 1911 to the idea of linkage (traits inherited together more often than chance predicts) and to propose crossing over, the breaking and rejoining of partner chromosomes, to explain the rare recombinant young. Morgan won the 1933 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries on the role of chromosomes in heredity.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hunt_Morgan ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thomas_Hunt_Morgan ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chromosomal_crossover

## Alfred Sturtevant's first gene map (1911, published 1913)

F11. Alfred Sturtevant was an undergraduate in Morgan's laboratory (he entered Columbia in 1908, joined the lab around 1910, took his B.A. in 1912 and Ph.D. in 1914). In 1911 he took Morgan's crossing-over data and, by his own account, spent most of a night, neglecting his homework, drawing the first chromosome map (Caltech: he "blew off his homework that night"). Caltech calls him a graduate student "at the time", meaning 1913; the questions say only "a student". The paper, "The Linear Arrangement of Six Sex-Linked Factors in Drosophila, as Shown by Their Mode of Association", appeared in the Journal of Experimental Zoology in 1913; the six genes were all on the X: yellow, white, vermilion, miniature, rudimentary and sable.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alfred_Sturtevant ; https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/first-genetic-linkage-map-38798 ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/linear-arrangement-six-sex-linked-factors-drosophila-shown-their-mode-association-1913-alfred

F12. His reasoning: the farther apart two genes lie on a chromosome, the more often crossing over separates them, so he read "higher percentages as indicative of greater physical separation" (Grokipedia). He used crosses with three genes at a time, and the distances added: "the distance between A-C would be the sum of the distances between A-B and B-C" (ASU Embryo Project); if so, B lay between them (Caltech). One percent of crossing over became one map unit, now called the centimorgan. Double crossovers make long distances less accurate (ASU).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alfred_Sturtevant ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/linear-arrangement-six-sex-linked-factors-drosophila-shown-their-mode-association-1913-alfred ; https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/first-genetic-linkage-map-38798 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Sturtevant

F13. What the map showed: genes are arranged on chromosomes "in a linear fashion, like beads on a necklace", each at a fixed place (locus) (Wikipedia); the data supported his view that the factors lie in a line, and his arrangement was later confirmed (ASU). Two of his factors (ASU's C and O) never appeared to recombine and were treated as one place, so the gaps between neighbours were not all alike; added distances fix an order along a line, not equal spacing (reasoning from F12). Genes far apart are split more often because more of the places where pieces may swap lie between them (DNA Learning Center: "Distant genes recombine frequently ... Nearby genes rarely recombine").
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Sturtevant ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/linear-arrangement-six-sex-linked-factors-drosophila-shown-their-mode-association-1913-alfred ; https://dnaftb.org/11/

F14. That crossing over is a physical swap of chromosome pieces was shown in 1931 by Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock in maize, using a chromosome 9 marked by a visible knob, and by Curt Stern in fruit flies: new combinations of traits went with visibly swapped chromosome segments.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chromosomal_crossover ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_McClintock ; https://profiles.nlm.nih.gov/spotlight/ll/feature/cornell

## A two-trait cross

F15. Mendel crossed true-breeding peas with round yellow seeds and peas with wrinkled green seeds (round and yellow are dominant); the first generation was all round and yellow. Letting those hybrids self-pollinate gave a second generation in a ratio of about 9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green, so 1/16 are recessive for both traits, and round green and wrinkled yellow are combinations that neither parent had. Mendel's counts were 315 round yellow, 108 round green, 101 wrinkled yellow and 32 wrinkled green, 556 seeds in all (UNL, arXiv lecture notes; Visionlearning misprints 108 as 101). The new combinations show that the genes for the two traits sort into sex cells independently (the law of independent assortment); the exception is genes linked on one chromosome.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dihybrid_cross ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihybrid_cross ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendelian_inheritance ; https://passel2.unl.edu/view/lesson/c36f9d4eff39/8 ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.04929 (found by search, figures 315, 108, 101, 32 of 556)

F16. The arithmetic: each trait alone comes out about 3 dominant to 1 recessive, so round and green is about 3/4 x 1/4 = 3/16 (Wikipedia: 9/16 show both dominant traits, 3/16 each single-dominant class, 1/16 both recessive). Mendel's 108 of 556 is about 19 percent, close to 3/16 (about 19 percent).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihybrid_cross ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dihybrid_cross

## Beyond simple dominance

F17. Incomplete dominance: neither allele is fully dominant, so the plant with one of each is in between. Red-flowered snapdragons (Antirrhinum majus) crossed with white-flowered ones give all pink offspring; two pinks crossed give red, pink and white in a 1:2:1 ratio. The alleles "remain discrete", so red and white come back; pink is not evidence of blending inheritance. Carl Correns first studied the same pattern in the four o'clock plant (Mirabilis jalapa) in 1903.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dominance_(genetics) ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-2-characteristics-and-traits

F18. Codominance: "both alleles of a heterozygous genotype are fully and equally expressed" (Grokipedia). A person with one A allele and one B allele (genotype IA IB) has group AB blood, "expressing both A and B antigens on the surface of red blood cells"; incomplete dominance gives an in-between form, codominance shows both fully. (OpenStax uses the MN blood groups as its codominance example.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dominance_(genetics) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/ABO_blood_group_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABO_blood_group_system

F19. Multiple alleles: the ABO gene has three alleles, IA, IB and i (O), though each person carries only two. A and B are codominant with each other and both dominant over O. Group A is IA IA or IA i; B is IB IB or IB i; AB is IA IB; O is ii. The gene is on chromosome 9.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/ABO_blood_group_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABO_blood_group_system ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-2-characteristics-and-traits (multiple alleles exist in a population while each individual has two)

F20. Markers and antibodies: group A red cells carry the A antigen and the plasma carries anti-B; group B carries B and anti-A; group AB carries both A and B antigens and neither antibody; group O carries neither antigen and both antibodies. So group O red cells suit patients of every ABO group (O-negative is kept for emergencies), and AB patients can take red cells of any ABO group.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/ABO_blood_group_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABO_blood_group_system

## Karl Landsteiner ends the transfusion lottery

F21. Before ABO typing, transfusions were done without typing and outcomes were unpredictable (Grokipedia); some led to "devastating reactions" (EBSCO). In 1900, doing serological research at the University of Vienna, Karl Landsteiner (1868-1943) saw red cells clump when mixed with the serum of "different healthy individuals, including himself and his colleagues" (Grokipedia); he took blood from his colleagues, suspended the red cells in saline and "mixed each person's serum individually with a sample from every cell suspension" (EBSCO). His 1901 paper sorted human blood into groups A, B and C; C was later renamed O (by von Dungern and Hirszfeld, 1910). Grokipedia says his results covered up to 22 people; the number is not used.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Karl_Landsteiner ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Landsteiner ; https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/discovery-human-blood-groups ; https://grokipedia.com/page/ABO_blood_group_system

F22. In 1902 Landsteiner's students Alfred von Decastello and Adriano Sturli found a fourth, rarer group: "Out of 155 individuals, 4 had no antibodies in their serums (2.5 percent)," yet "their cells were clumped by the other types of serums" (EBSCO). The group was named AB "because both A and B substances are present on red cells" (EBSCO).
    https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/discovery-human-blood-groups ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABO_blood_group_system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/ABO_blood_group_system

F23. Reuben Ottenberg, at Mount Sinai Hospital in New York, first matched blood types for a transfusion in 1907; cross-matching later became standard. Landsteiner won the 1930 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for his discovery of human blood groups"; he was then at the Rockefeller Institute.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Karl_Landsteiner ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Landsteiner ; https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/discovery-human-blood-groups ; https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1930/summary/

## How the three alleles were found

F24. Emil von Dungern and Ludwik Hirszfeld studied 348 people in 72 families and showed in 1910 that the blood groups are inherited by Mendel's rules. A year later they proposed "two independent pairs of genes", A or non-A and B or non-B; that model allowed an AB parent to have an O child, which was "ruled out by later observations" (Czerwinski et al., Vox Sanguinis 2021). Felix Bernstein, a German mathematician, proposed in 1924 three alleles, A, B and O, at one gene, a model that "holds up to this day"; Wikipedia: he "showed the inheritance pattern of multiple alleles at one locus". OMIM: "Application of the Hardy-Weinberg principle to population data by Felix Bernstein" and "analysis of family data" excluded the two-gene model. EBSCO: the mode of inheritance "remained controversial until the work of Felix Bernstein in 1924."
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9297968 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABO_blood_group_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_type ; https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/discovery-human-blood-groups ; https://www.omim.org/entry/616093 (read through the mirror https://git.lsit.ucsb.edu/publicdata/nih-gov/raw/commit/f361c7df980b9223e271a590e77e0740726da779/www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/omim/616093)

F25. What each model predicts (worked from F19 and F24): in the two-gene model, an AB parent carrying one A gene and one B gene (AaBb) can pass on neither, so with an O partner (aabb) a quarter of the children would be O. In Bernstein's model the AB parent is IA IB and passes on A or B, so with an O partner (ii) every child is A or B (IA i or IB i), never O. Both models agree that two O parents have only O children, that two A parents can have an O child (Aabb x Aabb; IA i x IA i), and that an A parent and a B parent can have an AB child (Aabb x aaBb; IA i x IB i). Honest caveat: a group O child can very rarely be born to an AB parent (the Bombay phenotype, cis-AB), found long after.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABO_blood_group_system ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9297968 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/ABO_blood_group_system

## Sex-linked traits

F26. X-linked traits: a male has one X chromosome (hemizygous), so a single recessive allele on his X shows; a female with one such allele is usually a carrier with no signs. A carrier mother and an unaffected father pass the condition to about half their sons and carrier status to about half their daughters. A father passes his X to his daughters and his Y to his sons, so he cannot pass an X-linked trait to a son. Some forms of colour blindness and haemophilia are X-linked (OpenStax).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-2-characteristics-and-traits ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Haemophilia

F27. Red-green colour blindness: the genes for the eye's red- and green-sensing cone pigments (OPN1LW and OPN1MW) lie on the X chromosome. It affects about 1 in 12 males (8 percent) and 1 in 200 females (0.5 percent; Wikipedia's epidemiology section gives 0.4) of Northern European descent. A male with one faulty allele is colour-blind; a female is colour-blind only if both of her X chromosomes carry it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Color_blindness ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_blindness

F28. John Dalton, the English chemist, gave the first scientific account of colour blindness in a paper to the Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society in 1794 ("Extraordinary facts relating to the vision of colours", published 1798). Because both he and his brother were colour-blind, he concluded the condition was hereditary. He believed the fluid of his eye had a blue tint and asked that his eyes be examined after his death; no blue colour was found. A study of his preserved eye in the 1990s (Wikipedia 1995, Grokipedia's DNA date 1994) showed deuteranopia: the gene for the green-sensitive pigment was missing.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dalton ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_blindness ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Color_blindness

F29. Haemophilia is an inherited bleeding disorder in which the blood does not clot properly, from a missing or faulty clotting factor VIII (haemophilia A) or IX (haemophilia B). It is X-linked recessive, so it mainly affects males; females are usually carriers without symptoms. An affected male passes the allele to all his daughters and to none of his sons. About 30 percent of cases arise from new mutations with no family history. Haemophilia A affects about 1 in 5,000 male births.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Haemophilia ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemophilia

F30. Queen Victoria (nine children) was a carrier of haemophilia B. Her son Prince Leopold had it and died in 1884, aged 30, after a minor fall; her sons Edward VII, Alfred and Arthur were not haemophiliacs. Her daughters Alice and Beatrice, who showed no signs, were "confirmed carriers", shown by their affected sons (Alice's son Friedrich; Beatrice's son Leopold of Battenberg). Through them it reached the royal houses of Russia (Tsarevich Alexei, Alice's grandson) and Spain. There is no record of haemophilia in the family before Leopold's birth in 1853; the mutation was most likely new (de novo), perhaps in Victoria or in her father, who was 51 at her birth (an open question). In 2009 Rogaev and colleagues showed from the Romanov remains that the disease was haemophilia B, a single-letter change in the factor IX gene.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Haemophilia_in_European_royalty ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemophilia_in_European_royalty

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- Each egg or sperm gets all of one grandparent's chromosomes, or every sex cell a person makes is the same (F6: each pair sorts by chance on its own).
- Dominant alleles or chromosomes are passed on more often (F6, F15: the sorting is random).
- New trait combinations in offspring must be new mutations (F10: crossing over regroups existing genes).
- Pink snapdragons mean the colours blended, so all their offspring stay pink (F17: red and white return, 1:2:1).
- AB blood is a blend of A and B, or a third marker of its own; or AB cells, like O cells, are clumped by no serum (F18, F20, F22: AB cells carry both markers; it is AB serum that clumps no one).
- Sex-linked means carried on the Y, or that a father passes it to his sons (F26: on the X; a father gives his sons his Y).
- A gene is dominant in one sex and recessive in the other (F26: a male has only one X, so dominance does not come into it).
- A disease that skips people "skips a generation" by rule (F26, F30: it passes silently through carrier daughters).
- The halving in meiosis gives each parent an equal share, or weeds out bad genes (F3: it keeps the number from doubling).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-27)

F31. Sturtevant's 1913 map: the paper's title names six factors, which he lettered B, C, O, P, R and M; C and O had never been seen to recombine, so he treated them as sharing one place (CO), giving the order B, CO, P, R, M: five places (ASU Embryo Project). The map held the genes y, w, v, m and r (yellow, white, vermilion, miniature, rudimentary), in the order they still appear on standard maps (Nature Scitable summary, via search excerpt); O is eosin (Caltech), and white and eosin were later shown to be alleles of one gene (Morgan 1914, "Another Case of Multiple Allelomorphs in Drosophila", as the fact-check found). So the map placed five genes. (Grokipedia's Sturtevant page says "six X-linked genes" and lists sable; the primary-based sources do not, so it is not followed.)
    https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/linear-arrangement-six-sex-linked-factors-drosophila-shown-their-mode-association-1913-alfred ; https://www.nature.com/scitable/content/16655/Sturtevant_1913.pdf (search excerpt) ; https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/first-genetic-linkage-map-38798 ; https://resolver.caltech.edu/CaltechAUTHORS:20150811-093313946

F32. Linked genes lying close together are rarely, not never, separated: "the physical proximity of loci on a chromosome reduces the likelihood of separation through crossing over"; "An RF near 0% reflects very tight linkage, with recombination events being rare" (Grokipedia, Genetic linkage). DNA Learning Center: "Nearby genes rarely recombine." So two genes close together on one chromosome give only a few new combinations.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_linkage ; https://dnaftb.org/11/

F33. Identical (monozygotic) twins come from "a single fertilized egg that divides into two separate embryos" and "share nearly 100% of their nuclear DNA, making them genetically identical" (Grokipedia, Twin). So brothers or sisters differ in their chromosomes except identical twins.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Twin

F34. Carl Correns and the four o'clock: Grokipedia (Dominance) says the pattern in Mirabilis jalapa was "first studied by Carl Correns in 1903"; Correns's own 1905 paper in the Berichte der Deutschen Botanischen Gesellschaft cites his Mirabilis report in "Bd. XX, S. 594 u. f. (Dezember 1902)". Told as "in the early 1900s".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dominance_(genetics) ; https://www.zobodat.at/pdf/Ber-Deutschen-Bot-Ges_23_0070-0085.pdf (search excerpt)

F35. Segregation: "the two alleles for each gene in a diploid organism separate from each other, such that each gamete receives only one allele"; a parent with one dominant and one recessive allele makes gametes 50 percent of each (Grokipedia, Mendelian inheritance, worked example). So meiosis passes on the dominant copy no more often than the recessive one, and does not keep "the stronger copy" (the misconception used as a wrong choice).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendelian_inheritance ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Meiosis
