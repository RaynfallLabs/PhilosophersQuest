## The heart and the two loops

F1. The human heart has four chambers: two atria above and two ventricles below. Oxygen-poor blood from the body returns through the venae cavae into the right atrium; the right ventricle pumps it through the pulmonary artery to the lungs; oxygen-rich blood comes back from the lungs through the pulmonary veins into the left atrium; the left ventricle pumps it out through the aorta to the body. An adult heart is roughly the size of a closed fist and beats 60 to 100 times a minute at rest.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Heart ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ventricle_(heart)

F2. Blood travels in two circuits: the pulmonary circulation (from the right ventricle to the lungs and back to the heart) and the systemic circulation (from the left ventricle out to the body and back). The heart works as a dual pump, one side for each loop.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Circulatory_system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Heart

F3. The left ventricle's wall is about three times as thick as the right ventricle's (Grokipedia: about 8-12 mm against 3-5 mm; the Heart page gives 1.3-1.5 cm against 0.3-0.5 cm), because it must pump blood out to the whole body against much higher pressure (about 120 mmHg at its peak, against about 25 mmHg in the right ventricle, which sends blood the short distance to the lungs).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ventricle_(heart) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Heart

F4. The right and left ventricles contract nearly at the same moment, set off together by the heart's electrical signal; neither beats faster than the other.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cardiac_cycle

## Arteries, veins and capillaries

F5. Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry it back to the heart. Arteries usually carry oxygen-rich blood and veins oxygen-poor blood, but the pulmonary artery is the exception among arteries: it carries oxygen-poor blood from the right ventricle to the lungs. The pulmonary veins are the exception among veins, bringing blood back from the lungs.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Artery ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pulmonary_artery ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Circulatory_system

F6. Arteries have thick, muscular, elastic walls to stand the high pressure of each heartbeat, and the pressure wave can be felt as a pulse at the wrist. Veins have thinner walls and carry blood at lower pressure. Many veins, especially in the arms and legs, have one-way valves that stop blood flowing backward.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Artery ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Circulatory_system

F7. Capillaries are the smallest blood vessels, about 5 to 10 micrometres across, just wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file. Their wall is a single layer of flattened cells (about 0.5 micrometres thick). They are where oxygen, food (nutrients), carbon dioxide and wastes pass between the blood and the cells around them, by diffusion across the thin wall. In the body's tissues, oxygen leaves the blood for the cells and carbon dioxide made by the cells enters the blood; in the lungs the two gases go the other way.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Capillary ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Circulatory_system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Respiratory_system

F8. Red blood cells carry oxygen bound to haemoglobin; about 98 percent of the oxygen in blood leaving the lungs is carried this way.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Circulatory_system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Respiratory_system

## The discovery: Galen, Harvey and Malpighi

F9. Galen (AD 129 - c. 216), a Greek doctor from Pergamon in the Roman Empire whose physiology shaped medicine "for over a millennium" (Grokipedia), held that the liver made venous blood continuously from digested food, that the veins carried it out to the body, and that the tissues used it up, so it had to be replaced all the time; he also held that blood seeped through invisible pores in the wall between the heart's two sides.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Galen ; https://grokipedia.com/page/William_Harvey ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Circulatory_system ; https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/history-magazine/article/history-william-harvey-medicine-heart

F10. William Harvey (1578-1657), an English physician (physician to King James I and later King Charles I), studied at Padua under Hieronymus Fabricius and published Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus (De Motu Cordis) in Frankfurt in 1628. It showed that the blood circulates in a closed loop, driven by the heart's pumping.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/William_Harvey ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Harvey ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Circulatory_system

F11. Harvey's reckoning (De Motu Cordis, chapter 9): the left ventricle holds about two ounces when full; even if only an eighth of that were thrown out at each beat, the heart makes more than a thousand beats in half an hour, so in half an hour it sends out more blood than the whole body contains, and more than the food eaten could ever supply. He concluded that the blood "could be furnished in no other way than by making a circuit and returning." (Other accounts of his sums: 2 fluid ounces a beat gives 8,640 ounces an hour.)
    https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/harvey-blood.asp ; https://lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/william-harvey ; https://web.stanford.edu/class/history13/Readings/harvey.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Harvey

F12. Harvey tied a tight band (ligature) around a person's upper arm; the veins below it swelled and showed little bumps, which he recognised as the valves of the veins described by his teacher Fabricius (De Venarum Ostiolis, 1603). Harvey found that blood in such a vein could easily be pushed up the arm, toward the heart, but would not move down it; a vein pressed empty refilled from below, from the hand's side. He concluded that the veins carry blood toward the heart and that the valves keep the flow one way. (Galen's view had veins carrying blood outward from the liver.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Harvey ; https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/harvey-blood.asp ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hieronymus_Fabricius ; https://lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/william-harvey

F13. Harvey could not see how blood passed from the arteries to the veins; he never saw capillaries. Marcello Malpighi, an Italian anatomist, saw them in 1661 with an early microscope in the lungs of a frog (De pulmonibus), a network of thin-walled vessels joining arteries to veins: the missing link that completed Harvey's circulation. Malpighi also described the lung's air sacs.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Marcello_Malpighi ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Capillary ; https://grokipedia.com/page/William_Harvey ; https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/history-magazine/article/history-william-harvey-medicine-heart

## The lungs and the air sacs

F14. Air passes from the nose and mouth through the throat (pharynx) and voice box (larynx) into the windpipe (trachea, about 11-13 cm long), which branches into the bronchi and the ever smaller bronchioles, ending in the alveoli, the tiny air sacs. A pair of adult lungs holds hundreds of millions of alveoli (Grokipedia: about 300 million; Wikipedia: about 480 million), each 200-500 micrometres across, wrapped in a dense net of capillaries. Together they give 70 to 80 square metres of surface for gas exchange, and the barrier between air and blood is only about 0.2 to 0.6 micrometres thick. The vast surface is what lets so much gas pass.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pulmonary_alveolus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulmonary_alveolus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Respiratory_system

F15. Oxygen passes from the air in the alveoli into the blood, and carbon dioxide from the blood into the alveoli to be breathed out, each moving by diffusion from where there is more of it to where there is less (alveolar oxygen about 100 mmHg against about 40 mmHg in the blood arriving; carbon dioxide about 45-46 mmHg in that blood against about 40 mmHg in the alveoli). In the blood, oxygen binds to haemoglobin in the red blood cells.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pulmonary_alveolus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Respiratory_system

F16. In emphysema the walls of the alveoli are destroyed, and neighbouring sacs merge into fewer, larger, irregular air spaces, which reduces the surface area available for gas exchange. Its main symptom is shortness of breath. Cigarette smoking causes 80-90 percent of cases.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Emphysema ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulmonary_alveolus

## The gut: the small and large intestines

F17. The digestive system runs from the mouth through the throat, the food pipe (oesophagus), the stomach, the small intestine and the large intestine; the salivary glands, liver, gallbladder and pancreas add their juices (accessory organs). The pancreas sends digestive juice into the small intestine.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Human_digestive_system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Organ_system

F18. Absorption happens mainly in the small intestine (about 3 metres long in a living person), which takes up about 90 percent of the nutrients from digested food and about 90 percent of the water taken in. Its folds, villi and microvilli enlarge its surface more than 600-fold, to about 200 square metres. "Unlike the small intestine, the colon does not play a major role in absorption of foods and nutrients" (Wikipedia).
    https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/23-5-the-small-and-large-intestines ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Human_digestive_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_intestine

F19. The large intestine (about 1.5 metres long) mainly absorbs water and salts (electrolytes) from what the small intestine leaves: about 1.5-2 litres enter it a day, and it reclaims about 90 percent of that, forming solid waste (faeces). Its bacteria make some vitamins, chiefly vitamin K and biotin. If waste moves through too quickly, not enough water is absorbed and diarrhoea can result; if it stays too long, more water is absorbed and the waste grows hard (constipation).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Large_intestine ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_intestine ; https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/23-5-the-small-and-large-intestines

## The kidneys and the urinary system

F20. The urinary system is an organ system of two kidneys, two ureters, the bladder and the urethra. The kidneys filter the blood and remove wastes such as urea; urine flows down the ureters to the bladder, a muscular sac that stores up to about 400-600 millilitres; it leaves the body through the urethra.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Urinary_system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kidney

F21. The two kidneys filter about 150-180 litres of fluid (blood plasma) out of the blood each day (the Urinary system page gives about 180), yet make only about 1-2 litres of urine: about 99 percent of the filtered water and useful substances is taken back (reabsorbed) into the blood. Each kidney holds about a million filtering units (nephrons), and the kidneys receive about 20-25 percent of the heart's output.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kidney ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Urinary_system

F22. Urea is made in the liver from the ammonia released when proteins (amino acids) are broken down; it dissolves into the blood and is removed by the kidneys in urine.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urea ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kidney ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Urinary_system

F23. When the kidneys fail, waste products build up in the blood (urea, creatinine, potassium; untreated, a fatal condition called uraemia), and fluid can build up too. Dialysis removes wastes, extra fluid and salts by running the blood through a filter (dialyzer) outside the body, across a thin membrane, usually three times a week for 3-5 hours a session. Willem Kolff, a Dutch doctor, built the first working artificial kidney in 1943. A kidney transplant is the best long-term treatment.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kidney_dialysis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kidney

## Levels of organisation, and systems working together

F24. Levels of organisation: a tissue is a group of similar cells doing a common job; an organ is a distinct structure built of two or more kinds of tissue; an organ system is two or more organs working together; organ systems make up the organism. The human body has eleven major organ systems. One organ can belong to two systems: the pancreas is listed under both the digestive and the endocrine systems.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Organ_system

F25. In hard exercise the heart beats faster (heart rate "increases linearly with exercise intensity", to supply more blood to the muscles) and the heart's output rises from about 5 litres a minute at rest to 20-25 litres a minute in ordinary people (up to 5-6 times the resting rate); breathing rises from about 10 litres of air a minute to over 100. Blood flow to the digestive organs and the kidneys is cut by up to 75 percent, so that about 80-85 percent of the heart's output goes to the muscles and skin; the blood vessels in the working muscles widen (vasodilation), set off by chemicals the working muscle releases.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Exercise_physiology ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cardiac_output ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Heart_rate

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. "Arteries carry oxygen-rich blood and veins oxygen-poor blood." The rule is direction, away from or toward the heart; the pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood (F5).
M2. "The large intestine is where the body takes in most of its water and food." The small intestine takes in about 90 percent of both; the large intestine takes back most of the water that is left (F18, F19).
M3. Galen's model: blood made in the liver from food, sent out through the veins and used up by the body (F9). Harvey's reckoning (F11) and his arm experiment (F12) overturned it.
M4. Right and left mix-ups: the right ventricle, not the left, pumps to the lungs; the right atrium, not the left ventricle, receives blood from the body (F1).
M5. Gas exchange mix-up: in the lungs oxygen enters the blood, but in the body's tissues it leaves the blood for the cells (F7, F15).
M6. "The kidneys only get rid of water." Most filtered water goes back into the blood; urine carries wastes such as urea (F21, F22).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F26. Fabricius described the vein valves; he did not discover them. Linda
Hall: "the valves had been noticed several times before"; Fabricius described
and drew them in De venarum ostiolis (1603) and thought they kept blood "from
pooling in the hands and feet"; Harvey used them as evidence "that blood in
the veins can flow only towards the heart".
    https://lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/girolamo-fabrici-dacquapendente

F27. Why the capillaries are where blood trades with the cells: their "walls
consist of a single layer of flattened endothelial cells", which allows
"efficient diffusion across the vessel barrier" (Grokipedia); arteries have
thick muscular walls and veins thinner but layered ones, with the one-way
valves (F6). Blood moves slowest in the capillaries: about 30 cm a second in
the aorta, slowing "dramatically to about 0.026 cm/sec" in the capillary beds
(OpenStax), about a thousand times slower. Arterial blood is the
oxygen-richest (F5); capillary blood is giving its oxygen up (F7).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Capillary ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/40-4-blood-flow-and-blood-pressure-regulation

F28. The heart's two sides do not mix their blood: "The right and left sides
are separated by septa to prevent mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated
blood" (Grokipedia). (Galen's pores through the wall, F9, were wrong.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Heart

F29. Where the blood goes in hard exercise: flow to the gut (splanchnic
organs) and kidneys is cut "by up to 75%" by sympathetic vasoconstriction
(Grokipedia, Exercise physiology); cerebral blood flow "increases", by about
20-50% (same page); coronary flow to the heart muscle is "capable of
increasing four- to five-fold during exertion" (Grokipedia, Coronary
circulation); the lungs receive "the entire cardiac output from the right
ventricle", and their flow rises several-fold with the higher output
(Grokipedia, Pulmonary circulation). Output rises "up to 5-6 times resting
levels" in adults (F25); a girl's peak in litres is lower than a man's (the
fact-check: young women about 17.7 L/min at peak), so the stem says "three or
four times as much" and gives no litres.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Exercise_physiology ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Coronary_circulation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pulmonary_circulation

F30. What Harvey's reckoning showed, and what it did not. Chapter IX: the
blood sent out is more "than could by any possibility be supplied by the food
consumed", more "than can either be supplied by the whole of the ingesta, or
than can be contained in the veins at the same moment", "a larger quantity in
every case than is contained in the whole body". Chapter VIII: it could not
go on "without the veins on the one hand becoming drained, and the arteries on
the other getting ruptured". So 'the vessels would burst' and 'the body could
not hold it' are Harvey's own arguments and are NOT used as wrong choices; the
wrong choices are the heart or the veins making blood from food, and the body
holding more blood than thought, none of which the sums showed.
    https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/harvey-blood.asp

F31. Neither the heart nor the veins make blood: in adults blood cells are
made in the red bone marrow ("erythropoiesis primarily occurs in the red bone
marrow"); the heart only pumps and the vessels carry.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Red_blood_cell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haematopoiesis
