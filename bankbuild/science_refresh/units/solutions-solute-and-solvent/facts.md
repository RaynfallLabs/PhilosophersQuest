## Solute, solvent and kinds of solution

F1. A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances, its particles spread so evenly that no separate particles can be seen. The solute is the substance that dissolves, usually present in the smaller amount; the solvent is the substance it dissolves in, the component present in the greater amount.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solution_(chemistry) ; https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Introductory_Chemistry/Introductory_Chemistry_(CK-12)/16:_Solutions/16.01:_Solute-Solvent_Combinations ("A solute is the material present in the smaller amount in the solution. A solvent is the material present in the larger amount")

F2. A solution need not be a liquid: air is a gas-in-gas solution, with nitrogen, the gas present in the greatest amount, counted as the solvent and the other gases as solutes; brass and steel are solid solutions; carbon dioxide in soda is a gas-in-liquid solution.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solution_(chemistry) (air "with nitrogen as the main medium"; brass and steel; CO2 in soda) ; https://kids.britannica.com/kids/article/solution/399604 ("There is more nitrogen than any other gas in air, so nitrogen is considered the solvent. All the other gases in air are the solutes.") ; LibreTexts CK-12 16.01 (air "qualifies as a solution")

F3. Dry air by volume is about 78.08 percent nitrogen, 20.95 percent oxygen, 0.934 percent argon and about 0.043 percent carbon dioxide. Water vapour comes on top of that, from near 0 percent in cold, dry air to about 4 percent in saturated tropical air near the ground, so in air water is only a minor gas.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Earth ("Water vapor, varying from near 0% to 4% depending on temperature and location")

## Water, the universal solvent

F4. Water is called the universal solvent because it dissolves more substances than any other liquid, though it cannot dissolve everything. Wherever it goes, through the air, the ground or the body, it carries dissolved chemicals, minerals and nutrients with it.
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/water-universal-solvent ("more substances than any other liquid"; "it cannot dissolve everything") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water ("the universal solvent capable of dissolving a wide range of substances"; it helps "facilitate nutrient transport")

F5. Water dissolves so much because its molecule is polar: the oxygen side carries a slight negative charge and the hydrogen side a slight positive charge. These charged ends attract ions and other polar molecules; water molecules pull the sodium and chloride ions of salt apart, breaking the bond that held them together. Polar and ionic substances dissolve readily in water; nonpolar substances (oils, benzene) dissolve poorly ("like dissolves like").
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/water-universal-solvent ("Water molecules pull the sodium and chloride ions apart") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water ("polar and ionic compounds dissolve readily"; "nonpolar substances have low solubility") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solvent ("like dissolves like")

F6. Dissolving is a physical change: the solute's particles spread through the solvent and no new substance forms (dissolved salt's ions form "no covalent bonds" and no "new compounds"); the solute can be got back by evaporating the water.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction (cited in T1 mixtures-and-solutions F23: "dissolving sugar in water is a physical change")

F7. Water is not the only solvent. Ethanol (alcohol), acetone and hexane are solvents too. Iodine dissolves poorly in plain water, so tincture of iodine, an antiseptic put on cuts and scrapes, dissolves about 2 percent iodine in a base of roughly 50 percent ethanol.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tincture_of_iodine ("the solubility of elemental iodine in water is limited"; ethanol "dissolves iodine directly") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solvent (ethanol, acetone, hexane)

F8. A water molecule (H2O) holds two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom; a hydrogen molecule (H2) holds just two hydrogen atoms, the lightest element, so it is smaller and lighter than a water molecule. Water's molecules are not the smallest there are.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen ("the lightest and most abundant chemical element") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water

## Concentration and dilution

F9. Concentration is the amount of solute in a given amount (volume or mass) of solution. Dilute and concentrated are relative words for low and high amounts of solute.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solution_(chemistry) ; TEKS 7.6 solutions standard (aqueous solutions "in terms of solute and solvent, concentration, and dilution"), https://texasgateway.org/teks/s76e/alignments

F10. Seawater holds on average about 35 grams of dissolved salts in each kilogram (3.5 percent by weight); sodium and chloride ions make up about 85 percent of them.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Seawater

F11. A tide pool cut off at low tide changes concentration: evaporation in warm, still pools can raise salinity to 50 parts per thousand or higher, while rain and incoming waves can lower it below 30 parts per thousand. Sun-exposed pools can reach 40 degrees Celsius or more.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide_pool

F12. Dilution: when solvent is added, the concentration of the solute falls while the total amount of solute stays the same (C1V1 = C2V2). Ten millilitres of a solution made up to 100 millilitres holds the same solute at a tenth of the concentration.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dilution_(equation) ("the concentration of a solute decreases upon addition of solvent, while the total amount of solute remains constant")

F13. Potassium permanganate forms dark purple to black crystals that dissolve to an intensely purple (magenta) solution; concentrated solutions look deep purple and dilute ones pale pink. It is a strong oxidiser that burns skin and eyes, a lab chemical handled with gloves and goggles.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Potassium_permanganate (deep purple above about 10^-2 M, "pale pink" below about 10^-4 M; severe skin and eye burns)

## How fast a solid dissolves

F14. Surface area: a given amount of solute dissolves faster when it is ground into small particles, because more of its surface is exposed to the solvent.
    https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Introductory_Chemistry/Introductory_Chemistry_(CK-12)/16%3A_Solutions/16.02%3A_Rate_of_Dissolving ("A given quantity of solute dissolves faster when it is ground into small particles") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility ("Smaller particles expose more surface area")

F15. Stirring (agitation): if the solution is not stirred, the water right at the solid's surface fills up with dissolved solute and dissolving slows; stirring carries that layer away and brings fresh solvent to the solid.
    LibreTexts CK-12 16.02 ("the water right at the surface of the solute becomes saturated"; stirring brings fresh solvent) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility (agitation thins "the hydrodynamic boundary layer adjacent to the solute surface")

F16. Temperature: heating a solvent gives its particles more kinetic energy, so they move faster and collide with the solute more often, and a solid dissolves faster in warmer water.
    LibreTexts CK-12 16.02 ("Heating up a solvent gives the molecules more kinetic energy"; they "collide with the solute with greater frequency") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility

F17. Stirring and crushing change only how fast a solid dissolves, never how much of it can dissolve; a change of temperature changes both the rate and the amount.
    LibreTexts CK-12 16.02 ("neither stirring nor breaking up a solute affect the overall amount of solute that dissolves"; "a temperature change not only affects the rate of dissolution, but also affects the amount") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility (solubility is the maximum amount; dissolution rate is how quickly it gets there)

F18. A saturated solution holds the most solute that can dissolve at that temperature and pressure; extra solid stays undissolved, in balance with the dissolved solute.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solution_(chemistry)

F19. For most solids, solubility in water rises with temperature. Potassium nitrate (saltpetre, a white crystalline salt) rises steeply: about 316 grams per litre at 20 C and about 2,439 grams per litre at 100 C, more than seven times as much. Copper sulfate also rises steeply (about 2,033 g/L of the pentahydrate at 100 C). Table salt barely changes (about 35.9 g per 100 g of water at 20 C, 39.2 g at 100 C).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility (KNO3 "rises steeply"; NaCl "nearly flat") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_nitrate (infobox: 133 g/L at 0 C, 316 g/L at 20 C, 2439 g/L at 100 C) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Potassium_nitrate (white crystalline solid; "increasing markedly with temperature") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Copper(II)_sulfate ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper(II)_sulfate

F20. Halite is the mineral form of sodium chloride, called rock salt. It dissolves readily in water (about 35.9 g per 100 mL at 25 C). It forms as an evaporite when seawater or salt lakes dry up, crystallising after about 90 percent of the water has evaporated.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Halite

F21. Epsom salt is magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, colourless crystals that dissolve readily in water (about 710 g/L at 20 C, more when warmer) and are used in bath soaks for sore muscles.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnesium_sulfate

F22. Copper sulfate (copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate) forms bright blue crystals; it is a toxic lab chemical. Dissolving it in hot water is dissolving, not melting: it does not melt at all but decomposes, the anhydrous salt at about 590 C.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Copper(II)_sulfate ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper(II)_sulfate (infobox: anhydrous 590 C, decomposes)

F23. Particles do not grow when heated: a heated thing expands because its particles move more and spread farther apart, not because the particles get bigger.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_expansion ("greater average separation and thus expansion") ; T2 particles-in-motion F17

## Gases dissolved in water

F24. Gases behave the opposite way to most solids: their solubility in water generally falls as the temperature rises, as heat helps gas molecules escape the liquid. Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen and less carbon dioxide.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility ("generally decreases with increasing temperature") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Seawater ("warmer waters hold less CO2") ; https://arbs.nzcer.org.nz/node/7346 (warmer water holds fewer gas particles)

F25. Fresh water at sea-level pressure can hold about 14.6 milligrams of oxygen per litre at 0 C, about 9.1 at 20 C, about 7.6 at 30 C and about 6.4 at 40 C.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Oxygen_saturation ("from 14.6 mg/L at 0 C to 7.6 mg/L at 30 C"; about 9 mg/L at 20 C) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility (9.1 mg/L at 20 C to 6.4 mg/L at 40 C)

F26. Fish and other water animals breathe oxygen dissolved in the water. Cold water can hold more dissolved oxygen than warm water, so it is usually highest in winter and early spring and lower in summer; fast, tumbling streams hold a lot. Warm, still water is hard on water life, and hot, calm weather can kill many fish. Below about 2 mg/L water is hypoxic; above 5 to 6 mg/L supports a varied community.
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/dissolved-oxygen-and-water ("Cold water can hold more dissolved oxygen than warm water") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Oxygen_saturation

F27. Fizzy (carbonated) water is water with carbon dioxide dissolved in it under pressure; the water is chilled (about 0 to 5 C) and the gas forced in at 3 to 6 bar. By Henry's law a gas's solubility is proportional to the pressure of that gas above the liquid, so more pressure keeps more gas dissolved. When the bottle is opened the pressure drops, the water holds more gas than it can now keep, and the gas comes out as bubbles.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carbonated_water ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility ("CO2 is dissolved under high pressure during bottling, and the fizz appears when the pressure drops")

F28. Water boils at 100 C at sea-level air pressure; cold water at ordinary air pressure does not boil.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Boiling_point ; T1 solids-liquids-and-gases (boiling at 100 C at sea level)

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Melting and dissolving mixed up: the Learning in Science Project found "25% of the students interviewed used the terms melt and dissolve as if they were synonyms", partly because hot water speeds dissolving as heat speeds melting.
    https://arbs.nzcer.org.nz/node/7346

M2. Warm water holds more of everything, gases included. Author's judgment: the solid rule (F19) carried over to gases, which go the other way (F24); NZCER notes warmer water holds fewer gas particles.

M3. Stirring, crushing or waiting lets more solid dissolve. Author's judgment; the CK-12 text answers it directly (F17).

M4. Dissolving makes a new substance, or the solvent reacts with what it dissolves. Author's judgment; dissolving is a physical change (F6), and the T3 physical-or-chemical-change unit sorts the two.

M5. Particles swell when heated. Brook et al (1984) quote students saying particles "begin to expand" (T2 particles-in-motion M6).

M6. Concentration confused with amount: diluting to ten times the volume leaves "a tenth as much" solute. Author's judgment (F12 says the amount stays).

M7. The bubbles in fizzy water are air that gets in when the cap comes off. Author's judgment.

M8. A heavier lump dissolves as fast as the same mass of grains, since the mass is the same. Author's judgment (F14).

## Notes for the author

- Salt's solubility barely changes with temperature (F19): use potassium nitrate or copper sulfate, never salt, for "warmer water holds more".
- Low dissolved oxygen in a pond also comes from night-time respiration and decaying algae (USGS; Grokipedia Tide_pool); questions ask only what warming does to how much oxygen water can hold.
- Fish need more oxygen when warmer as well; no choice may say so, since it would be partly true.
