## Linnaeus and the two-word name

F1. Carl Linnaeus (23 May 1707 - 10 January 1778), later Carl von Linne, was a Swedish botanist, zoologist and physician, called the founder (or father) of modern taxonomy, the science of naming and sorting living things. The first edition of his Systema Naturae was printed in Leiden, in the Netherlands, in 1735: a pamphlet of about a dozen large pages (Grokipedia gives 11 pages in one place, 12 in another; Wikipedia says twelve) that divided nature into three kingdoms.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carl_Linnaeus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Systema_Naturae ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus

F2. Before Linnaeus, a species' scientific name was a polynomial: a generic name followed by a Latin phrase of one to several words that described the species. Such names did two jobs at once, labelling the species and describing (diagnosing) it, and "as more species were found, the names necessarily became longer and unwieldy" (Wikipedia). Linnaeus's short trivial names "were much easier to remember and use than the parallel polynomial names" and replaced them; his real step was to separate the label from the description.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_nomenclature ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Binomial_nomenclature

F3. Linnaeus used one-word trivial names consistently, printed in the margin beside the descriptive phrase, in Species Plantarum (two volumes, 1 May 1753) for plants, and in the 10th edition of Systema Naturae (1758) for animals (about 4,400 species). Zoologists treat 1 January 1758 as the starting point of animal names. (Counts of the plants named in 1753 differ by source: Grokipedia gives about 5,900 in one article and over 7,700 in another; no question uses a count.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carl_Linnaeus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_nomenclature ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae

F4. Linnaeus named the western honey bee Apis mellifera in 1758. His book still gives the bee its descriptive Latin phrase, "A. pubescens, thorace subgriseo, abdomine fusco, pedibus posticis glabris utrinque margine ciliatis" (A. stands for Apis): in plain English, a downy bee with a greyish thorax, a brown abdomen and smooth hind legs fringed with hairs on both edges. Apis is the genus and mellifera the specific epithet; Apis mellifera means "honey-bearing bee".
    https://honeybee.drawwing.org/book/taxonomy ; https://tb.plazi.org/GgServer/html/28EF98F9C8E40940AC6501380B59FB87

F5. A scientific name has two parts, the genus and then the specific epithet; together they name the species (Homo sapiens, Canis lupus, Panthera leo). The genus begins with a capital letter, the epithet with a small letter "even when derived from a proper noun", and both are printed in italics. An epithet may be used in two or more genera but never twice within one genus. The Latin form gives "linguistic consistency and international recognition", while common names "vary culturally and can refer to multiple species" and are "usually different in every language".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Binomial_nomenclature ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_nomenclature

## The ranks

F6. The principal ranks, from the largest group to the smallest, are domain, kingdom, phylum (often called division in botany), class, order, family, genus and species. Linnaeus himself used kingdom, class, order, genus and species; phylum and family were added later. OpenStax: "At each sublevel, the organisms become more similar because they are more closely related", so the smaller the group two living things share, the closer their kinship.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Taxonomic_rank ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/20-1-organizing-life-on-earth

F7. The rose family, Rosaceae, holds the roses (Rosa) and also the apples (Malus), pears (Pyrus), cherries and plums (Prunus) and strawberries (Fragaria). It sits in the order Rosales, which also holds the nettle family (Urticaceae), the mulberry and fig family (Moraceae), the hemp family and the elm family. The stinging nettle, Urtica dioica, is classed in class Magnoliopsida, order Rosales, family Urticaceae. So the rose shares its family with the apple tree but only its order with the stinging nettle.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rosaceae ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rose ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Urtica_dioica ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosales

F8. The Christmas rose, Helleborus niger, is "an evergreen herbaceous perennial in the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae)": class Magnoliopsida, order Ranunculales. "Rose" is only in its common name (it flowers about Christmas time). So it shares the rose's class (Magnoliopsida) but not its order or family.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Helleborus_niger ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Urtica_dioica

F9. Mosses belong to the division (phylum) Bryophyta: small flowerless plants with no xylem or phloem, which spread by spores. Flowering plants such as the rose are vascular plants (the stinging nettle's listing gives phylum Tracheophyta), so a moss shares a flowering plant's kingdom but not its phylum.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Moss ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Urtica_dioica

## Grouping by shared features, and now by DNA

F10. In the 1735 Systema Naturae Linnaeus placed humans among the four-footed animals in an order he called Anthropomorpha ("manlike"); in the 10th edition (1758) he named the order Primates, holding Homo (humans), Simia (monkeys and apes), Lemur and Vespertilio (bats), and gave our species the name Homo sapiens. He grouped them by "shared anatomical features derived from direct observation and dissection" (Grokipedia). In a 1747 letter to the naturalist Johann Gmelin he wrote, "I seek from you and from the whole world a generic difference between man and simian", adding, "I absolutely know of none" (Wikipedia's translation).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carl_Linnaeus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Systema_Naturae ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae

F11. Linnaeus held that "species were immutable forms established by [God] at the time of creation"; his hierarchy reflected design, not descent. Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (24 November 1859) argued that the "natural system" of the taxonomists makes sense only through genealogy: living things share features because they descend from common ancestors. Linnaeus's grouping of humans with apes therefore came a century before any theory of common descent.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carl_Linnaeus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/On_the_Origin_of_Species

F12. In the first edition of Systema Naturae, whales and manatees were classed as fish (as was then thought); in the 10th edition (1758) Linnaeus moved them into the class Mammalia, which he defined as animals that suckle their young (Wikipedia). Linnaeus's classes were built from study of internal anatomy, not habitat. (Background only: Grokipedia's Mammal article tells this differently and less clearly, so no question uses it.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10th_edition_of_Systema_Naturae ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mammal

F13. Linnaeus sorted plants by his sexual system: classes by the number of stamens, orders by the pistils (24 classes). It was artificial: it split closely related species whose stamen counts differed, and Buffon faulted it for ignoring other features. Antoine-Laurent de Jussieu's Genera Plantarum (1789) instead "integrated multiple morphological characteristics" to find natural groups, the root of today's plant families. (Background; no question uses it.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carl_Linnaeus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antoine_Laurent_de_Jussieu

F14. Linnaeus placed the tomato in the genus Solanum, beside the potato, as Solanum lycopersicum in 1753. Philip Miller (1691-1771), an English botanist and gardener of Scottish descent and chief gardener of the Chelsea Physic Garden in London for nearly 50 years, removed it from Solanum into a genus of its own, Lycopersicon (the genus in 1754; the species name Lycopersicon esculentum in 1768; sources give these dates differently). "Genetic evidence shows that Linnaeus was correct to put the tomato in the genus Solanum"; the tomato and the potato "are very close relatives, forming a tight clade within Solanum" (Wikipedia), and "molecular evidence has supported its reclassification into Solanum" (Grokipedia). Tobacco, Nicotiana tabacum, is in the same nightshade family (Solanaceae) but another genus.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomato ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tomato ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solanum_sect._Lycopersicon ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Miller ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solanum ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nicotiana

F15. Classification "historically has focused on grouping organisms mainly by shared characteristics" (OpenStax); today scientists combine data from many sources, DNA among them, to work out relationships, and molecular phylogenetics reveals "evolutionary relationships beyond Linnaeus's morphological focus" (Grokipedia).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/20-1-organizing-life-on-earth ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Carl_Linnaeus

## Common names mislead

F16. The cougar, Puma concolor, is also called the puma, mountain lion, panther and catamount; it "holds the Guinness record for the animal with the greatest number of names, with over 40 in English alone" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: "the mammal with the most different names in English alone", "over 40 documented common names"). Its range runs "from the Yukon in Canada to the southern Andes in Chile"; the Florida panther is a subspecies of it (P. c. coryi). Linnaeus first described it in 1771 as Felis concolor; concolor means "uniformly colored".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cougar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cougar

F17. Four "fish" that are not all fish. Jellyfish are invertebrates of the phylum Cnidaria, "more closely related to corals and sea anemones than to true fish", and lack vertebrae, fins or gills. Starfish are invertebrates of the phylum Echinodermata, kin of sea urchins, sand dollars and sea cucumbers; marine biologists promote the name "sea star" because they are not fish. Cuttlefish are cephalopod molluscs, kin of squid and octopuses; the cuttlebone that gives them their name is "a porous, internal shell", and they do have gills and fins. Lungfish are true bony fish, lobe-finned fishes (Sarcopterygii) of fresh water, whose lungs "supplement gill respiration". True fish are vertebrates (animals with a backbone).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Jellyfish ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Starfish ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cuttlefish ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lungfish

## The dichotomous key

F18. A dichotomous key is "a series of paired statements, called couplets", each offering exactly two contrasting options; the user picks the one that fits the specimen, and each choice leads to another couplet or to a name. Keys are a standard part of floras and identification manuals and later of field guides; the first printed text key is credited to Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, Flore Francoise (1778).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Single-access_key ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antoine_Laurent_de_Jussieu

F19. Facts behind the tree key: pine needles grow in bundles (fascicles) of 2 to 5 (rarely 1 or more than 5); spruce needles are "attached singly to the twigs"; maple leaves "are arranged oppositely on the stems" and are typically palmately lobed with 3 to 5 lobes; oak leaves are "alternate", and often lobed or toothed.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pine ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spruce ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Maple ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Oak

## Kingdoms and domains

F20. Linnaeus "distinguished two kingdoms of living things", animal and vegetable, with minerals as a third kingdom later dropped. Ernst Haeckel proposed in 1866 "a third kingdom of life, the Protista", for microscopic organisms "neither animal nor plant". Robert Whittaker, an American plant ecologist, proposed five kingdoms in 1969: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia, "based mainly upon differences in nutrition" (Wikipedia): plants mostly many-celled makers of their own food (photosynthesis), fungi many-celled feeders that absorb their food (saprotrophs), animals many-celled feeders that take food in (ingestion). Many US textbooks use six kingdoms (Archaea, Bacteria, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia); there is "no consensus on a fixed number of kingdoms". Grokipedia's Fungus article draws the same line between the feeders: fungi are "heterotrophs that secrete digestive enzymes externally" and absorb what they release, unlike "phagotrophic animals that ingest food particles".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kingdom_(biology) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_(biology) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Whittaker_(ecologist) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fungus

F21. Carl Woese, an American biophysicist and microbiologist at the University of Illinois, with George E. Fox, compared the sequences of 16S ribosomal RNA (a molecule in the ribosomes of every cell) from many microbes, and in 1977 found that the methane-making microbes (methanogens) and other microbes of extreme places formed a group "genetically distinct from both bacteria and eukaryotes". They were first called archaebacteria and had been classed as bacteria; they are now the archaea. The idea "faced substantial initial resistance"; later findings on archaeal membrane lipids and cell walls helped win acceptance. In 1990 Woese, Otto Kandler and Mark Wheelis proposed three domains, Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya, a rank above the kingdoms.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carl_Woese ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Archaea ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-domain_system

F22. Archaea are single cells "lacking a membrane-bound nucleus", sharing the common shapes of bacteria (spheres and rods), which is why the first ones found "were initially classified as bacteria"; their membrane lipids differ from those of bacteria and their cell walls lack peptidoglycan. Methanogens live in wetlands, sediments and "the digestive tracts of ruminants" such as cattle, and in the human gut.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Archaea ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Methanogen

F23. Eukaryotes (domain Eukarya) are "organisms whose cells contain a membrane-bound nucleus", from single-celled protists such as amoebae, and yeasts, to the many-celled animals, plants and fungi; bacteria and archaea lack a nucleus. Plants and fungi (yeast included) have cell walls; cell walls are "notably absent in animal cells".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Eukaryote ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-domain_system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_wall

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. That a common name tells what group a living thing is in: a jellyfish, starfish or cuttlefish is a fish; a Christmas rose is a rose; a "panther" in one land is the same animal as in another (F8, F16, F17).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Starfish ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Jellyfish ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Helleborus_niger

M2. Mixing up the order of the ranks, so that sharing an order or a class seems closer kinship than sharing a family (F6). An author's note from the ranks' definition.

M3. That Linnaeus grouped humans with apes because he believed in evolution: the anachronism of reading Darwin back into him (F10, F11).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carl_Linnaeus

M4. That archaea are just an odd kind of bacteria (the view before Woese), or that they live only in hot springs and other extreme places (Grokipedia: they are "ubiquitous", in soils, oceans and the human gut) (F21, F22).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Archaea

M5. That a living thing fixed in one place must be a plant, or a fungus if it is not green; Whittaker's kingdoms turn on how a thing feeds (F20).

M6. That every lobed leaf is a maple's (F19). An author's note: oaks have lobed leaves too.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F24. Three bee names: the eastern (Asiatic) honey bee is Apis cerana (Fabricius, 1793), one of about 11 species of the genus Apis with the western honey bee, Apis mellifera; the buff-tailed bumblebee, Bombus terrestris (first described by Linnaeus in 1758), is a bumblebee of the genus Bombus, not a honey bee, though both genera are in the family Apidae.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Apis_cerana ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Bombus_terrestris

F25. "Black panther" is a common name for the dark (melanistic) colour form of the leopard (Panthera pardus) and of the jaguar (Panthera onca); black jaguars are documented in Central and South America. "There is no authenticated case of a truly melanistic cougar" (Wikipedia). So one common name, "panther", covers more than one species.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Black_panther ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_panther

F26. Leaf arrangement: "Alternate phyllotaxis involves a single leaf per node", each leaf turned about half a circle from the one before (staggered); opposite phyllotaxis has "two leaves per node positioned 180° apart" (in pairs across the twig). Ash (Fraxinus) leaves "are arranged oppositely on the stems and are pinnately compound" (split into leaflets). Beech (Fagus) leaves are "arranged alternately", with entire, wavy or toothed margins and no lobes. (Maple: opposite, simple, lobed; oak: alternate, often lobed; F19.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Phyllotaxis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fraxinus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Beech

F27. The tomato's genus after Linnaeus: Philip Miller formally named the genus Lycopersicon in 1754 (IPNI 30022251-2), but his own Lycopersicon also took in the potato: his Gardeners Dictionary, 8th edition (1768), names it Lycopersicon tuberosum (L.) Mill., basionym Solanum tuberosum L. (IPNI 816719-1). Later botanists (Dunal, 1813, and others) kept Lycopersicon for the tomatoes, apart from the potato; Lycopersicon esculentum "came into wide use" and was conserved in 1983 (Wikipedia Tomato). Chloroplast DNA work by Spooner, Anderson and Jansen (1993) "supported inclusion of Lycopersicon in Solanum", and later sequence data "unambiguously show tomatoes to be deeply nested in Solanum" (Peralta, Knapp and Spooner, Tomato Genetics Cooperative Report 56, read as a search excerpt). So for about two centuries, from the early 1800s to the 1990s, most botanists kept the tomatoes in a genus of their own. Chili peppers are the genus Capsicum, "a genus of flowering plants in the nightshade family" (Grokipedia); tobacco is Nicotiana (F14).
