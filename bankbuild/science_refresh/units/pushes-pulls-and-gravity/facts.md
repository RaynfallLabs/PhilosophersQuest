# Pushes, Pulls and Gravity: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-25. Grokipedia first; NGSS, Texas TEKS, Florida (CPALMS), NASA Space Place, TeachEngineering, Oak National Academy and the Science Learning Hub to corroborate and to fix the grade.

## Facts

F1 A force is a push or a pull on an object, coming from its interaction with another object. https://grokipedia.com/page/Force ("a force is a push or pull upon an object resulting from the object's interaction with another object"); Florida K-5 Big Idea B, https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1738 ("Energy change is understood in terms of forces--pushes or pulls"); CPP and BSCS RESPeCT Grade 3 Forces Module, https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_3rd/forces/gr3.forces_common_student_ideas.pdf ("All forces are interactions (a push or pull, a touch or tug) between two objects")

F2 A force is not energy, not motion and not a thing an object holds: energy can be stored and passed on, but a force is an interaction between two objects. https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_3rd/forces/gr3.forces_common_student_ideas.pdf ("Force isn't a thing that can transfer from one place or object to another"; "Students often confuse force with energy"; "Objects don't contain force, so they can't run out of force")

F3 A force can make an object speed up, slow down or change direction; a force has both a strength and a direction. https://grokipedia.com/page/Force ("can cause the object to accelerate, decelerate, or change direction"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ps2-1-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions (PS2.A: "Each force acts on one particular object and has both strength and a direction"; "Forces that do not sum to zero can cause changes in the object's speed or direction of motion")

F4 Balanced forces add up to zero and leave an object's motion unchanged: equal pushes on a box from both sides do not move it. https://grokipedia.com/page/Force ("Balanced forces occur when the resultant force is zero, resulting in no acceleration"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Net_force ("A net force of zero results in no change in the object's velocity"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ps2-1-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions ("balanced forces pushing on a box from both sides will not produce any motion at all")

F5 Unbalanced forces (forces that do not add up to zero) change an object's motion: an unbalanced push on one side starts a still ball moving. In a tug-of-war the rope moves only when one side's pull is greater than the other's; when both pulls are equal it stays put however hard both teams pull. https://grokipedia.com/page/Force ("unbalanced forces produce a non-zero resultant force, causing acceleration"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ps2-1-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions ("an unbalanced force on one side of a ball can make it start moving"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Net_force (acceleration's "magnitude depends on the imbalance of forces"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Tug_of_war (each team aims "to generate superior pulling force")

F6 When a force pushes on an object and it does not move, another force is pushing back just as hard, so the forces are balanced (Florida grade 5 benchmark). For a crate on a floor that force is friction, which holds a still object in place only up to a limit: push harder than the limit and it slides. https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1741 (SC.5.P.13.4: "when a force is applied to an object but it does not move, it is because another opposing force is being applied by something in the environment so that the forces are balanced"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Friction (static friction "acts on stationary objects to prevent the initiation of motion"; "the static friction threshold")

F7 Friction is a force between two surfaces that are moving, or trying to move, across each other; it always acts opposite to the motion, so it slows moving things. https://grokipedia.com/page/Friction (friction acts "always in the direction opposite to the impending or actual motion"; kinetic friction "acts on moving objects to oppose sliding"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-primary-ks2/units/forces-including-simple-machines/lessons/friction-plan ("Friction is the force between two surfaces that are moving, or trying to move, across each other"; "Friction always acts in the opposite direction to the movement of an object"); https://www.teachengineering.org/lessons/duk_friction_smary_less ("the friction a cup encounters as it slides across a flat table will eventually stop the cup")

F8 A kicked ball rolling over grass slows down and stops because the grass and the air push against its motion, not because it "runs out of force". https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_3rd/forces/gr3.forces_common_student_ideas.pdf ("The grass the ball rolls over and the air it moves through both exert forces that push the ball in the opposite direction of the kick, causing the ball to slow down and eventually stop"; "On Earth, friction is a force that resists or pushes in the opposite direction of an object's motion")

F9 Rough surfaces make more friction than smooth ones; a toy car rolls best on smooth floors and is slowed most on the textured surface of a carpet. https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-primary-ks2/units/forces-including-simple-machines/lessons/friction-plan (starter quiz: more friction on "a rough surface"; the pupils' path for this lesson is science-primary-year-5); https://www.teachengineering.org/lessons/duk_friction_smary_less ("it is the roughness of the surfaces that determines the amount of friction"; "There is usually less friction when two smooth surfaces slide past each other"); https://www.teachengineering.org/activities/view/cub_measurement_lesson01_activity2 (grades 3-5: cars "roll best on smooth surfaces rather than the textured surfaces of carpets"; "friction between the wheels and the ramp or floor slows it down")

F10 Friction turns motion energy into heat. https://grokipedia.com/page/Friction ("a dissipative force that converts mechanical energy into thermal energy"); https://www.teachengineering.org/lessons/duk_friction_smary_less ("friction generates heat")

F11 Fifth graders test a force's effect with a car on a ramp (or a balloon rocket on a string). A fair test changes only one thing (one variable) and keeps the others the same, such as the car and the floor; raising the ramp gives the car more energy at the top. https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-7 (TEKS 5.7B: "design a simple experimental investigation that tests the effect of force on an object in a system such as a car on a ramp or a balloon rocket on a string"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-primary-ks2/units/forces-including-simple-machines/lessons/friction-plan ("Only changing one variable ensures that no other factor affects the test results"); https://www.teachengineering.org/activities/view/cub_measurement_lesson01_activity2 ("Control variables are independent variables that are held constant in an experiment"; "modify ramp height to add more potential energy")

F12 Gravity is a pull between all things that have mass; Earth's gravity pulls objects toward Earth's centre, makes things fall and keeps people on the ground. https://grokipedia.com/page/Gravity ("an attractive force between any two objects that possess mass or energy"; "pulling it downward toward the planet's center"; "causing objects to fall toward Earth's surface"); https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/what-is-gravity/en/ ("Gravity is the force by which a planet or other body draws objects toward its center"; "Earth's gravity is what keeps you on the ground and what makes things fall")

F13 "Down" means toward the centre of the round Earth, so it points a different way for people in different places, and people all round the globe are held on by gravity (a 5th-grade standard). https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ps2-1-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions (5-PS2-1: "Support an argument that the gravitational force exerted by Earth on objects is directed down"; clarification: "'Down' is a local description of the direction that points toward the center of the spherical Earth"; PS2.B: "The gravitational force of Earth acting on an object near Earth's surface pulls that object toward the planet's center")

F14 Gravity pulls without touching: it acts at a distance, unlike a push from a hand. https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/294-alternative-conceptions-about-gravity ("The Earth pulls any object towards the centre of the Earth without touching it"); Florida K-5 Big Idea C, https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1738 ("Some forces act through physical contact, while others act at a distance")

F15 Gravity pulls on an object whether it is moving or not: a book resting on a shelf is pulled down as hard as one falling past it, and the shelf pushes up on the resting book just as hard, so the forces on it are balanced. https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/294-alternative-conceptions-about-gravity ("Gravity acts whether an object is moving or not"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Net_force (a book at rest on a table: "the net force is zero because supporting forces balance out"); https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_3rd/forces/gr3.forces_common_student_ideas.pdf ("A sandwich sitting on a plate exerts a downward force on the plate, while the plate exerts an equal upward force on the sandwich")

F16 A falling object speeds up as it falls: near Earth's surface it gains about 9.8 metres per second of speed each second. Air slows a fall only after a long drop (a skydiver is still speeding up after about 12 seconds), so a rock dropped from a ladder speeds up the whole way down. https://grokipedia.com/page/Free_fall ("often approximated as 9.8 m/s² (about 32 ft/s²) for introductory purposes"; a skydiver "attains about 53 m/s after roughly 12 seconds of free fall"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Gravity (standard value 9.80665 m/s²)

F17 An object's weight is the force of Earth's gravity on it, measured in newtons; heavier objects are pulled harder. https://grokipedia.com/page/Weight ("weight is the force exerted on an object by the gravitational attraction of a massive body"; "The derived SI unit for weight, treated as a force, is the newton (N)"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Force ("Near Earth's surface, the gravitational force on an object is known as its weight"); https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/what-is-gravity/en/ ("That's what gives you weight")

F18 Mass is the amount of matter in an object and stays the same everywhere; weight changes with place (an object weighs about one-sixth as much on the Moon). Volume is the space an object takes up; density is how much mass is packed into each bit of volume. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass ("a quantitative measure of the amount of matter in an object"; "Mass is distinct from weight, which is the gravitational force on an object and varies with location"; "an object with the same mass weighs roughly one-sixth as much on the Moon"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Weight (mass "remains constant regardless of the object's location in the universe"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Volume ("the measure of the three-dimensional space occupied by matter"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Density ("the mass of a substance per unit volume")

F19 A bigger force makes a bigger change in an object's motion, and the more mass an object has, the less a given force changes its motion (Florida grade 5 benchmarks). https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1739 (SC.5.P.13.2: "the greater the force applied to it, the greater the change in motion of a given object"); https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1740 (SC.5.P.13.3: "the more mass an object has, the less effect a given force will have on the object's motion")

F20 The grade: NGSS puts "gravity pulls toward Earth's centre" at grade 5 (5-PS2-1); Texas puts equal and unequal forces and the car-on-a-ramp test at grade 5 (TEKS 5.7A, 5.7B); Florida's grade 5 benchmarks name pushes, pulls, gravity on falling objects, friction-style balancing forces, and bigger force / bigger mass effects (SC.5.P.13.1-4). https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ps2-1-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions; https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-7 (5.7A: "investigate and explain how equal and unequal forces acting on an object cause patterns of motion and transfer of energy"); https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1738 (SC.5.P.13.1: "Identify familiar forces that cause objects to move, such as pushes or pulls, including gravity acting on falling objects")

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 A force is energy, a kind of motion, or a thing an object carries and can run out of. CPP and BSCS RESPeCT, Common Student Ideas about Forces and Motion: "If an object is moving, a force must be acting on it"; "An object stops because it runs out of force or energy"; "Force transfers from one object to another object"; "Students often confuse force with energy". https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_3rd/forces/gr3.forces_common_student_ideas.pdf. Science Learning Hub: "Students may consider gravity to be a 'thing' rather than a force". https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/294-alternative-conceptions-about-gravity

M2 All moving objects slow down and stop by themselves. RESPeCT: "All objects slow down and eventually stop." (same PDF)

M3 An object at rest has no forces on it, or is held still by gravity or the air. RESPeCT: "If an object is at rest, no forces are pushing or pulling it"; "They may think that air pressure, gravity, or an intervening object (like a table) keeps another object stationary." (same PDF)

M4 Gravity acts only on falling things, or stops once a thing reaches the ground. Science Learning Hub: "Gravity only acts on falling objects"; "They may think that gravity acts on an object while it is falling but then stops when it reaches the ground." https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/294-alternative-conceptions-about-gravity. The belief that a shelf or table "blocks" gravity is the author's reading of RESPeCT's "an intervening object (like a table) keeps another object stationary"; no survey quotes those words.

M5 Gravity comes from the air, from magnetism, or from Earth's spin. Science Learning Hub: "Students may also think that gravity exists due to the presence of air or the Earth's atmosphere"; Stover and Saunders (2000), Journal of Elementary Science Education: "Gravity is generated by magnetic fields" and "The weight of an object is related to the Earth's rate of rotation" (via SCIMAP, https://wordpressua.uark.edu/scimap/?p=778).

M6 People on the far side of the round Earth would fall off. Vosniadou and Brewer (1991) interviewed 60 children aged 6 to 11; when asked about walking far enough, "many replied that you would fall off" although they thought Earth a sphere. https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=5248

M7 Mixing up weight with mass (or with size). The weight/mass distinction is named in Grokipedia (F17, F18); that 5th graders confuse the two is the author's judgment, no survey found.

M8 A falling object falls at one steady speed. Author's judgment (no survey found); corrected by F16. (The older belief that heavier bodies fall faster, which Grokipedia calls "the prevailing Aristotelian notion", is not used here.) https://grokipedia.com/page/Free_fall

M9 In a fair test, keep the thing being tested (or the thing measured) the same. Author's judgment; corrected by F11.

## Notes and cautions

- Air resistance also slows a rolling ball (F8). Keep air out of the wrong choices of the friction question, or a knowing student could defend it.
- A soft carpet slows a car partly by giving under the wheels as well as by its roughness; the question asks only on which floor the car stops soonest, which is not in doubt.
- Do not use the Leaning Tower of Pisa drop as history: it is probably a story. Not asked.
- "Down" questions must not put two people exactly opposite each other and offer "toward the other person" as a choice: that direction runs through Earth's centre too.
- The Sun's gravity on the planets is asked in `the-sun-and-its-planets`; this unit asks only Earth's gravity on things at Earth's surface.
- No weight question: curriculum line G5.P1 says mass and weight are both introduced at grade 5 but "the difference is not tested at this grade", and `mass` would be the only honest lure. F17-F18 stay for the contexts.
- Left to other units: the fair test itself (`measuring-and-the-fair-test`, T1), the normal force and net force (`forces-and-net-force`, T2), the balloon rocket's action and reaction (`action-and-reaction`, T2), the ramp's stored energy (`kinetic-and-potential-energy`, T2).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-25)

F21 Air pushes back harder on a falling thing the faster it falls, so light things with a large surface for their weight soon stop speeding up: falling leaves top out at about 0.5-2 metres per second and snowflakes under 1 metre per second. A dense rock dropped from a ladder is far below its top speed when it lands, so it speeds up the whole way (F16). https://grokipedia.com/page/Terminal_velocity ("falling leaves demonstrate variable low terminal velocities of 0.5-2 m/s"; "Aggregate snowflakes typically achieve speeds less than 1 m/s"; the drag force "grows proportionally to the square of the velocity"; a thin flat plate "reaches it quickly")

M10 Grip helps a thing move, so a carpet that grips a toy car's wheels would carry it farther (the way shoes and tyres grip). Author's judgment (the judge's suggested lure; no survey found). A coasting car has nothing pushing it along the floor, so the carpet's friction can only slow it (F7, F9).
