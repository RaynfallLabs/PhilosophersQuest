## James Lind's trial on the Salisbury (1747)

F1. Lind's own account: "On the 20th of May 1747, I took twelve patients in the scurvy, on board the Salisbury at sea." All had "putrid gums, the spots and lassitude, with weakness of their knees." "They lay together in one place, being a proper apartment for the sick in the fore-hold; and had one diet common to all" (water-gruel with sugar, mutton broth, puddings, biscuit, barley, rice, sago and wine). JLL quotes him: the cases were "as similar as I could have them" (one modern transcription prints "familiar", a slip for "similar"). Grokipedia: twelve sailors, six pairs, housed together in the forehold, beginning 20 May 1747 in the English Channel.
    Lind 1753, excerpt: https://www.americanrevolutioninstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Excerpt-from-James-Lind.pdf ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/who-was-james-lind-and-what-exactly-did-he-achieve/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Lind

F2. The six pairs, in Lind's words: "a quart of cyder a-day"; "twenty-five gutts of elixir vitriol three times a-day" (dilute sulphuric acid, Wikipedia); "two spoonfuls of vinegar three times a-day"; "a course of sea-water", half a pint a day; "two oranges and one lemon" each, every day; and "an electuary" (a paste) "made of garlic, mustard seed, rad. Raphan. [radish root], balsam of Peru, and gum myrrh", with barley water soured with tamarinds and a gentle purge.
    Lind 1753 excerpt (above) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lind ; https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Lind

F3. Lind chose which pair got which remedy. The seawater went to "the worst patients, with the tendons in the hand rigid, (a symptom none of the rest had)" (Lind). Grokipedia's trial page: Lind's trial is "often regarded as the first controlled clinical experiment, despite its small scale and non-random assignment."
    Lind 1753 excerpt (above) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Randomized_controlled_trial

F4. Result, in Lind's words: "the most sudden and visible good effects were perceived from the use of the oranges and lemons; one of those who had taken them, being at the end of the six days fit for duty." The other "was the best recovered of any in his condition" and was made nurse to the rest. The fruit lasted only six days, "having consumed the quantity that could be spared." Next best was the cider: at the end of the fortnight its pair's gums, "lassitude and weakness, were somewhat abated." There was "no remarkable alteration" in the electuary, seawater and vinegar pairs; the elixir of vitriol only left the mouths cleaner. Lind: "the result of all my experiments was, that oranges and lemons were the most effectual remedies for this distemper at sea." Grokipedia: the citrus pair "recovered most rapidly"; cider gave "modest improvement".
    Lind 1753 excerpt (above) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Lind ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lind

F5. Two men a pair is a very small trial: Grokipedia's trial page notes its "small scale". (A trial this small cannot rule out luck; the other pairs share every condition but the remedy, so they rule out the berth and the diet as the cure.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Randomized_controlled_trial ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_control

## What Lind made of it

F6. Lind's own view of scurvy: he "shared medical opinion at the time that scurvy had multiple causes", among them "the consumption of salt meat in a damp atmosphere which inhibited healthful perspiration and normal excretion" (Wikipedia, Scurvy). He saw it as "a disease of faulty digestion and excretion, exacerbated by environment" (JLL); "Lind conceived of scurvy not as a disease of dietary deficiency, but of faulty digestion" (Bartholomew 2002, Postgrad Med J 78:695). Wikipedia (Lind): he "later believed it had multiple causes needing multiple remedies."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scurvy ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/who-was-james-lind-and-what-exactly-did-he-achieve/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12496338/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lind

F7. "Lind himself never actively promoted lemon juice as a single 'cure'" (Wikipedia, Scurvy); he "nowhere states clearly that citrus juice is a cure for scurvy" and kept promoting other remedies, including malt (JLL); he also recommended watercress (Wikipedia, Lind). The trial "occupied only a few paragraphs in a work that was long and complex" (Wikipedia, Scurvy). SOURCE CONFLICT: Grokipedia says the Treatise "advocated for citrus-based prevention" and gives no account of his theory; Wikipedia, JLL and Bartholomew are followed, and the questions never say he did not recommend the fruit at all (he called it the most effectual remedy, F4).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scurvy ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/who-was-james-lind-and-what-exactly-did-he-achieve/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lind ; https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Lind

F8. The "rob": to carry the juice to sea, Lind boiled lemon juice down to a concentrate. "This process destroyed the vitamin C and was therefore unsuccessful" (Wikipedia, Scurvy); the rob "has subsequently been shown to contain practically no vitamin C" (JLL). So Lind believed the boiled juice kept its power.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scurvy ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/who-was-james-lind-and-what-exactly-did-he-achieve/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lind

F9. The Treatise of the Scurvy came out in 1753 (Britannica says 1754; Grokipedia, Wikipedia, JLL and Bartholomew give 1753). The Royal Navy acted only in 1795: in June 1795 the Admiralty ordered "lemon juice and sugar" issued "as a daily ration to the crews of all warships" (Wikipedia); Grokipedia: in 1795 the Navy "mandated a daily ration of three-quarters of an ounce of lemon juice." It followed the scurvy-free voyage of HMS Suffolk and the writings of Gilbert Blane and Thomas Trotter (Wikipedia); JLL: Blane "championed Lind's work." At Haslar hospital (1758-1783) Lind wrote in 1772 that he had relieved "many hundred patients" with lemon juice (JLL).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scurvy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Lind ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/who-was-james-lind-and-what-exactly-did-he-achieve/

## Letting chance decide (randomisation)

F10. Random allocation "distributes known and unknown prognostic factors evenly across groups to minimize selection bias and confounding"; it "balances both known and unknown confounders between treatment and control groups" (Grokipedia). Allocation concealment means "individuals responsible for enrolling participants cannot foresee or predict upcoming treatment assignments." The Medical Research Council's 1948 streptomycin trial for tuberculosis, led by the statistician Austin Bradford Hill, "incorporated random allocation via sealed envelopes" (Grokipedia); its schedule of random numbers was hidden from those deciding which patients to admit (JLL, Chalmers 2011). (Matching by hand balances only the factors the doctor thinks of, and leaves the doctor choosing.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Randomized_controlled_trial ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/why-the-1948-mrc-trial-of-streptomycin-used-treatment-allocation-based-on-random-numbers/

## Placebos and sham operations

F11. A placebo is "an inactive substance or sham intervention that simulates an active treatment but contains no therapeutic agent"; the placebo effect is improvement "attributable primarily to the patient's anticipation of therapeutic benefit" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia lists "improvements due to natural healing" among the parts of the placebo response, alongside regression to the mean, and says these can be ruled out by comparing a placebo group with a no-treatment group.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Placebo ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placebo

F12. Angina is "chest pain or discomfort that occurs when the heart muscle does not receive enough oxygen-rich blood" (Grokipedia). From 1939 (Davide Fieschi) surgeons treated it by tying off both internal mammary arteries in the chest wall; early reports said about three-quarters of patients improved, and the operation became very popular (Science-Based Medicine).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Angina ; https://sciencebasedmedicine.org/orbita-another-clinical-trial-demonstrating-the-need-for-sham-controls-in-surgical-trials/

F13. Leonard Cobb and colleagues, "An Evaluation of Internal-Mammary-Artery Ligation by a Double-Blind Technic," New England Journal of Medicine 1959;260:1115-1118. 17 patients: 8 had the arteries tied, 9 had only skin cuts in the same place (SBM; Grokipedia gives 17 patients and skin incisions "mimicking the surgical exposure without actual vessel ligation"). "Both arms showed comparable symptom relief, with roughly 63% of patients in each reporting improvement"; the sham comparison led to the operation's "abandonment" (Grokipedia). SBM: "the beginning of the end" for the operation.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sham_surgery ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/?p=14793 ; https://sciencebasedmedicine.org/orbita-another-clinical-trial-demonstrating-the-need-for-sham-controls-in-surgical-trials/

F14. Henry K. Beecher (1904-1976), anaesthetist-in-chief at Massachusetts General Hospital and a Harvard professor of anaesthesia research, published "The Powerful Placebo" in 1955: 15 trials, 1,082 patients. Kienle and Kiene's abstract and other accounts give his figure as 35 percent (35.2 percent) "satisfactorily relieved by a placebo alone"; Grokipedia gives "about 30%". SOURCE CONFLICT on 30 against 35 percent: the questions say "about a third".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Henry_K._Beecher ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9449934/ ; https://www.painscience.com/blog/origin-of-the-legend-of-placebo.html

F15. Gunver S. Kienle and Helmut Kiene, "The powerful placebo effect: fact or fiction?", Journal of Clinical Epidemiology 1997;50:1311-1318, re-examined Beecher's trials and found no evidence of a placebo effect in any of them. Their list of what can give a false impression of a placebo effect: "spontaneous improvement", "fluctuation of symptoms", "regression to the mean", "additional treatment", "scaling bias", "answers of politeness", "misquotation" and others. Grokipedia: they attributed the 30-35 percent figure "largely to the absence of no-treatment control groups, which would have shown how much patients improved without any treatment."
    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9449934/ (abstract read via Europe PMC) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Henry_K._Beecher ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Placebo

F16. Asbjørn Hróbjartsson and Peter Gøtzsche, "Is the placebo powerless?", New England Journal of Medicine 2001;344:1594-1602: 130 trials that compared a placebo with no treatment, 114 analysed (8,525 patients) in about 40 conditions. They "found no significant effects on objective or binary outcomes," and "only small improvements in continuous subjective measures," pain among them (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: the only condition where a significant effect had been shown "was for pain." Morton and Torgerson (2003) summarise it as "little evidence for the placebo effect."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Placebo ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placebo ; https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/42/1/1083.pdf

## Why one success story is not evidence: getting better anyway

F17. Regression to the mean: when a group is picked for extreme values, the next measurement tends to be less extreme, with or without treatment. Galton saw it in 1886 in the heights of parents and children (Morton and Torgerson). In medicine: "patients entering trials with severe symptoms" tend to improve on re-evaluation, which "can mimic treatment efficacy" (Grokipedia). Morton and Torgerson (BMJ 2003;326:1083): "some clinicians may yield to the temptation of trying out the treatment on the patients who are most ill. This understandable desire to treat clinical outliers will usually produce a gratifying and sharp response to treatment because of regression to the mean." Wikipedia: a treatment is judged effective "only if the treatment group improves more than the untreated group."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Regression_toward_the_mean ; https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/42/1/1083.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_toward_the_mean

F18. Osteoporosis is "a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by low bone mineral density" (Grokipedia), followed with bone-density (DXA) scans. Steven Cummings and colleagues, "Monitoring osteoporosis therapy with bone densitometry: misleading changes and regression to the mean," JAMA 2000;283:1318-1321: among women on alendronate whose hip bone density fell by more than 4 percent in the first year, 83 percent gained hip bone density in the second year (mean gain 4.7 percent) on the same treatment; women who had gained 8 percent or more in the first year lost an average of 1 percent in the second. Similar results with raloxifene. Morton and Torgerson: "most patients (> 80%) who lost bone in the first year of treatment went on to gain bone in the second year despite no change in treatment."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Osteoporosis ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10714731/ (abstract read via Europe PMC) ; https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/42/1/1083.pdf

## Blinding the doctor too

F19. "Single-blind experiments involve blinding only the participants"; "Double-blind experiments extend blinding to both participants and the experimenters administering the treatment"; observer bias is when "experimenters' expectations or preconceptions unconsciously influence how they collect or interpret data"; performance bias is "differential treatment or adherence influenced by knowledge of the intervention" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Blinded_experiment

F20. Multiple sclerosis is "a chronic autoimmune disease of the central nervous system" (Grokipedia). Plasma exchange (plasmapheresis) "selectively removes and replaces a patient's plasma," the liquid part of the blood (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Multiple_sclerosis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plasmapheresis

F21. Noseworthy JH, Ebers GC and others, "The impact of blinding on the results of a randomized, placebo-controlled multiple sclerosis clinical trial," Neurology 1994;44:16-20 (reprinted 2001, 57 Suppl 5:S31-S35): the Canadian cooperative trial had three groups (intravenous cyclophosphamide and prednisone; weekly plasma exchange with oral cyclophosphamide and prednisone; sham plasma exchange with placebo medicines). "All patients were examined by both a blinded and an unblinded neurologist at each assessment." By the blinded neurologists, neither active treatment beat placebo; the unblinded neurologists' scores showed "an apparent treatment benefit" for the plasma-exchange group at 6, 12 and 24 months. Conclusion: "Physician blinding prevented an erroneous conclusion about treatment efficacy."
    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8290055/ (abstract read via Europe PMC) ; https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/the-impact-of-blinding-on-the-results-of-a-randomized-placebo-con

## The polio field trial (1954)

F22. Thomas Francis Jr. of the University of Michigan directed the 1954 field trial of Jonas Salk's killed-virus polio vaccine, about 1.8 million children in all (Grokipedia; Wikipedia, Thomas Francis Jr.). Grokipedia calls it "the largest clinical trial in medical history"; Meldrum (BMJ 1998) "among the largest and most publicised clinical trials ever undertaken." Results were announced on 12 April 1955, and the vaccine was licensed that same day (Grokipedia), so no child could have had it outside the trial in 1954.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Polio_vaccine ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Francis_Jr. ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1114166 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Announcement_of_polio_vaccine_success

F23. The two designs (Francis 1955). Observed-control areas: "vaccinate all children in the second grade of school whose parents requested it", while "all the children of the first and third grades were to be kept under observation as controls" (1,080,680 children; 221,998 vaccinated; 725,173 first- and third-grade controls). Placebo-control areas: "all children of the first, second, and third grades whose parents requested their participation, with a clear understanding that half would receive vaccine and the other half a placebo"; "knowledge of the inoculum was available only by a code held at the evaluation center" (749,236 children; 200,745 vaccinated; 201,229 placebo; 338,778 not inoculated). Meldrum: 84 placebo-control areas in 11 states, 127 observed-control areas in 33 states (her text also gives 34). Meier: assignment gave "each individual ... an equal chance of getting vaccine or placebo", and "Each vial of injection fluid was identified only by a code number."
    https://unl-statistics.github.io/stat349/instructions/methods-papers/1954FieldTrial.html (Francis 1955, read at the AMA meeting, 7 June 1955; summary report Am J Public Health 45, May 1955, pt 2) ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1114166 ; https://webpages.math.luc.edu/~mgb/courses/s203/MeierPolio.htm

F24. Results for paralytic polio (Francis 1955, Table 3). Placebo areas: vaccinated 33 cases (16 per 100,000); placebo 115 (57 per 100,000); not inoculated 121 (36 per 100,000). Observed areas: vaccinated 38 (17); first- and third-grade controls 330 (46); second-graders not inoculated 43 (35). For laboratory-confirmed paralytic cases "an effectiveness of 80 to 90% was observed in the placebo study areas", 60 to 80 percent in the observed areas. By virus type: about 60-70 percent against type 1, over 90 percent against types 2 and 3 (Grokipedia).
    https://unl-statistics.github.io/stat349/instructions/methods-papers/1954FieldTrial.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Polio_vaccine

F25. Why the size: paralytic polio was "a disease of relatively low incidence" (Meldrum). Paul Meier worked it through: at about 50 cases per 100,000, groups of 40,000 vaccinated and 40,000 unvaccinated would give about 20 control cases and 10 vaccinated cases even if the vaccine halved the risk, a difference that "could fairly easily be attributed to random variation." Francis: "larger numbers would have created greater security." (So it is the number of CASES, not of children, that decides what a trial can show.)
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1114166 ; https://webpages.math.luc.edu/~mgb/courses/s203/MeierPolio.htm ; https://unl-statistics.github.io/stat349/instructions/methods-papers/1954FieldTrial.html

F26. Why the doctors who diagnosed were kept blind: Francis wrote that the placebo plan was adopted "to achieve strict impartiality in the study of patients in test and control groups"; in observed areas there was "the ready opportunity for the introduction of bias at any stage in the diagnosis and investigation of cases", since "It can readily be determined whether a child was vaccinated or not" and "that knowledge could influence subsequent consideration." Meier: many cases were hard to call, and "Because no one knew, no one could be influenced to diagnose differently for vaccinated cases and for controls."
    https://unl-statistics.github.io/stat349/instructions/methods-papers/1954FieldTrial.html ; https://webpages.math.luc.edu/~mgb/courses/s203/MeierPolio.htm

F27. Volunteers differ. Francis: in observed areas "The vaccinated persons are those who specifically volunteer", while "the controls comprise both those who might volunteer and those who would not", and "the nonrequesting population might well be a more resistant group than the inoculated"; "the uninoculated, or refused, portion of the second grade in observed areas also had a lower rate" (35 against 46, where no one had a dummy shot). Meier: volunteers "tend to be better educated and, generally, more well-to-do"; polio "seemed to hit hardest those who were better off hygienically"; he reads the placebo-against-not-inoculated gap as the volunteer effect, "clearly evident". Koller (1957) likewise named social differences from "the selection of families willing to be vaccinated".
    https://unl-statistics.github.io/stat349/instructions/methods-papers/1954FieldTrial.html ; https://webpages.math.luc.edu/~mgb/courses/s203/MeierPolio.htm ; https://www.thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/s-0028-1114686?lang=en

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- A placebo group measures "mind over matter" only (F11, F15, F17: it also holds natural recovery and regression to the mean).
- If a sham and a real operation both help, both work (F13: the real step added nothing).
- A patient who improves after a treatment was helped by it (F17, F18).
- Blinding is only about the patient (F19, F21, F26).
- A small trial shows the same result as a big one, only more cheaply (F25).
- No gap in a small trial means the treatment failed (F25: too few cases to tell).
- Lind proved citrus cured scurvy, knew it was a lack in the diet, and the Navy acted at once (F6-F9).
- Lind's boiled "rob" kept the juice's power (F8).
