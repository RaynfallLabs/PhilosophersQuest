## Facts

F1 Natural resources are materials and energy that occur in nature and that people use: water, air, soil, minerals, forests, fossil fuels, sunlight and wind. https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_resource ("Natural resources are naturally occurring materials and substances in the environment"; lists minerals, forests, water, soils and fossil fuels; abiotic resources include "atmospheric air, and solar radiation")

F2 A renewable resource is one that nature replaces within a time comparable to a human life: sunlight, wind, flowing water and timber are examples, new trees grow back in about 20 to 100 years, and fresh water is replenished by the water cycle. https://grokipedia.com/page/Renewable_resource ("can be replenished through natural processes within a timeframe comparable to human use"; "typically years to decades for biological materials or continuously for energy flows"; "freshwater from hydrological cycles"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_resource ("sunlight, wind, flowing water, and sustainably harvested timber"; table: "tree regrowth: 20-100 years")

F3 A nonrenewable resource is one that nature does not replace within a human lifetime: coal, oil, natural gas, uranium, and metal and mineral ores. They take millions of years or more to form. https://grokipedia.com/page/Non-renewable_resource ("cannot be replenished by geological or biological processes within a timeframe relevant to human consumption"; "over timescales ranging from millions to billions of years"; "fossil fuels, metallic and non-metallic minerals, and nuclear fuels like uranium"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/nonrenewable-resources/ (glossary: "exhaustible relative to the human life span, such as gas, coal, or petroleum"; fossil fuels formed "over millions of years")

F4 Coal, oil and natural gas are fossil fuels and are nonrenewable; oil formed over millions of years. https://www.eia.gov/kids/energy-sources/oil/ (oil listed under "Nonrenewable"; "Over millions of years"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Non-renewable_resource (coal's formation spans "tens to hundreds of millions of years")

F5 Wood is a renewable energy resource (biomass), along with wind, sunlight (solar), flowing water (hydropower) and Earth's heat (geothermal). https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/renewable-sources/ (renewable sources: "Biomass", including "Wood and wood waste"; "Hydropower"; "Geothermal"; "Wind"; "Solar")

F6 Sunlight and wind are not used up by being used: they are "virtually inexhaustible", though only so much arrives at a time. https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/renewable-sources/ ("naturally replenishing but flow-limited"; "renewable resources are virtually inexhaustible"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Renewable_resource (energy flows replenished "continuously")

F7 A renewable resource can still run out if people take it faster than nature replaces it: forests can be degraded and fish populations have collapsed from overfishing. https://grokipedia.com/page/Renewable_resource (renewables "are vulnerable to overexploitation when human demands surpass replenishment"; "collapsed fish populations"; "exceeding this yield leads to resource depletion")

F8 Metals and minerals are nonrenewable even though many can be recycled: recycling makes the metal already mined last longer, but nature makes no new ore in a human lifetime. https://grokipedia.com/page/Non-renewable_resource ("Mineral and metal ores represent non-renewable resources", owing to "the irreplaceable geological timescales required for their formation"; recycling extends the "effective lifespan of mineral ores" and conserves "primary ore needs")

F9 Copper is mined from ores in rock (chiefly the mineral chalcopyrite; most ores hold only about 0.5 to 3 percent copper) and is the main metal in electrical wiring. Recycled copper keeps its chemical and physical properties, so it can be recycled again and again. https://grokipedia.com/page/Copper ("The most important sulfide mineral is chalcopyrite"; "copper dominates wiring, cables"); https://superfund.arizona.edu/resources/modules/copper-mining-and-processing/background (copper "can be perpetually recycled, without losing its properties"); https://copper.org/education/copper-is/elements/recyclable.html

F10 Copper does not rust. Rust is iron oxide, which forms on iron and steel. Copper slowly grows a thin green coat (patina) over 5 to 20 years or more, and the coat protects the metal beneath, so copper roofs last over a century. https://grokipedia.com/page/Rust (rust "forms on iron and steel surfaces"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Patina ("a protective green layer known as verdigris"; "prevents deeper corrosion"; roofs "lasting over a century")

F11 Topsoil forms very slowly: about 2.5 centimetres (one inch) takes roughly 500 to 1,000 years under natural conditions; field measurements give a few hundred to several hundred years per inch. Erosion by water and wind often removes topsoil faster than it forms. https://grokipedia.com/page/Topsoil ("2.5 cm (approximately 1 inch) of soil may require 500 to 1,000 years to accumulate"; erosion removes topsoil "at rates often exceeding formation"); https://www.nrdc.org/stories/soil-erosion-101 (an inch of topsoil can take several hundred years to develop); https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/a-case-for-no-till-farmin/ (300 to 850 years per inch at the sites studied)

F12 Topsoil is made from rock broken into small grains, mixed with decayed plant and animal matter. https://grokipedia.com/page/Topsoil ("Physical and chemical weathering of bedrock or unconsolidated sediments supplies the mineral components of topsoil"; it "accumulates through the decomposition of plant residues and animal matter"; "plant litter, root exudates, and animal residues that decompose into humus"); humus itself is taught in the decomposers-and-recycling-matter unit.

F13 Plastic is made by people, not found in nature, and over 90 percent of it is made from fossil fuels (petrochemicals); under 1 percent is plant-based bioplastic. In the United States the raw materials are natural gas and the products of crude-oil refining. https://grokipedia.com/page/Plastic ("over 90% of plastics originate from fossil-based petrochemicals"; bioplastics "less than 1%"); https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=34&t=6 ("Plastics are produced from natural gas, feedstocks derived from natural gas processing, and feedstocks derived from crude oil refining"); https://www.eia.gov/kids/energy-sources/oil/ (crude oil is used to "Make products such as medicines and plastics")

F14 Paper is made from cellulose fibres from wood pulp. Natural rubber is made from latex, the milky sap of the rubber tree. Most glass (soda-lime glass, nearly 90 percent of all glass) is made from silica sand, soda ash and limestone. (Used for the wrong choices of the plastic question.) https://grokipedia.com/page/Paper_recycling ("primarily cellulose derived from wood pulp"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_rubber ("a milky white latex emulsion from the bark of the Pará rubber tree"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Soda–lime_glass ("silica sand, soda ash (sodium carbonate), and limestone (calcium carbonate)"; "nearly 90% of all glass manufactured worldwide")

F15 Paper can be recycled only about five to seven times: each time its fibres get shorter and weaker, until new fibre must be mixed in. https://grokipedia.com/page/Paper_recycling ("limiting reuse to 5-7 cycles before blending with primary fibers is required"; fibres shorten and lose strength with each pass)

F16 Glass, aluminium and steel can be melted and remade again and again without wearing out. https://grokipedia.com/page/Recycling (glass recycling is "a process repeatable indefinitely without degradation"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Aluminium (recyclability "infinite", "without quality loss"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Steel_and_tin_cans (steel "supports infinite recyclability without downcycling")

F17 Aluminium is made from an ore called bauxite. Making aluminium from recycled cans takes only about 5 percent of the energy needed to make it from ore, a saving of about 95 percent; each tonne of recycled aluminium also saves about four tonnes of bauxite. https://grokipedia.com/page/Aluminium ("The primary mineral source of aluminium is bauxite"; "95% energy savings"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Aluminium_recycling ("Recycling demands merely 5% of the energy associated with virgin production"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Recycling ("aluminum recycling consumes 95% less energy than smelting bauxite ore"; one tonne from scrap "avoids the need for approximately four metric tons of bauxite ore")

F18 Reusing means using a thing again, as it is or for a new job, without breaking it down; recycling means collecting used materials and processing them into new products. Composting food scraps and yard waste is counted as recycling them. https://grokipedia.com/page/Reuse (reuse is "employing an item, material, or product again", in its original form or adapted for a new function; recycling subjects materials "to industrial processes to extract raw components for new production"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Recycling (materials "remanufactur[ed]" into new products); https://grokipedia.com/page/Compost (food waste "recycled ... via composting")

F19 The waste hierarchy ranks reducing (prevention) first, then reuse, then recycling, then energy recovery, then disposal; reducing comes first because it avoids making the waste at all. Recycling itself takes energy to collect, sort and process. https://grokipedia.com/page/Waste_hierarchy ("prevention at the apex, followed by preparing for reuse"; it "eliminates waste generation at the source"; recycling's "collection, sorting, and processing"); https://archive.epa.gov/wastes/homeland/web/html/hierarchy.html (EPA tiers, source reduction most preferred)

F20 Conservation means using less of a resource and wasting less of it. Fixing leaks, turning off the tap while brushing teeth, showering instead of bathing, and sweeping steps instead of hosing them all conserve water; switching off unneeded lights conserves electricity. https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_conservation ("the deliberate reduction of water consumption and waste"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Energy_conservation ("the deliberate reduction of energy consumption", including "behavioral adjustments"); https://www.epa.gov/watersense/start-saving ("Turn off the tap while shaving or brushing teeth"; "Identify and Fix Leaks"; "Showers use less water than baths"; "Sweep driveways, sidewalks, and steps rather than hosing off"); https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/save-electricity-and-fuel/lighting-choices-save-you-money/when-turn-your-lights (turn lights off when they are not needed)

F21 Most plastic does not rot: bacteria and fungi break down almost none of it. Sunlight and wear make it brittle and break it into smaller and smaller pieces; pieces under 5 millimetres are called microplastics. Plastic lasts from decades to centuries, and the pieces have been found in oceans, sea-floor mud, soil and air. https://grokipedia.com/page/Plastic (decomposition is abiotic "rather than biological breakdown"; weathering and UV "do not eliminate the polymer but convert macroplastics into smaller fragments"; "100 to 1,000 years or more"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Microplastics ("less than 5 millimeters"; microbial degradation "negligible under ambient conditions"; found in "ocean sediments, soils, remote air samples"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Plastic_pollution ("fragmentation into microplastics rather than full breakdown"; "decades to centuries")

F22 Plastic litter harms animals: over 700 kinds of animals, among them seabirds, fish, sea turtles and sea mammals, have been found to swallow plastic, mistaking it for food, and many are tangled in old nets and packaging. https://grokipedia.com/page/Plastic_pollution ("Ingestion occurs when animals mistake plastics for food"; "Over 700 species"; entanglement causes "restricting movement, causing injuries, drowning")

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Anything that can be recycled is renewable. Project Learning Tree notes students often do not know which resources are renewable and which are recyclable or reusable, and the Greenville "Renewable and Nonrenewable Resources" lesson stresses that many nonrenewable resources are recyclable (plastic from oil). https://www.plt.org/activity-resources/prek-8-activity-14-renewable-or-not/ ; https://de.slideshare.net/durgarai526/111renewableandnonrenewableresources . Corrected by F8, F9.

M2 A fuel that is burned (firewood) must be nonrenewable, because it is used up. Author's judgment (no survey found); wood is renewable biomass (F5).

M3 A natural resource does no harm, and renewable means clean. McGraw-Hill's Page Keeley probe: "if it is a natural resource, some students believe it does not harm the environment." https://www.mheducation.com/unitas/school/explore/6-12-science/Page-Keeley-Probe.pdf . Not used as a key claim.

M4 Plastic rots away like a leaf or an apple core in time. Author's judgment (no survey found); corrected by F21.

M5 Metals are made in factories, not found in nature. Author's judgment (no survey found); corrected by F9.

M6 Recycled material is always as good as new (paper is), or always wears out (copper does). Author's judgment; F15 and F16 sort the two.

M7 Renewing, reusing, recycling and conserving are the same thing. Author's judgment; the three Rs and conservation are taught as different actions (F18, F19, F20).

## Notes and cautions

- Do not say a renewable resource "can never run out": F7 says trees and fish can, if overused. Sunlight and wind are the ones not used up by use (F6).
- Do not call soil flatly "nonrenewable": Grokipedia calls topsoil a "finite resource" and soil fertility renewable when managed. Say only that new topsoil forms over hundreds of years (F11), far longer than a human life.
- Topsoil rates vary widely with climate (F11): say "hundreds of years" for about 2.5 cm, not one exact number.
- The plastic key is "oil and natural gas" together, never "oil" alone: in the United States natural gas is a main raw material (F13).
- Microplastics' harm to health is still being studied (Grokipedia Microplastics: outside the lab "substantive adverse effects ... remain largely speculative"). Say only that animals swallow plastic and get tangled in it (F22), not that microplastics poison them.
- Neighbours: energy-changes-form owns the Sun as the source of energy, coal's energy from ancient plants, and solar panels; rock-layers-and-fossils owns how coal and oil formed from buried life; the water-cycle unit owns fresh water's share and water used over and over; ecosystems-living-and-nonliving owns soil washing off a cleared hillside and fertilizer in a lake; telling-materials-apart and mixtures-and-solutions own magnets pulling iron and steel. T2 caring-for-air-water-and-soil owns air and water pollution's sources, erosion control, and efficiency (the LED bulb). None of their facts is keyed here.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F23 Fuel ethanol is made from biomass, and in the United States nearly all of it is made from corn; biofuels are one of the renewable energy sources the EIA lists, under biomass, since a new crop grows each year. https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/biofuels/ethanol.php ("Ethanol is made from biomass"; "nearly all fuel ethanol is produced from corn kernel starch"); https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/renewable-sources/ (renewable sources: Biomass, including "Wood and wood waste" and "Biofuels"; "Hydropower"; "Geothermal"; "Wind"; "Solar")

F24 Hydropower is energy in moving water, and the EIA counts it among renewable sources. Water flows through a dam and turns the blades of a turbine; the river is kept flowing by rain and snow, which the water cycle brings again and again. https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/hydropower/ ("Hydropower is energy in moving water"; water "pushes against and turns blades in a turbine"; "Water vapor condenses into clouds and falls as precipitation—rain and snow"; hydropower counted in "renewable electricity generation")

F25 Copper is widely used for the water pipes in buildings. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_tubing ("it is used for water distribution systems"; "Solder-connected rigid copper is the most popular choice for water supply lines in modern buildings"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Copper ("Plumbing, roofing, and heat exchangers leverage its corrosion resistance")

F26 Aluminium foil is thin (household foil about 15-18 microns) and can be recycled again and again without loss of quality, like other aluminium (F16). https://grokipedia.com/page/Aluminium_foil (foil is "fully recyclable without loss of quality"; "infinitely recyclable without loss of quality"; "Standard/regular household aluminium foil typically has a thickness of 15–18 microns")

F27 Plastic bags left in the open air break into pieces: in Imogen Napper and Richard Thompson's University of Plymouth study (2019), every bag material left outdoors had fallen into fragments within nine months, while some buried in soil or sunk in the sea still worked as carrier bags after three years. https://plymouth.ac.uk/news/biodegradable-bags-can-hold-a-full-load-of-shopping-three-years-after-being-discarded-in-the-environment ("After nine months in the open air, all the materials had completely disintegrated into fragments"; bags "remained functional as carrier bags after being in the soil or the marine environment for over three years")

F28 Paper's fibres shorten and weaken a little with each recycling (F15). That paper can be recycled only five to seven times is the long-standing, often-repeated figure, not a settled count: a 2021 test at Graz University of Technology, led by Rene Eckhart for the carton industry, ran cartonboard through 25 recycling loops and found no loss in the strength it measured. https://grokipedia.com/page/Paper_recycling (5-7 cycles; fibres shorten and lose strength with each pass); https://www.procarton.com/its-official-carton-fibres-can-be-recycled-at-least-25-times/ (fibre packaging "can be recycled more than 25 times with little to no loss of integrity"; calls the four-to-seven figure "the common myth")

F29 Loose sediment takes thousands to millions of years to harden into rock, far longer than topsoil takes to form (F11). https://grokipedia.com/page/Lithification (lithification "typically unfolds over thousands to millions of years following deposition")
