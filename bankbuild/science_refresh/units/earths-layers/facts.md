## The four layers, in order

F1. Earth has four main layers, one inside another. From the outside in they are the crust, the mantle, the outer core and the inner core.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Internal_structure_of_Earth ("four primary concentric layers: the crust, mantle, outer core, and inner core") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_structure_of_Earth

F2. Earth's mean radius is about 6,371 km, so its centre lies about 6,371 km below the surface.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Internal_structure_of_Earth ("The mean radius ... is 6,371 km")

## The crust

F3. The crust is the outermost solid layer and the thinnest of Earth's major layers; it is less than 1 percent of Earth's volume (Wikipedia: 0.6 percent).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Crust_(geology) ("the outermost solid shell of the planet"; "the thinnest of Earth's major layers"; "less than 1% of Earth's total volume") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_structure_of_Earth ("0.6% of the volume is the crust")

F4. Oceanic crust, under the ocean basins, is typically 5 to 10 km thick (about 6-7 km on average). Continental crust is about 30 to 50 km thick, up to about 70 km in some regions, and thickest (over 70 km) under major mountain belts. So the crust is several times thicker under the continents than under the oceans.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Crust_(geology) ("5 to 10 kilometers under the oceans"; "30 to 50 kilometers (and up to 70 kilometers in some continental regions)"; "maximum thicknesses exceeding 70 km beneath major mountain belts") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Internal_structure_of_Earth ("Oceanic crust is typically 5 to 10 kilometers ... thick"; "continental crust averages 30 to 70 kilometers") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_structure_of_Earth (oceanic crust "underlies the ocean basins (5-10 km)")

F5. Oceanic crust is mostly basalt, about 3.0 g/cm3; continental crust is mostly granite-like rock, about 2.7 g/cm3. (Context only: the rock types are `the-rock-cycle`'s.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Crust_(geology) ("primarily basaltic"; "predominantly granitic")

F6. The continental shelf, under the shallow sea near a coast (commonly 100-200 m deep at its edge), is the drowned edge of a continent and is underlain by continental crust, not oceanic crust.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Continental_shelf ("the gently sloping, submerged prolongation of a continent's continental crust"; "This region is underlain primarily by continental crust")

## The deepest holes

F7. The Kola Superdeep Borehole, on the Kola Peninsula in the far north-west of Russia, is the deepest artificial hole ever drilled into Earth. Drilling began on 24 May 1970 and reached a true vertical depth of 12,262 m in 1989; drilling stopped in the early 1990s (Grokipedia: suspended 1992, rising temperatures and funding; Wikipedia: ended 1995, lack of funding).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kola_Superdeep_Borehole ("Drilling commenced on May 24, 1970"; "the deepest artificial hole ever drilled into the Earth"; "reaching its record depth of 12,262 meters in 1989") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kola_Superdeep_Borehole ("the deepest human-made hole on Earth since 1979")

F8. The Kola hole went only about a third of the way through the continental crust there, which is about 35 km thick, and about 0.2 percent of the way to Earth's centre. No hole has reached the mantle. Rock at the bottom was soft and plastic, and the temperature at about 12 km was about 180 degrees Celsius, far hotter than predicted.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kola_Superdeep_Borehole ("approximately one-third of the continental crust thickness in the Pechenga region"; "just 0.2% of the distance to the planet's core"; "Temperatures soared to 180°C (356°F) at 12 kilometers") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kola_Superdeep_Borehole (Baltic Shield crust "estimated to be around 35 kilometres (22 mi) deep"; 180 °C in 1992)

F9. A well can be longer than Kola without being deeper: Qatar's Al Shaheen BD-04A well is 12,289 m long but only about 2,800 m deep vertically. (Note for wording: say "deepest", not "longest".)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kola_Superdeep_Borehole

F10. The world's deepest mine, the Mponeng gold mine in South Africa, reaches about 4 km below the surface. Its rock is about 60 degrees Celsius or more (Wikipedia: up to 66 °C), and slurry ice is pumped underground to cool the tunnel air.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mponeng_Gold_Mine ("approximately 4 kilometers"; "the world's deepest underground mine"; rock temperatures "exceeding 60°C"; "slurry ice systems") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mponeng_Gold_Mine ("over 4 kilometres (2.5 mi) below the surface"; "66 °C (151 °F)"; "slurry ice underground to cool the tunnel air")

## The mantle

F11. The mantle reaches from the base of the crust to about 2,900 km deep. It is the thickest layer of the planet and makes up about 84 percent of Earth's volume (and about two-thirds of its mass).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_mantle ("roughly 2,900 kilometers (1,800 miles) in depth"; "the thickest layer of the planet"; "approximately 84% of Earth's total volume"; about 67% of mass) ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/mantle/ ("about 2,900 kilometers (1,802 miles) thick"; "84 percent of Earth's total volume") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_structure_of_Earth (to "a depth of 2,890 km", the thickest layer)

F12. The mantle is made of hot, dense silicate rock rich in iron and magnesium. It is mostly solid, yet it flows very slowly (creeps) over long ages, on the order of 1 to 10 cm a year.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_mantle ("dense silicate rocks rich in iron and magnesium"; "mostly solid") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mantle_convection ("the slow, creeping motion of the Earth's solid silicate mantle"; "sluggish velocities of 1-10 cm/year") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_structure_of_Earth ("Although solid, the mantle's extremely hot silicate material can flow over very long timescales") ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/mantle/ ("the mostly solid bulk of Earth's interior"; rocks "soft and able to move plastically (over the course of millions of years)")

F13. Mantle temperature runs from about 1,000 degrees Celsius near the crust (NatGeo; Grokipedia gives about 1,300 °C at 100 km) to more than 3,000 degrees Celsius near the core (NatGeo: 3,700 °C).
    https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/mantle/ ("from 1000°C (1832°F) near its boundary with the crust, to 3700°C (6692°F) near its boundary with the core") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_mantle (temperatures "exceeding 3,000°C near the core boundary")

F14. Only small parts of the mantle are partly melted (a layer with about 1-3 percent melt under the plates); the mantle is not a sea of magma.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_mantle ("partly molten rock", about 1-3% melt, at the lithosphere-asthenosphere boundary)

## The outer core

F15. The outer core lies below the mantle, from about 2,890 km to about 5,150 km deep, about 2,200-2,260 km thick. It is liquid (molten) metal: an iron-nickel alloy, about 85-90 percent iron and 5-10 percent nickel, with some lighter elements.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_outer_core ("a fluid layer of molten metal"; "primarily composed of iron and nickel"; "a thickness of about 2,260 kilometers") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Internal_structure_of_Earth ("a molten iron-nickel alloy containing 85-90% iron, 5-10% nickel") ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/core/ ("about 2,200 kilometers (1,367 miles) thick, is mostly composed of liquid iron and nickel")

F16. The outer core's swirling liquid iron makes Earth's magnetic field (the geodynamo). (Lure use only; the field is a T3/T4 unit's.) Its temperature is NOT used: Grokipedia's Outer core page says about 4,000-5,700 °C, its Internal structure page says 4,000-5,700 K, and NatGeo says 4,500-5,500 °C while giving the inner core only 5,200 °C; the sources disagree.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_outer_core ("generates the planet's geomagnetic field through a process known as the geodynamo") ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/core/

## The inner core

F17. The inner core is a solid ball of iron and nickel at Earth's centre, with a radius of about 1,220 km.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_inner_core ("a dense, solid sphere primarily composed of iron and nickel alloys"; "a radius of approximately 1,220 kilometers") ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/core/ ("a radius of about 1,220 kilometers")

F18. The inner core is the hottest layer: Grokipedia puts the outer core at about 4,000 K near the mantle rising to 5,700 K, and the inner core at about 5,700-6,000 K at its boundary (about 5,400 °C or more). That is about the temperature of the Sun's surface (the photosphere, about 5,772 K, or about 5,500 °C).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Internal_structure_of_Earth (outer core "roughly 4,000 K near the core-mantle boundary to 5,700 K"; inner core "around 5,700-6,000 K at the inner core boundary") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_inner_core ("up to 5,400°C (9,800°F)"; about 5,700 K, "comparable to the surface temperature of the Sun") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_inner_core ("approximately 5,700 K"; "about the temperature at the surface of the Sun") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosphere ("approximately 5772 K")

F19. The inner core stays solid, though hotter than the liquid outer core, because of its enormous pressure: at such pressure the temperature iron needs to melt rises far above its surface melting point.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth%27s_inner_core ("The inner core is solid at high temperature because of its high pressure"; "its melting temperature increases dramatically at pressures of that magnitude") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_inner_core ("immense pressure from the overlying layers keeps it" solid) ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/core/ ("The inner core's intense pressure ... prevents the iron from melting")

F20. At the surface (1 atmosphere), iron melts at 1,538 degrees Celsius and nickel at 1,455 degrees Celsius; mixing in nickel does not raise the melting point.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Iron ("The melting point of iron is 1538°C (1811 K) at 1 atm") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nickel ("Its melting point is 1455°C")

## Temperature and pressure rise with depth

F21. Temperature rises with depth: in typical continental crust by about 25-30 degrees Celsius per kilometre. The heat flows outward from Earth's hot interior; it is heat left from Earth's formation plus heat from radioactive decay.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Geothermal_gradient ("approximately 25-30 °C per kilometer"; "conduction of heat from the planet's hotter interior toward the cooler surface"; "residual thermal energy from Earth's formation" and decay of U-238, Th-232, K-40) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_gradient ("about 25-30 °C/km"; "the crust temperature rises with depth due to the heat flow from the much hotter mantle")

F22. The Sun's heat reaches only the top layer of the ground: the seasonal swing fades within about 8-20 m, and below that the ground's temperature rises with depth by the geothermal gradient.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_source_heat_pump ("to a depth of about 8-20 metres depending on soil type, the thermal mass of the ground causes temperature variation to decrease exponentially with depth"; below, "rising about 0.025 °C per metre according to the geothermal gradient") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Geothermal_gradient (shallow effects are "transient solar or seasonal effects")

F23. Pressure in the rock rises with depth because of the weight of the rock above (overburden or lithostatic pressure), about 1 psi per foot (22.7 kPa per metre) in the crust. At only 5 km down it is about 1,300-1,400 atmospheres.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Overburden_pressure ("the vertical stress exerted at a subsurface point due to the weight of the overlying rock"; it "increases with depth"; "approximately 1 psi/ft (22.7 kPa/m)") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Internal_structure_of_Earth ("approximately 130-140 MPa (roughly 1,300-1,400 atmospheres)" at about 5 km)

F24. At the centre the pressure is about 330-365 gigapascals, nearly 3.6 million times the air pressure at sea level.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_inner_core (about 330 GPa at the inner core boundary, about 365 GPa at the centre) ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/core/ ("nearly 3.6 million atmospheres (atm)") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_gradient (central pressure near 360 GPa)

F25. Earth's spin makes it bulge OUTWARD at the equator (equatorial radius about 21 km more than the polar); spin does not squeeze rock inward. (Lure use.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Equatorial_bulge ("The primary cause of the equatorial bulge is the planet's rotation"; "about 21.4 km")

## Why the iron is at the centre

F26. Early Earth was partly melted (magma oceans about 4.5 billion years ago). Dense iron sank toward the centre while lighter silicate rock rose, so the layers are sorted by density: the iron-nickel core averages about 11 g/cm3, the mantle about 4.4 and the crust about 2.7. Core formation was complete within about 30 million years.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Planetary_differentiation ("heavier elements like iron sinking toward the center while lighter silicates rise to the surface"; "a nickel-iron core with an average density of about 11 g/cm3"; "core segregation within the first 30 million years") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Iron ("Iron is the dominant component of Earth's core, comprising approximately 85% of its mass")

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. The mantle (or its lower part, or the layer under the plates) is liquid, a sea of melted rock. In fact the mantle is mostly solid rock that creeps (F12, F14). SERC's list of Earth-science misconceptions (NAGT) names "Asthenosphere is liquid" and "Lower Mantle is liquid".
    https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/intro/misconception_list.html

M2. Magma comes from a molten layer just beneath the crust, or from the outer core. SERC lists "Magma comes from molten layer beneath Earth's crust" and "Magma comes from Earth's outer core". Used for the "melted rock just below" and "liquid melted rock" lures (F14, F15).
    https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/intro/misconception_list.html

M3. Hotter means melted, so the liquid outer core must be hotter than the solid inner core. In fact the inner core is the hotter (F18, F19). (Teaching note.)

M4. The ground stays cool deep down, as a cellar or cave does; in fact only the top few metres follow the surface, and the rock warms with depth (F21, F22). (Teaching note.)

M5. The core is the biggest part of Earth; in fact the mantle is about 84 percent of Earth's volume (F11). (Teaching note.)

M6. Melting needs air, as burning does; melting needs only heat, so the lack of air at the centre does nothing to keep iron solid. (Teaching note; physical change, no oxygen involved.)

M7. Sediment layers (rock layers seen in a cliff) are Earth's layers. They are layers of the crust only (`rock-layers-and-fossils`); not used here.
