## The first law

F1. Conservation of energy: "the total energy of an isolated system remains constant over time"; energy "can neither be created nor destroyed but only transformed between different forms." The principle rules out perpetual-motion machines of the first kind. It emerged in the mid-1800s: Julius Robert Mayer proposed it in 1842, James Joule measured the mechanical equivalent of heat in the 1840s, and Hermann von Helmholtz framed it as a universal law in 1847.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Conservation_of_energy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_equivalent_of_heat

F2. Julius Robert Mayer, a German physician, sailed as ship's doctor on a Dutch ship to the East Indies in 1840; he found sailors' venous blood brighter red in the tropics and concluded that the body burns less fuel for heat in a warm climate. His 1842 paper argued that the forces of nature are "indestructible and convertible" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: "Mayer was the first person to state the law of the conservation of energy." His work was at first overlooked, his "experimental method wasn't as convincing" (Wikipedia, Mechanical equivalent of heat), a bitter priority dispute with Joule followed, and he received the Royal Society's Copley Medal in 1871.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Julius_von_Mayer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Robert_von_Mayer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_equivalent_of_heat

F3. Joule's paddle wheel: falling weights, on strings wound round a shaft, turned a brass paddle wheel with eight arms between fixed vanes inside a copper cylinder of water; the work put in (weight times distance fallen) was compared with the water's rise in temperature. He read "On the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat" to the British Association at Cambridge in June 1845. Joule ran his family's brewery in Salford.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Prescott_Joule ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Prescott_Joule ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4360093/

F4. Joule's 1850 paper (Philosophical Transactions) gathered five series: water stirred by a brass paddle wheel (772.692 foot-pounds per British thermal unit), mercury stirred by a wrought-iron paddle wheel in a cast-iron cylinder (772.814 and 775.352), and a cast-iron wheel rubbing against a stationary cast-iron wheel under mercury (776.045 and 773.930). All fall between about 772.7 and 776.0. His conclusions: "the quantity of heat produced by the friction of bodies, whether solid or liquid, is always proportional to the quantity of force expended", and warming a pound of water by 1 degree Fahrenheit needs "the fall of 772 lb through the space of one foot."
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4360093/ (Royal Society commentary on Joule 1850) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mechanical_equivalent_of_heat ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Prescott_Joule (772.692)

F5. Joule's principle in his own words: "Wherever mechanical force is expended, an exact equivalent of heat is always obtained." Reception: silence at Cork in 1843; the Royal Society rejected his paper of 20 June 1844; William Thomson (Kelvin) was sceptical at Oxford in 1847, but by 1851 wrote that "the whole theory of the motive power of heat is founded on two propositions, due respectively to Joule, and to Carnot and Clausius." Michael Faraday, refereeing the 1850 paper, praised the experiments but would not accept that friction converts mechanical power into heat; that proposition was cut.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Prescott_Joule ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4360093/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Prescott_Joule

F6. Joule's work on heat and mechanical work led to the law of conservation of energy and so to the first law of thermodynamics (Wikipedia); the mechanical equivalent "had an important part in the development and acceptance of the conservation of energy" (Wikipedia, Mechanical equivalent of heat). The modern value is about 4.18 joules per calorie.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Prescott_Joule ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_equivalent_of_heat ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mechanical_equivalent_of_heat

## Caloric and Count Rumford

F7. The caloric theory held that heat is a "self-repellent fluid", weightless, flowing from hotter bodies to colder ones, and conserved: it "could neither be created nor destroyed." Antoine Lavoisier was its central figure; the word "caloric" was coined in 1787 by Guyton de Morveau working with him.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caloric_theory ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Benjamin_Thompson

F8. Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford, superintended the boring of cannon in the workshops of the military arsenal at Munich and was struck by the heat of the brass and of the chips, hotter than boiling water. He fitted a deliberately blunted borer against a cannon enclosed in a watertight box of water, turned by horses; the water (18.77 lb, starting at 60 degrees F) "actually boiled" at two hours thirty minutes, and he wrote of "the surprise and astonishment expressed in the countenances of the bystanders" at water made to boil without any fire. Paper: "An Inquiry Concerning the Source of the Heat Which Is Excited by Friction", Philosophical Transactions 88 (1798).
    https://av8n.com/physics/refs/rumford-1798.html (Rumford's text) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Inquiry_Concerning_the_Source_of_the_Heat_Which_Is_Excited_by_Friction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Benjamin_Thompson

F9. Rumford's argument: the source of the heat "appeared evidently to be inexhaustible", and "anything which any insulated body, or system of bodies, can continue to furnish without limitation cannot possibly be a material substance"; he could form no distinct idea of it "except it be MOTION." He also found that chips put into water warmed it neither more nor less than solid slips of the same metal, so the metal had not changed (nothing had been squeezed out of it). The surrounding water "was continually receiving heat from the machinery and could not, at the same time, be giving to" it; no chemical decomposition of the water took place (he checked for gas); and with the machinery "kept immersed in water, the access of the air of the atmosphere was completely prevented." Wikipedia: "Heat was a form of motion, not the caloric of then-current thinking."
    https://av8n.com/physics/refs/rumford-1798.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Thompson ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Benjamin_Thompson

F10. Honest caveat: Rumford's paper "met with a hostile reception"; most established scientists, such as William Henry and Thomas Thomson, believed caloric could be adapted to the results (critics said caloric was "liberated" from compressed states in the solid). Joule's more exact measurements of the 1840s were pivotal in retiring caloric; it was superseded by the mid-1800s but lingered in some popular texts to the end of the century. MISCONCEPTION: that Rumford's cannon ended caloric at a stroke.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Inquiry_Concerning_the_Source_of_the_Heat_Which_Is_Excited_by_Friction ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caloric_theory ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Benjamin_Thompson

## The second law: Carnot and Clausius

F11. Sadi Carnot published "Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire" in Paris in 1824 (600 copies, printed at his own cost). He reasoned within the caloric theory, comparing caloric falling from a hot body to a cold one with water falling through a height; an engine needs both a hot body and a cold body. The motive power's "quantity is fixed solely by the temperatures of the bodies" between which it works, independent of the working substance (steam or air). The book drew little notice until Émile Clapeyron's commentary of 1834. Carnot died of cholera on 24 August 1832, aged 36.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nicolas_Léonard_Sadi_Carnot ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_L%C3%A9onard_Sadi_Carnot

F12. "The theoretical maximum efficiency of any heat engine depends only on the temperatures it operates between" (Wikipedia, Heat engine); the Carnot efficiency is 1 - Tc/Th in absolute temperatures, so the wider the gap, the larger the share of heat that can become work, and no engine reaches 100 percent (Grokipedia). Heat engines "distinguish themselves from other types of engines" in that their efficiency is "fundamentally limited by Carnot's theorem."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Second_law_of_thermodynamics ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Heat_engine

F13. Two engines for comparison. A coal-fired steam plant runs on steam "typically at steam temperatures around 540°C" and is cooled by water from rivers, lakes or cooling towers; such plants turn about 30 to 40 percent of their heat into electricity (Energy Education: an average coal plant about 33 percent). Ocean thermal energy conversion (OTEC) runs a heat engine on the gap between warm surface seawater and cold deep seawater, typically "a difference of 20°C or more"; its "Theoretical Carnot efficiency is capped at 6–8% due to the modest temperature gradient", and net efficiency is often below 3 percent.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_power_station ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ocean_thermal_energy_conversion ; https://energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Heat_engine ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Heat_engine

F14. Rudolf Clausius, "On the Moving Force of Heat" (1850). Carnot had said "expressly that no heat is lost in the process, but that the quantity of heat remains unchanged", the work being found "in the mere transfer of heat from a hotter to a colder body." Clausius, taking Joule's measurements as near proof that heat is made in proportion to work, kept the transfer but rejected the "undiminished": "the accomplishment of work requires not merely a change in the distribution of heat, but also an actual consumption of heat." So an engine turns part of its heat into work and passes the rest to the cold body; the caloric theory and Carnot's principle fit together "if the conservation of heat was replaced by the conservation of energy" (Wikipedia). Wikipedia (Heat engine): an engine converts part of the heat "to useful work and giving off the rest as waste heat to the cold temperature heat sink."
    https://mdpi.org/lin/clausius/clausius.htm (Le Moyne College Classic Chemistry, Clausius 1850 and 1865 excerpts from Magie's Source Book in Physics) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caloric_theory ; https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Clausius/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine

F15. Power plants give off waste heat: steam Rankine cycles "reject up to two-thirds of input energy as waste heat" through the condenser and cooling towers (Grokipedia, Cooling tower); condenser losses are "typically 50-60% of input energy" (Grokipedia, Thermal power station), because a heat engine must reject heat to a cold sink at a lower temperature.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cooling_tower ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_power_station ; https://energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Heat_engine ("a large cold reservoir is needed in order to get rid of waste heat")

F16. The second law's statements. Clausius (1854, in English 1856): "Heat can never pass from a colder to a warmer body without some other change, connected therewith, occurring at the same time." Kelvin-Planck: it is impossible for a device working in a cycle to receive heat from a single reservoir and turn all of it into work. Grokipedia: the law rules out "perpetual motion machines of the second kind."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Second_law_of_thermodynamics

F17. Clausius coined "entropy" in 1865 from the Greek for "transformation", formed "so as to be as similar as possible to the word energy"; the 1865 paper closes: "The energy of the universe is constant. The entropy of the universe tends toward a maximum." The entropy of an isolated system left to itself cannot decrease.
    https://mdpi.org/lin/clausius/clausius.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Entropy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics

F18. Energy conserved, usefulness lost: "When entropy increases, a certain amount of energy becomes permanently unavailable to do work. The energy is not lost, but its character is changed" (OpenStax College Physics 2e, 15.6). Grokipedia: entropy "measures the amount of thermal energy unavailable for conversion into mechanical work"; Peter Atkins: it measures how far a system's energy "becomes spread out." The "disorder" gloss "is vague and subjective" (Grokipedia). Spontaneous heat transfer from hot to cold is irreversible (OpenStax). MISCONCEPTION: that energy is "used up".
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/15-6-entropy-and-the-second-law-of-thermodynamics-disorder-and-the-unavailability-of-energy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Entropy

## Refrigerators

F19. "A refrigerator causes heat to flow from cold to hot by inputting work"; refrigerators do not break the second law because of "the work needed as input to the system", and a refrigerator "must exhaust more heat to the room than it removes from the inside" (Energy Education). Left open in a closed room, a fridge does not cool it: "some of the electrical energy gets converted to heat, so more heat gets dumped outside the fridge than gets pumped out", and "the fridge will still be heating the room more than cooling it" (University of Illinois physics Q&A). MISCONCEPTIONS: a fridge "makes cold"; an open fridge cools a room.
    https://energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Refrigerator ; https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/1790 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Second_law_of_thermodynamics (refrigerators "require work input to transfer heat from a cold reservoir to a hot one")

## Perpetual motion

F20. Kinds of impossible machine: the first kind produces work without a matching energy input and violates the first law; the second kind "spontaneously converts thermal energy into mechanical work" (Wikipedia), converting heat entirely into work with no waste heat, and violates the second law; a second-kind machine "would not violate the first law" (Lund University's Fysikens frågelåda, answering a question about a ship that takes heat from seawater). A third kind merely tries to remove all friction; that is unattainable but not forbidden by thermodynamics (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Perpetual_motion ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_motion ; https://fragelada.fysik.lu.se/?id=19554 ; https://home.iitk.ac.in/~gtm/thermodynamics/lecture17/17_2.htm

F21. The ship that runs on seawater heat alone is a second-kind machine (Lund Q&A, quoting "It is impossible to convert heat quantitatively into work with no other result"). Real ocean heat plants work only by using the temperature difference between warm surface water and cold deep water (F13).
    https://fragelada.fysik.lu.se/?id=19554 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ocean_thermal_energy_conversion

F22. The overbalanced wheel: Bhāskara II designed a wheel with curved spokes partly filled with mercury around 1150 CE; a perpetual-motion drawing appears in the 13th-century sketchbook of the French master mason Villard de Honnecourt; Leonardo da Vinci drew overbalanced wheels but scorned the pursuit. The design fails because the side with weights farther from the centre "has fewer weights than the other side", so the turning forces (torques) balance (Wikipedia); Grokipedia: the torque on the descending side is offset by that on the ascending side. A weight's turning effect grows with its distance from the axle (lever rule), which is why the far-out weights look as if they should win. Over a full turn each weight comes back to where it began, so it is lifted as far as it falls (reasoning from F1).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_motion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Perpetual_motion ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bh%C4%81skara%27s_wheel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_perpetual_motion_machines

F23. The Royal Academy of Sciences in Paris stated in 1775 that it "will no longer accept or deal with proposals concerning perpetual motion" (Wikipedia); the same decision barred squaring the circle and trisecting the angle (Marie Jacob, Revue d'histoire des mathématiques 11, 2005). Encyclopaedia Universalis: under Laplace's influence the Academy condemned perpetual motion; it granted that, if friction and resistances were removed, a motion once given to some systems might last forever, but held that "un effet étant toujours égal à sa cause" (an effect is always equal to its cause), and judged the pursuit guilty of uselessly consuming "les talents, le temps et la fortune" of too many ingenious mechanics. The US Patent Office still asks for a working model in perpetual-motion cases.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_perpetual_motion_machines ; https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/mouvement-perpetuel/5-la-condamnation-est-elle-sans-appel/ ; https://eudml.org/doc/252116 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_motion

## Efficiency of real machines

F24. "Only about 12%–30% of the energy from the fuel you put in a conventional vehicle is used to move it down the road"; "In gasoline-powered vehicles, most of the fuel's energy is lost in the engine, primarily as heat"; "Smaller amounts of energy are lost through engine friction, pumping air into and out of the engine" and combustion inefficiency; engine losses run 64% to 75% (highway to city). Electric vehicles convert "over 77% of the electrical energy from the grid to power at the wheels"; an electric car's drive system loses "15% to 20%" against "64% to 75% for a gasoline engine." A car's engine is a heat engine, one that "produces macroscopic motion from heat" (Energy Education).
    https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/atv.shtml ; https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/evtech.shtml ; https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/atv-ev.shtml ; https://energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Heat_engine

F25. Electric motors convert electrical energy into mechanical energy directly (Energy Education), with efficiencies up to 97 percent for induction motors at full load and 90 percent or more for high-efficiency motors (Grokipedia). They are not heat engines, and only heat engines have an efficiency "fundamentally limited by Carnot's theorem" (Wikipedia, Heat engine; compare fuel cells, which Grokipedia excludes from heat engines because they turn chemical energy into electrical work without a temperature gradient).
    https://energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Electric_motor ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electric_motor ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Heat_engine

## Entropy and the arrow of time

F26. Rising entropy gives time its direction: "eggs break but do not unbreak" (Grokipedia); a film of dye spreading through water looks wrong run backwards (Wikipedia). The microscopic laws are time-symmetric: "individual particle trajectories can be reversed without changing the laws of motion" (Grokipedia). Reverse processes are not forbidden but "overwhelmingly improbable", because disordered states vastly outnumber ordered ones (Grokipedia); the second law is statistical, and a gas gathering on one side is possible in principle but so improbable it has never been seen (Wikipedia). Boltzmann: higher entropy means more possible microscopic arrangements (Grokipedia, Entropy).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Entropy_as_an_arrow_of_time ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_as_an_arrow_of_time ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Entropy

F27. Arthur Eddington developed the idea of the arrow of time in 1927: "I shall use the phrase 'time's arrow' to express this one-way property of time"; his 1928 book The Nature of the Physical World spread it.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow_of_time ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Entropy_as_an_arrow_of_time

F28. Open question: why the universe had such low entropy in the past is listed as an unsolved problem (Wikipedia); the "Past Hypothesis" is "a provisional explanation, pending deeper insights from quantum gravity" (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_as_an_arrow_of_time ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Entropy_as_an_arrow_of_time

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- Energy is "used up" or destroyed when fuel burns or brakes rub (F1, F18).
- Heat is a fluid stored in matter and squeezed out by rubbing (F7, F9, F10).
- A free-energy machine fails only because of friction; or a machine that runs on the heat around it makes energy from nothing (F20, F21).
- Gravity pulls more weakly on a weight farther from an axle (F22: only the lever arm changes the turning effect).
- A fridge "makes cold", so an open fridge cools a room (F19).
- An engine that takes in the same heat must give the same work whatever its temperatures (F12, F13); more water or more total heat means more work.
- The laws of motion forbid motion run backwards; a broken egg cannot reform because it would need new energy (F26).
- An engine burns its fuel incompletely, and that is where most of its energy goes (F24: the loss is mostly heat).

## Added in revision (sources read 2026-11-27)

F29. Rumford's set-up, in his own words: "a blunt steel borer being so forcibly shoved (by means of a strong screw) against the bottom of the bore of the cylinder", while "the cylinder was turned round on its axis (by the force of horses)"; so the borer was held still and the horses turned the cannon against it. With "the machinery being kept immersed in water, the access of the air of the atmosphere was completely prevented"; the chips' heat was "much greater than that of boiling water, as I found by experiment"; and the source of the heat "appeared evidently to be inexhaustible", for anything a body "can continue to furnish without limitation cannot possibly be a material substance."

F30. Burning a hydrocarbon fuel such as gasoline does not destroy its matter: "In complete combustion of hydrocarbons, carbon dioxide and water form stoichiometrically" (Grokipedia), and Lavoisier's closed-vessel weighings showed mass conserved. So a car's fuel leaves the exhaust as carbon dioxide and water, not as nothing.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Combustion

F31. The overbalanced wheel: "moving weights are attached to a wheel in such a way that they fall to a position further from the wheel's center for one half of the wheel's rotation" and closer for the other half; "the side with weights further from the center has fewer weights than the other side", so "the torque is balanced"; Wikipedia's figure is captioned as showing "that the torques on both sides even out on average." (Not at every angle: the wheel settles where they balance, per the fact-check.)
