# How Light Travels: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-25. Grokipedia first; Wikipedia, curriculum documents and university pages to corroborate.
Neighbouring units: `the-sun-and-its-planets` asks that the Moon shines by reflected sunlight; `day-night-and-shadows` asks that a shadow forms where an object blocks light and how shadows change through the day; `waves-carry-energy` (T2) has light crossing empty space; `energy-changes-form` has light as a form of energy. None of those is asked here.

## Facts

F1 Light travels in straight lines through one material (air, water, glass) that is the same all through; it changes direction only when it is reflected or passes into a different material. It does not curve round things on its own. https://grokipedia.com/page/Rectilinear_propagation ("light rays travel in straight lines" in a uniform medium); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.8c-how-light-travels ("Light moves in straight paths until it meets something. It does not curve on its own")

F2 The three-card test: a hole is punched in each of three cards, and the cards stand in a row between a flashlight and a wall. With the holes lined up, the light passes through all three; move the middle card to one side so its hole is out of line and the light is stopped, because it cannot bend round the card. https://serc.carleton.edu/sp/mnstep/activities/20128.html (grades 3-6: "When the three holes on the cards are lined up the light travels through the holes"; "If the middle card is moved to one side so that the holes are not lined up the light cannot 'bend' around the middle card"; "light travels in a straight line and cannot bend around an object")

F3 A shadow is a dark area where light from a source is blocked by an object, and it has the object's outline (its silhouette); because light travels in straight lines, a shadow has the same shape as the object that casts it. A wide light source blurs the shadow's edges, so a shadow is not always sharp. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow ("a dark area on a surface where light from a light source is blocked by an object"; "a two-dimensional silhouette"; "the wider the light source, the more blurred the shadow becomes"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Rectilinear_propagation (a point source and an opaque object give "a sharp, completely dark region known as the umbra"); England's national curriculum, Year 6 (ages 10-11), https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study ("use the idea that light travels in straight lines to explain why shadows have the same shape as the objects that cast them")

F4 Reflection is light bouncing off a surface; a mirror is the clearest example. https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.8c-how-light-travels ("Reflection: Light bounces off a surface. A mirror is the clearest example, and most surfaces reflect some light"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Reflection_(physics) ("Reflection enables technologies like mirrors in optics")

F5 A mirror's surface is smooth (glass with a thin coat of silver or aluminium behind it), so it reflects light in an orderly way and forms a clear image. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mirror ("forms an image of an object by reflecting light rays in a predictable manner"; "a glass substrate coated with a thin layer of silver or aluminum"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Reflection_(physics) ("specular (on smooth surfaces, producing coherent images)")

F6 Nearly all surfaces reflect light, not only shiny ones. A rough surface such as paper or cloth scatters the light it reflects in many directions (diffuse reflection), so it shows no clear image; that scattered light is how most everyday objects are seen. https://grokipedia.com/page/Diffuse_reflection ("the scattering of light rays in multiple directions upon encountering a rough or irregular surface", paper and cloth as examples; "diffuse reflection does not produce clear images"; it "enables the visibility of color, texture, and shape for most everyday objects"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.8c-how-light-travels ("Nearly all surfaces reflect light. Mirrors are smooth, so they reflect light in one consistent direction, which forms a clear image")

F7 White objects reflect nearly all the colours of light about equally; that is why they look white. https://grokipedia.com/page/Diffuse_reflection (white objects "diffusely reflect all visible wavelengths nearly equally")

F8 An object is seen when light from it enters the eyes: a light source (the Sun, a lamp, a flame) gives out its own light, and other things are seen because light from a source reflects off them into the eyes. Without light nothing can be seen; dark is the absence of light. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/4-ps4-2-waves-and-their-applications-technologies-information-transfer (PS4.B: "An object can be seen when light reflected from its surface enters the eyes"); England's national curriculum, Year 6 ("objects are seen because they give out or reflect light"; "light travels from light sources to objects and then to our eyes") and Year 3 ("they need light in order to see things and that dark is the absence of light"), https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study

F9 The eyes do not send out light or rays. The old "emission" idea that vision comes from rays sent out of the eye was held by Euclid and Ptolemy; Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen) argued in his Book of Optics that light from objects enters the eye. https://grokipedia.com/page/Emission_theory_(vision) ("vision arises from the active emission of rays or a subtle substance from the eyes"; Euclid's "conical rays projecting from the eye"; intromission: "light rays originating from external objects enter the eye passively")

F10 Refraction is the bending (change of direction) of light as it passes from one material into another, such as from water into air; it happens because light changes speed. Light travels more slowly in water and glass than in air. https://grokipedia.com/page/Refraction ("Refraction is the change in direction of a wave"; it "results from a change in its transmission speed"; "Light travels more slowly in denser media, such as glass compared to air"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_light (refractive index about 1.33 for water and 1.5 for glass); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.8c-how-light-travels ("Refraction: Light bends when it passes from one substance into another")

F11 A straw or pencil standing in a glass of water looks bent or broken at the water's surface because of refraction; the straw itself is not bent, since the light from its underwater part bends as it moves from the water into the air. https://grokipedia.com/page/Refraction ("a straw or pencil partially immersed in a glass of water appears bent at the water's surface"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.8c-how-light-travels ("The pencil is not broken. Refraction bends the light from the submerged part as it moves from water into air")

F12 Refraction also makes a pool or lake look shallower than it really is. https://grokipedia.com/page/Refraction (refraction can make "pools or lakes seem shallower than they are")

F13 A lens is a piece of clear material, usually curved glass, that uses refraction to bring light rays together or spread them apart. A magnifying glass is a single convex (converging) lens; it bends the light so that a small thing seen through it looks bigger. Lenses are used in eyeglasses, cameras, microscopes and telescopes. https://grokipedia.com/page/Lens_(optics) ("transmissive optical component made of transparent material"; "a simple magnifying glass employs a converging lens"; cameras, binoculars, microscopes, telescopes; corrective eyeglasses); https://grokipedia.com/page/Refraction ("Lenses are optical devices that exploit refraction to converge or diverge light rays"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnifying_glass ("a single convex lens"; the lens refracts light and the eye sees an enlarged image)

F14 A prism (a block of clear glass with flat, slanting sides) spreads white light into a band of colours, a spectrum, from red to violet, because each colour bends by a different amount: violet most, red least. https://grokipedia.com/page/Prism_(optics) ("different colors deviate by different amounts and separate into a spectrum"; "shorter wavelengths (e.g., violet) deviating more than longer ones (e.g., red)"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Dispersion_(optics) ("the separation of white light into its constituent spectral colors"); Virginia grade 5 standard SOL 5.3 key concepts, https://www.solpass.org/science4-5/guides/light-standards.html (a glass prism separates white light into its colors, red to violet; combined, the colors make white light)

F15 White light, sunlight included, is a mix of all the colours of the rainbow. Isaac Newton's prism experiments showed that white sunlight is "a heterogeneous mixture of distinct colored rays", and a second prism turned the other way gathered the coloured rays back into a single beam of white light. https://grokipedia.com/page/Opticks ("recombined the colored rays into a single beam of white light"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Color (white light is "a composite of these separable components"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Light ("the spectral nature of white light")

F16 Before Newton, a respectable theory (Robert Hooke's modification theory) held that colours are made when refraction alters white light, so that the glass gives colour to plain, colourless light; Newton's recombination of the colours into white "refuted the idea that refraction imparts color to colorless light". https://grokipedia.com/page/Opticks

F17 Sunlight looks white to the human eye, not yellow, when all its colours arrive together. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sunlight (the spectrum "explains why sunlight appears white to the human eye when integrated across its spectrum")

F18 The colours of a spectrum or rainbow run smoothly into one another from red to violet; the seven named colours are a convention from Newton, not seven separate bands. A rainbow forms when sunlight is refracted, reflected inside and spread into colours by raindrops, red on the outer edge and violet on the inner, seen with the Sun behind the watcher. https://grokipedia.com/page/Rainbow ("formed by the refraction, internal reflection, and dispersion of sunlight within suspended water droplets"; "longer wavelengths (red) on the outer edge and shorter wavelengths (violet) on the inner edge"; "The convention of seven discrete colors"; "the sun directly behind them")

F19 Red, yellow and blue are the painters' traditional primary colours for mixing paints. Light mixes differently (red, green and blue light together make white), and sunlight holds a continuous range of colours from red to violet, not just three. https://grokipedia.com/page/Primary_color ("red, yellow, and blue (RYB) have been taught as subtractive primaries"; for light, "the primary colors are red, green, and blue (RGB)"; sunlight disperses into a "continuous spectrum of colors ranging from red to violet")

F20 Absorption is light being taken in (soaked up) by a material; the absorbed light's energy mostly turns into heat. Dark things absorb most of the light that hits them, and white things reflect more of it, so in sunlight a black shirt gets hotter than a white one. https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/1804 (dark clothes "absorb most of the light that hits them"; that energy "is changed into heat"; white clothing "simply reflects more of the light"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.8c-how-light-travels ("Absorption: Light soaks into a material and usually turns into heat. A black shirt in sunlight gets hotter than a white one"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Black (black pigments work "by absorbing rather than reflecting incident light", with "energy dissipation as heat"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Albedo (dark surfaces have low albedo, "enhancing absorption and local warming"; fresh snow reflects 0.80 to 0.90 of visible light)

F21 An object's colour comes from the colours of light it reflects; it absorbs most of the others. A red object reflects mainly red light and absorbs most of the rest; leaves look green because chlorophyll reflects green and absorbs red and blue. https://grokipedia.com/page/Color (colour results from "the selective reflection, absorption, or emission of light wavelengths by surfaces"; chlorophyll "absorbs red and blue light strongly but reflects green"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Pigment (a red pigment absorbs blue and green and sends the red back); a Grade 5 unit "Interacting With Light", https://www.slideshare.net/STEM4ALL/why-do-objects-have-color (red objects reflect red and absorb the other colours; white reflect all; black absorb all)

F22 Clear glass, water and air are transparent (light passes through); frosted glass and wax paper are translucent (some light passes, scattered); wood, metal and brick are opaque (no light passes). https://grokipedia.com/page/Transparency_and_translucency (transparent: "allows light to pass through it with minimal absorption, scattering, or distortion"; translucent: frosted glass; opaque: "light is fully absorbed or reflected"); https://www.solpass.org/science4-5/guides/light-standards.html (Virginia grade 5 examples)

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Seeing by rays from the eye. Allen (2010), Misconceptions in Primary Science, lists "We see things because light travels from our eyes towards an object" (SCIMAP, grades PreK-5: https://wordpressua.uark.edu/scimap/2023/05/26/light/). Sampson and Schleigh (2013), NSTA Press, list "Our eyes produce light so we can see things" (https://static.nsta.org/connections/elementaryschool/201404Schleigh.pdf). Corrected by F8, F9.

M2 Only shiny things reflect light. NSTA list: "Only shiny materials reflect light"; students' claims "Only shiny objects that I can see myself in reflect light", "Only metals and water reflect light" (Sampson and Schleigh, above). Kesler TEKS 5.8C guide: "Only mirrors reflect light". Corrected by F6, F7.

M3 Things that reflect light are light sources. NSTA list: "Objects that reflect are sources of light (e.g., the Moon)". Used here only for an ordinary object (a wooden chair), not the Moon.

M4 The straw or pencil really bends. Kesler TEKS 5.8C guide: "A pencil really breaks when it goes in water". Corrected by F11.

M5 Light bends round corners on its own. Kesler TEKS 5.8C guide: "Light bends around corners on its own"; SERC's three-card test is built to show it cannot (F2).

M6 Black reflects no light at all, and white absorbs none. NSTA list: "Black does not reflect any light and/or white does not absorb any light." So the questions say "most" and "more", never "all" or "none".

M7 All colours of clothes warm the same in the sun. Kesler TEKS 5.8C guide: "All colors of clothes get equally warm in the sun." The "black pulls in heat from the air" form of the same mix-up is the author's judgment (no survey found); colour does not change how much heat a shirt takes from the air, it changes how much sunlight it absorbs (F20).

M8 A shadow is a reflection. NSTA list: "A shadow is a reflection from the Sun." The idea that a shadow is a dark something that spreads out of the object is the author's judgment, from teacher resources that list "shadows come from within objects" among children's beliefs (seen only in a search summary, not read in full).

M9 The prism (the glass) adds the colours to plain light. This is the honest wrong theory of Newton's day (F16, Hooke's modification theory). That white light is colourless, or a single colour, is the misconception the prism experiment corrects (author's reading of F15-F17).

M10 Sunlight is yellow. Author's judgment (children draw the Sun yellow); sunlight looks white when its colours arrive together (F17).

M11 White light is a mix of the paint primaries, red, yellow and blue. Author's judgment, from the known confusion of paint primaries with light primaries (F19).

M12 The colour of an object is reversed (a red apple absorbs red and reflects the rest), or the object makes its own coloured light, or turns white light into its colour. Author's judgment; all three are wrong by F21 and F8.

M13 Light needs air to travel. NSTA list: "Light needs air to travel." Not used here (light crossing empty space is the `waves-carry-energy` unit's).

## Notes and cautions

- Do not say shadows always have sharp edges: a wide light source blurs them (F3). Say only that a shadow has the object's shape.
- Do not say black absorbs ALL light or white reflects ALL light (M6): say "most" or "more".
- Do not ask that the Moon shines by reflected light (the-sun-and-its-planets), that a shadow forms where light is blocked or how shadows change through the day (day-night-and-shadows), or that light crosses empty space (waves-carry-energy).
- Real periscopes often use prisms as well as mirrors, so a periscope question with "prism" as a wrong choice would be broken; none is asked.
- No named scientist in the stems (T1); Newton and Hooke appear only in this fact sheet, and the prism question's context says only what a second prism does.
