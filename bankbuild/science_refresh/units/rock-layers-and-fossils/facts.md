# Rock Layers and Fossils: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; Wikipedia, NGSS, TEKS lesson pages and others to corroborate.
Curriculum line G5.E4 (TEKS 5.10B: "Model and describe the processes that led to the formation of sedimentary rocks and fossil fuels", as quoted on https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation).

## Facts

F1 Sediment is the loose, solid bits that come from the weathering and erosion of rock, together with shells and plant debris; water, wind, ice or gravity carry it and drop it somewhere else. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sediment ("solid particulate matter derived from the weathering and erosion of pre-existing rocks"; "transported by agents such as water, wind, ice, or gravity"; "biogenic remains such as shells and plant debris"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation ("Tiny bits of rock, sand, mud, and shells settle into layers in lakes and oceans")

F2 When the water or wind carrying sediment slows down, the sediment settles out and piles up in loose layers, each new layer laid on top of the one before; a layer of rock is called a stratum or bed. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sediment ("accumulating as loose, unconsolidated layers"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Stratum ("successive depositional events"; a bed is the basic layer of sedimentary rock)

F3 Loose sediment becomes solid sedimentary rock by compaction and cementation (lithification). Compaction: the weight of the layers above presses the grains together and squeezes out water. Cementation: minerals (calcite, silica, iron oxides) come out of the water in the spaces between grains and glue the grains together. https://grokipedia.com/page/Lithification ("forces grains closer together, expelling interstitial water"; cementation "binds the sediment grains into a durable rock by precipitating minerals from circulating groundwater"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation (layers "pressed down by the weight above them" and "stuck together by minerals"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sediment ("lithification through compaction and cementation to form sedimentary rocks")

F4 Turning sediment into rock is slow: it takes thousands to millions of years. https://grokipedia.com/page/Lithification ("typically unfolds over thousands to millions of years following deposition"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation (millions of years)

F5 Sandstone is sedimentary rock made of sand-sized grains, mostly quartz, pressed and cemented together; the sand gathers in rivers, deltas, beaches and deserts. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sandstone ("a clastic sedimentary rock composed mainly of sand-sized grains"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation ("Sandstone is sand that turned into rock")

F6 Shale is sedimentary rock made of compacted mud (clay and silt) that settled in quiet water such as lakes and the deep sea; it splits into thin, flat layers, and it is the most common sedimentary rock. https://grokipedia.com/page/Shale ("formed by the consolidation and compaction of clay, silt, or mud deposits"; "the tendency to split into thin, parallel layers"; "the most abundant type of sedimentary rock"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation ("Shale is mud that turned into rock")

F7 Limestone is sedimentary rock made mostly of calcite (calcium carbonate); much of it forms from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures such as corals and shellfish (molluscs) piled up in warm, shallow seas, and it is often full of fossil shells and corals. https://grokipedia.com/page/Limestone ("skeletal remains from marine organisms that secrete calcium carbonate structures"; "warm, shallow marine environments"; "fossiliferous layers where preserved shells, corals, or other biogenic remains are visibly embedded"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock ("Most types of limestone are formed from the calcareous skeletons of organisms such as corals, mollusks, and foraminifera"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation ("Limestone is shells and ocean bits that turned into rock")

F8 Sedimentary rock covers about three-quarters of Earth's land surface (73-75 percent), though it is only about 5 percent of the crust by volume. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sedimentary_rock ("only about 5% of the Earth's crustal volume"; "approximately 75% of the land surface"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock ("73% of the Earth's current land surface")

F9 Most fossils are found in sedimentary rock, because it forms at temperatures and pressures that do not destroy the remains of living things. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock ("fossils are most commonly found in sedimentary rock"; "sedimentary rocks form at temperatures and pressures that do not destroy fossil remnants"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation ("Fossils are almost always found in sedimentary rocks"); https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/fossils/rocks-layers.html (USGS: fossils are found almost exclusively in sedimentary rocks; page refuses automated fetches, wording seen in a search excerpt)

F10 Rock that forms from melted rock (magma underground, lava at the surface) almost never holds fossils: the molten rock wipes away the traces of living things. Rare exceptions are remains buried in volcanic ash. Magma is hot, melted rock underground; it is called lava when it erupts at the surface. https://www.discovermagazine.com/why-are-fossils-only-found-in-sedimentary-rocks-44759 ("the molten and partially molten material that make igneous rocks tend to wipe away the traces of ancient organisms"; ash fossils a "remarkably rare occurrence"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Magma ("a hot, molten or semi-molten mixture of liquid rock"; "When this material erupts through the surface, it is termed lava")

F11 A fossil is any preserved remains, impression (print) or trace of a living thing from long ago, usually more than about 10,000 years old. https://grokipedia.com/page/Fossil ("any preserved evidence of past life, including the remains, impressions, or traces of ancient" life; "typically dates to more than 10,000 years ago"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil ("any preserved remains, impression, or trace of any once-living thing from a past geological age"; "over 10,000 years old")

F12 Body fossils are the preserved parts of a living thing, mostly hard parts (bones, teeth, shells). Trace fossils record what a living thing did rather than its body: footprints, trackways, burrows and fossil dung (coprolites). https://grokipedia.com/page/Fossil (body fossils "the preserved physical remains of an organism's morphology"; trace fossils "indirect records of ancient biological activity": "footprints, burrows, borings, coprolites"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Trace_fossil ("a geological record of the biological activity of an ancient organism")

F13 Plants leave fossils too. An impression fossil forms when a plant or animal decays away entirely and leaves only its print in the rock; flat leaves such as ferns are often kept as compressions or impressions between fine layers of mud. https://home.nps.gov/teachers/classrooms/clay-fossils.htm (National Park Service lesson, grades 3-5: impression fossils form "when a plant or animal decays away entirely, only leaving behind an imprint or impression of itself"; compression fossils are "crushed in between fine layers of sediment such as clay"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil (compression fossils "such as those of ferns")

F14 Becoming a fossil is rare. Remains must be buried quickly by sediment, or scavengers and rotting destroy them; quick burial and little oxygen slow the decay, and hard parts (bones, shells, teeth) last much longer than soft parts. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil (organisms are "only rarely preserved as fossils in the best of circumstances"; remains must be covered by sediment soon after death or be destroyed by scavengers or decay); https://grokipedia.com/page/Fossil ("Hard parts like bones, shells, and teeth endure longer than soft tissues"); https://www.bridgew.edu/sites/bridgew/files/media/pdf_document/BSU-It%27s%20Not%20Easy%20to%20be%20a%20Fossil_0.pdf (Bridgewater State lesson: "Very few plants and animals actually become fossils"; "buried relatively quickly after dying")

F15 Many fossils have been turned to stone: minerals carried in groundwater fill or replace the tissue of a buried bone, shell or log. Petrified wood is wood replaced by minerals (mostly silica) that still shows its growth rings and bark. https://grokipedia.com/page/Fossil (permineralization: "mineral-bearing fluids" fill "the pores and cavities of organic tissues"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Petrified_wood ("the original organic material has been gradually replaced by minerals"; logs "retaining annual growth rings"; "retain external bark textures")

F16 Amber is fossilized tree resin. Fresh resin oozing from a tree is sticky and traps small creatures such as insects and spiders; buried over millions of years it hardens into amber that keeps fine detail of what it trapped. Amber is usually yellow, golden, orange or reddish-brown, and often see-through. https://grokipedia.com/page/Amber ("fossilized tree resin"; resin "initially viscous and adhesive"; hardening "over millions of years"; "preserves fine details of entrapped organisms"; "typically from pale yellow or white through golden, orange, and reddish-brown"; "transparent to opaque"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil ("objects preserved in amber"); https://www.msnucleus.org/membership/html/k-6/rc/pdf/rc5pastlife.pdf (Math/Science Nucleus, "Fifth Grade: Past Life" unit, pictures an insect in amber and teaches how fossils are preserved)

F17 Fossils show what a place was like long ago. Fossils of sea creatures found on dry land show that a sea once covered it; rock layers with sea-shell fossils above layers with plant fossils show land that turned to sea; fossils of warm-climate plants in cold lands show a warmer past. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/3-ls4-1-biological-evolution-unity-and-diversity (3-LS4-1: "marine fossils found on dry land", "tropical plant fossils found in Arctic areas"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/4-ess1-1-earths-place-universe (4-ESS1-1: "rock layers with marine shell fossils above rock layers with plant fossils and no shells, indicating a change from land to water over time"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Fossil (fossils reveal "past environmental conditions")

F18 Corals and starfish live only in the sea: starfish are found in no fresh water at all, and corals are sea animals. https://grokipedia.com/page/Starfish ("exclusively marine invertebrates"; "entirely absent from freshwater habitats"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Coral ("Corals are marine invertebrates"; salinities of 27-40 parts per thousand)

F19 Coal is a black or brownish-black sedimentary rock that burns. It formed from the remains of plants that grew in ancient swamps: the plant matter piled up as peat and was turned into coal by burial, heat and pressure over millions of years. Many coal swamps grew in the Carboniferous Period, about 359 to 299 million years ago. https://grokipedia.com/page/Coal ("a combustible black or brownish-black sedimentary rock"; "the compressed remains of ancient vegetation"; "plant matter accumulates in swamps, forms peat"; "under increasing heat, pressure, and burial over millions of years"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation ("Coal mainly comes from ancient plants that grew in huge swamps")

F20 Oil (petroleum) formed mostly from tiny algae and plankton of ancient seas. They died, sank to the sea floor and were buried under mud and sand; a few kilometres down, heat (about 60-120 degrees Celsius) and the pressure of the layers above slowly turned them into oil over millions of years. Natural gas forms alongside oil. https://grokipedia.com/page/Petroleum ("organic remains of ancient planktonic and algal organisms deposited in marine sediments"; gas released "alongside" the oil); https://www.sciencenorway.no/a/2024040 (Reidar Müller, University of Oslo: "oil comes from trillions of tiny algae and plankton"; buried a few kilometres deep at about 60 to 120 degrees); https://grokipedia.com/page/Fossil_fuel ("Petroleum originates predominantly from lipid-rich marine algae and plankton"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation ("Oil and natural gas mainly come from tiny ocean plants and animals (plankton)")

F21 Oil does not come from dinosaurs. Dinosaurs were land animals, and the geologist Reidar Müller calls the "dinosaur soup" idea "pure fantasy". https://www.sciencenorway.no/a/2024040 ("It sounds cool to say that oil is a kind of dinosaur soup, but this is pure fantasy"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation (misconception: "Coal and oil come from dinosaurs"; they mostly come from plants and plankton); https://www.efm.org/learn/does-oil-come-from-dinosaurs (dinosaurs lived on land, oil's source organisms in the sea)

F22 Coal, oil and natural gas are called fossil fuels because they formed from the buried remains of prehistoric plants and other living things, under heat and pressure over millions of years. https://grokipedia.com/page/Fossil_fuel ("formed through the anaerobic decomposition and geological alteration of prehistoric plant and animal remains"; "intense heat and pressure over millions of years")

F23 Words that are not sediment: bedrock is the solid rock that lies under soil and loose sediment; magma is melted rock underground (F10); humus is the dark, rotted plant and animal matter in soil. https://grokipedia.com/page/Bedrock ("the solid, consolidated rock that underlies soil, sediment, and other unconsolidated surface materials"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Humus ("the stable, dark-colored organic fraction of soil formed by the microbial decomposition" of plant and animal residues)

## Misconceptions students of this grade hold

M1 A fossil must be a bone or a shell; footprints, burrows and leaf prints do not count. https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=248 (Ohio State, Beyond Penguins: "Fossils only represent bones and shells of extinct animals"); American Geosciences Institute pre-assessment, https://www.americangeosciences.org/node/12708 (children "are more likely to equate fossils with bones" and some "do not think of trace fossils or impressions as fossils"; page now returns 404, wording seen in a search excerpt)

M2 A fossil is just a piece of a dead animal or plant, unchanged. https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=248 ("Fossils are pieces of dead animals and plants"); answered by F11 (prints and traces count) and F15 (many are turned to stone).

M3 Sedimentary rock forms quickly, or mud simply dries into rock. https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation (misconception: "Sedimentary rocks form quickly"); American Geosciences Institute pre-assessment (children's idea of mud building up around a fossil and drying into rock; search excerpt, see M1); answered by F3 and F4.

M4 Fossils can be found in any rock. https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.10b-sedimentary-rock-formation; answered by F9 and F10.

M5 Oil and coal come from dinosaurs. F21.

M6 Children's lessons often call amber hardened tree "sap" (https://www.bridgew.edu/sites/bridgew/files/media/pdf_document/BSU-It%27s%20Not%20Easy%20to%20be%20a%20Fossil_0.pdf: "trapped inside a trees sap"); the scientific word is resin (F16). So "sap" must never be a wrong choice.

M7 Fossils of warm-climate plants cannot be found in cold places. https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=248 ("Fossils of tropical plants cannot be found in cold or dry areas"); answered by F17.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F24 Erosion is the name of a process, not of a material: the process that detaches soil, rock and sediment and carries it away. Sediment is the material it moves (F1). https://grokipedia.com/page/Erosion ("Erosion is the geological process in which earthen materials such as soil, rock, and sediments are detached" and transported by natural agents)

F25 Granite is a coarse-grained igneous rock that forms when magma crystallizes slowly deep underground; its mineral crystals are big enough to see with the naked eye. Like other rock that forms from melted rock, it almost never holds fossils (F10). https://grokipedia.com/page/Granite ("Granite is a coarse-grained, intrusive igneous rock"; its "phaneritic texture, defined by its coarse-grained nature where individual mineral crystals are visible to the naked eye")

F26 Limestone is made mostly of calcium carbonate, and calcium carbonate is the hard material of seashells and corals. (Coral skeletons and most mollusc shells are aragonite and limestone is mostly calcite, two forms of the same calcium carbonate; bone is not calcium carbonate, so skeletons in general are not said.) https://grokipedia.com/page/Calcium_carbonate (calcium carbonate "forms the bulk of limestone, chalk, marble, and various shells and skeletal structures in marine organisms"; mollusc shells "are primarily composed of calcium carbonate, accounting for approximately 95% of their mass"; coral reefs "are massive accumulations of calcium carbonate skeletons secreted by polyps"; limestone "is chiefly composed of the mineral calcite (calcium carbonate)"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone (aragonite typical of molluscs and corals; cited in the fact-check)

F27 Heat and squeezing deep underground (metamorphism) usually destroy the fossils a rock held: a limestone that becomes marble keeps none of its shell fragments, and metamorphic rocks lack sedimentary traits such as fossils. https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/education/explorations/tours/fossil/5to8/metamorphism.html (UC Museum of Paleontology, grades 5-8: a rock "buried deeply" that undergoes "extreme pressure and heat (become metamorphic)" changes, and "any fossils within are likely to be melted"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Metamorphic_rock (metamorphic rocks lack "sedimentary traits such as fossils, bedding, or rounded clasts"; "marble shows no shell fragments typical of limestone")

F28 Fresh tree resin is a thick, sticky liquid: resins are "solid or highly viscous", and the resin that becomes amber is "initially viscous and adhesive". Resin is not a gum (gums are water-soluble; resins are not), though the words are loosely swapped in everyday speech. https://grokipedia.com/page/Resin (resin is "a class of solid or highly viscous organic substances"; "Amber represents a fossilized form of plant-derived resin"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Amber (resin "initially viscous and adhesive"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resin (resins "should not be confused with gums"; cited in the fact-check)
