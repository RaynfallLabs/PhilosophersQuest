## DNA, not protein, carries the genes

F1. Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty, at the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research, reported in 1944 (Journal of Experimental Medicine) that DNA is the "transforming principle" that carries an inherited trait from one strain of bacteria to another. Enzymes that break down protein, and one that breaks down RNA, left the activity intact; an enzyme that breaks down DNA destroyed it. Many scientists still favoured protein as the genetic material because proteins seemed more varied, and Alfred Mirsky suggested the DNA samples were contaminated with protein, a view he held into the early 1950s. Wikipedia: after Avery "there was still some hesitation within the general scientific community to accept this."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Avery–MacLeod–McCarty_experiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hershey%E2%80%93Chase_experiment

F2. Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase, at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory in 1952, used the T2 bacteriophage, a virus that infects the bacterium E. coli and is made of DNA inside a protein coat. They tagged the DNA of one batch with radioactive phosphorus (phosphorus-32), because phosphorus is in DNA but not in amino acids, and the protein of another batch with radioactive sulfur (sulfur-35), because sulfur is in protein but not in DNA. After infection, a Waring blender sheared the empty coats off the bacteria, and a centrifuge separated the bacteria from the liquid.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hershey–Chase_experiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hershey%E2%80%93Chase_experiment

F3. Hershey and Chase's results: most of the phosphorus (about 80 percent) stayed with the bacteria, "Only 20% of the 32P remained outside the cell" (Wikipedia), and the new viruses carried it; most of the sulfur (about 75-85 percent) stayed outside in the liquid with the coats, and less than 1 percent reached the new viruses. Some sulfur (roughly 15-25 percent) stuck to the cells. They concluded that DNA, not protein, entered the cell and directed the making of new viruses, though they were cautious in print. Hershey shared the 1969 Nobel Prize with Max Delbrück and Salvador Luria; Chase was not included.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hershey–Chase_experiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hershey%E2%80%93Chase_experiment

## The four bases and how they pair

F4. Erwin Chargaff worked at Columbia University. He broke DNA into its bases (acid or enzymes), separated them by paper chromatography and measured them by ultraviolet absorbance; his base-ratio rules had their first explicit publication in 1950 (Journal of Biological Chemistry), after 1949 reports. In DNA from any species, the amount of adenine (A) equals that of thymine (T), and guanine (G) equals cytosine (C); the ratio of A + T to G + C varies between species.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Erwin_Chargaff ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chargaff's_rules ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chargaff%27s_rules

F5. Chargaff's figures, as tabulated by Wikipedia (percent A, G, C, T): human 29.3, 20.7, 20.0, 30.0; yeast 31.3, 18.7, 17.1, 32.9; E. coli 24.7, 26.0, 25.7, 23.6. So human DNA has more A and T than G and C, while E. coli's four bases are close to equal. (Grokipedia's genome figures agree in shape: GC content 41.3 percent in humans, 50.8 percent in E. coli K-12.) Wikipedia: a DNA with A/T and G/C far from one, such as that of the virus phi X 174, "is indicative of single stranded DNA" (not used in a question).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chargaff%27s_rules ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chargaff's_rules

F6. James Watson and Francis Crick's paper "Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids" ran in Nature 171, pages 737-738, dated 25 April 1953, a little over one page. It describes two chains wound into a helix, running in opposite directions, with the phosphate backbones outside and the bases inside; adenine pairs only with thymine and guanine only with cytosine; "if the sequence of bases on one chain is given, then the sequence on the other chain is automatically determined"; and it cites the finding that "the ratio of the amounts of adenine to thymine, and the ratio of guanine to cytosine, are always very close to unity."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_Structure_of_Nucleic_Acids:_A_Structure_for_Deoxyribose_Nucleic_Acid ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/molecular-structure-nucleic-acids-structure-deoxyribose-nucleic-acid-1953-james-watson-and ; https://annex.exploratorium.edu/origins/coldspring/printit.html

F7. The paper's famous sentence near its end: "It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material." The paper did not describe the mechanism; a second paper later in 1953, "Genetical Implications of the Structure of Deoxyribonucleic Acid" (Nature 171: 964-967), took up how DNA might copy itself, with each separated strand serving as a template for a new partner strand.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_Structure_of_Nucleic_Acids:_A_Structure_for_Deoxyribose_Nucleic_Acid ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/molecular-structure-nucleic-acids-structure-deoxyribose-nucleic-acid-1953-james-watson-and ; https://annex.exploratorium.edu/origins/coldspring/printit.html

## Photo 51 and the credit

F8. Photo 51 was taken by Rosalind Franklin and her PhD student Raymond Gosling at King's College London in May 1952 (Wikipedia: 2 May; Grokipedia: captured 6 May after a 62-hour exposure, so the questions say only "May 1952"). It was so tagged because it was the 51st diffraction photograph Gosling had taken. Its X-shaped cross of reflections indicated a helix; Watson recognised the helix because Crick had earlier published what a helix's diffraction pattern looks like.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Photo_51 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photo_51

F9. How Watson saw it: when Franklin was leaving King's for Birkbeck early in 1953, Gosling began reporting to Maurice Wilkins and gave Wilkins the photograph (Gosling: he "took it down the corridor and gave it to him"; Grokipedia says Franklin instructed Gosling to provide it to Wilkins). On Watson's visit to King's on 30 January 1953, Wilkins showed him the photograph "without Franklin's knowledge or consent" (Grokipedia), "without Franklin's permission" (Wikipedia, Rosalind Franklin); "Franklin did not know about this at the time" (Wikipedia, Photo 51). Watson wrote that "my mouth fell open and my pulse began to race." Grokipedia notes that "stealing" is often said, but the photograph came through the laboratory's own channels, without her consent; defenders say informal sharing of data was the norm then.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Photo_51 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photo_51 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalind_Franklin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rosalind_Franklin

F10. A second channel: around mid-February 1953 Max Perutz gave Crick a copy of a December 1952 Medical Research Council report holding many of Franklin's calculations (Wikipedia); Grokipedia says the report, shared via Perutz, shaped the model's base pairing and its strands running in opposite directions.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalind_Franklin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Photo_51 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rosalind_Franklin

F11. Credit as printed: Watson and Crick wrote that they had been "stimulated by a knowledge of the general nature of the unpublished experimental results and ideas of Dr. M. H. F. Wilkins, Dr. R. E. Franklin and their co-workers at King's College, London" (Exploratorium transcription; Wikipedia: a "general knowledge" of their "unpublished" contribution). Franklin and Gosling's own paper, and one by Wilkins, Stokes and Wilson, ran in the same issue of Nature. Franklin's notebooks recorded "Evidence for spiral [meaning helical] structure" and, by February 1953, "Evidence for 2-chain (or 1-chain helix)"; her lecture notes put the phosphate groups near the outside (Wikipedia). She died of ovarian cancer on 16 April 1958, aged 37; the 1962 Nobel Prize went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins, and the prize is not awarded to the dead.
    https://annex.exploratorium.edu/origins/coldspring/printit.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalind_Franklin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rosalind_Franklin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photo_51

## How DNA is copied

F12. Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl, at the California Institute of Technology (Grokipedia: done in 1957, published 1958), grew E. coli in a medium with heavy nitrogen (nitrogen-15), so its DNA was heavy, then moved the bacteria to ordinary light nitrogen (nitrogen-14). A caesium chloride centrifuge sorted the DNA into bands by density. Three ideas were tested: conservative (the parent double helix stays whole, and the copy is wholly new), semi-conservative (the two strands separate and each gets a new partner) and dispersive (old and new material mixed along every strand).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Meselson–Stahl_experiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meselson%E2%80%93Stahl_experiment

F13. Predictions and results: conservative copying would give, after one generation, equal amounts of heavy and light DNA with nothing in between (two bands) (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: "Heavy parental DNA should persist alongside new light DNA"). After one generation they found "a single band of hybrid-density DNA," with no heavy or light band, which ruled out conservative copying; after two generations, equal hybrid and light bands, which fit the semi-conservative model and not the dispersive one (Wikipedia: dispersive "would have resulted in a single density"). The experiment has been called "the most beautiful experiment in biology" (John Cairns, quoted by Horace Judson; Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Meselson–Stahl_experiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meselson%E2%80%93Stahl_experiment

## From gene to protein

F14. Transcription: messenger RNA (mRNA) is built "complementary to the template strand of DNA, with uracil replacing thymine" (Grokipedia), so U pairs with A. OpenStax: "The mRNA product is complementary to the template strand and is almost identical to the other DNA strand"; "in mRNA, all of the T nucleotides are replaced with U nucleotides." Worked example (from these rules): a template strand reading TAC gives the messenger AUG. In a cell with a nucleus, mRNA is made in the nucleus and moves out to the cytoplasm, where ribosomes read it, with transfer RNA bringing the amino acids. Sydney Brenner, François Jacob and Matthew Meselson showed in 1961 that mRNA is a short-lived carrier of the message from DNA to the ribosomes.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Messenger_RNA ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-2-prokaryotic-transcription ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-1-the-genetic-code

F15. Translation reads mRNA as a series of three-base codons, each naming an amino acid or a signal; protein coding begins at the start codon AUG (methionine) and runs to a stop codon. There are 64 codons: 61 for amino acids and 3 stops; most amino acids have more than one. The code is "nearly universal across all domains of life" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Messenger_RNA ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_code ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-1-the-genetic-code

F16. Why threes: with four bases, words two letters long give "only 16 possible two-nucleotide combinations," too few for 20 amino acids, while triplets give 64, "far more than the number of amino acids" (OpenStax; Grokipedia: 4 x 4 x 4 = 64). George Gamow proposed an overlapping "diamond code" in 1954 (Grokipedia), which turned out wrong.
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-1-the-genetic-code ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_code

F17. Francis Crick, Sydney Brenner, Leslie Barnett and Richard Watts-Tobin ("General nature of the genetic code for proteins", Nature, 1961) used proflavin, a chemical that slips between DNA's bases and causes the insertion or deletion of a single base, on a gene (rIIB) of the T4 bacteriophage. Inserting one or two bases disrupted the protein, because the reading frame shifted; inserting three gave a working protein with one extra amino acid (OpenStax); function came back "only when the net addition or deletion was a multiple of three bases" (Grokipedia). This showed the code is read in non-overlapping triplets from a fixed starting point (Wikipedia: "read in non-overlapping triplets"; rescue put the bases "back into the correct reading frame").
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-1-the-genetic-code ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_code ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crick,_Brenner_et_al._experiment

F18. Marshall Nirenberg and the German postdoctoral researcher Heinrich Matthaei, at the National Institutes of Health, made a cell-free extract of broken-open E. coli that could still build protein. In May 1961 (the key run began at 3 a.m. on 27 May) they added poly-U, an artificial RNA made only of uracil (supplied by Leon Heppel and Maxine Singer), to mixtures of all 20 amino acids with one of them radioactive. Only the phenylalanine mixture was stimulated: the extract built polyphenylalanine, "a protein composed entirely of the amino acid phenylalanine," a result "consistent with the triplet codon UUU coding for phenylalanine." The control without poly-U read about 70 counts, against 38,000 counts per milligram of protein with it. Poly-C gave proline and poly-A gave lysine. Nirenberg presented it in Moscow in August 1961; he shared the 1968 Nobel Prize with Robert Holley and Har Gobind Khorana.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nirenberg_and_Matthaei_experiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirenberg_and_Matthaei_experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Genetic_code

## One changed amino acid: sickle-cell haemoglobin

F19. Haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to the tissues, is built in adults from two alpha chains and two beta chains of 146 amino acids each. In sickle-cell haemoglobin (HbS), glutamic acid is replaced by valine at position 6 of the beta chain; the gene's sixth codon reads GTG (valine) instead of GAG (glutamic acid), a single base substitution. When the haemoglobin gives up its oxygen, the valine makes a sticky, water-shunning patch that binds a neighbouring molecule, and the molecules stack into long fibres that bend the red cells into stiff sickle shapes.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hemoglobin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sickle_cell_disease

F20. Vernon Ingram, at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, reported "A Specific Chemical Difference between the Globins of Normal Human and Sickle-cell Anaemia Haemoglobin" (Nature, 1956) and "Gene Mutations in Human Haemoglobin" (Nature, 1957). Using the enzyme trypsin he cut the protein into peptide fragments and compared their chromatographic and electrophoretic properties (his "finger printing"), and found that a single peptide differed between the two; with John Hunt and Antony Stretton he determined that the change was glutamic acid replaced by valine at position 6 of the beta chain. He was the first to show that a single amino acid exchange in a protein can cause a disease. (Grokipedia: peptide fingerprinting in 1956-1957 pinpointed "a single amino acid substitution.") Linus Pauling's group had shown in 1949, by electrophoresis, that sickle-cell haemoglobin is an abnormal variant, "the first recognized molecular disease."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernon_Ingram ; https://www1.udel.edu/chem/white/C342/IngramBkgd07.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sickle_cell_disease

## Tools: PCR, the gel and the DNA fingerprint

F21. Kary Mullis invented the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in 1983 at the Cetus Corporation in California. In each cycle, heat separates the two strands of the double helix; each separated strand then guides an enzyme to build a matching partner strand, so the target DNA roughly doubles each cycle (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: "the two strands of the DNA double helix are physically separated," then "The two DNA strands then become templates for DNA polymerase," and "At every cycle, the amount of product is doubled." Early runs used an enzyme that heat destroyed, so fresh enzyme had to be added after every heating step; Taq polymerase, from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium of hot springs (Wikipedia: Yellowstone's Mushroom Spring), survives the heat and ended that. Mullis shared the 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Michael Smith. (Classroom concept only: no temperatures, primers or protocol are used.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Polymerase_chain_reaction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kary_Mullis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymerase_chain_reaction

F22. Gel electrophoresis: DNA carries a negative charge along its phosphate backbone, so an electric current moves it toward the positive electrode. In free solution its migration "is independent of molecular weight," so "there is no separation by size without a gel matrix" (Wikipedia); the gel "sieves the DNA by the size of the DNA molecule whereby smaller molecules travel faster" (Wikipedia), and nucleic acids sort by size "due to the uniform negative charge density" (Grokipedia). A ladder of fragments of known length is run beside the samples to read sizes, and a stain makes the bands visible.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gel_electrophoresis_of_nucleic_acids ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gel_electrophoresis_of_nucleic_acids

F23. Alec Jeffreys, at the University of Leicester, had his "eureka moment" at about 9:05 a.m. on 10 September 1984, looking at an X-ray film (an autoradiograph) of DNA: repeated stretches called minisatellites gave patterns that differed from person to person, "capable of uniquely identifying individuals" (Grokipedia). (Sources differ on whose DNA was on that film, two technicians or a technician's family, so that detail is not used.) In 1985 the method showed that a boy whose family came from Ghana was closely related to his family in Britain, settling an immigration case; in 1987-1988 it cleared Richard Buckland and convicted Colin Pitchfork (not used: the crimes are not suited to the game). He was knighted in 1994.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alec_Jeffreys ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alec_Jeffreys

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- Genes are made of protein, or protein and DNA both carry the instructions (F1-F3: the historical expectation; Hershey and Chase's sulfur stayed outside).
- The two strands of DNA are identical copies of each other (F6: they are complementary; each fixes the other).
- A pairs with G, or like with like (F4-F6: the counts match A with T and G with C).
- Watson stole Photo 51 from Franklin's desk, or Franklin gave it to him (F9: Wilkins showed it to him, without her knowledge).
- The Nobel committee snubbed Franklin (F11: she had died; the prize is not given to the dead).
- Copying keeps the old helix whole and builds a whole new one beside it (F12-F13: ruled out by Meselson and Stahl).
- Messenger RNA is an identical copy of the DNA strand it is read from, or the same strand with U for T (F14: it is complementary to that strand).
- The code could be read one or two bases at a time, or in overlapping words (F16-F17).
- A mutation always changes the whole protein, or always deletes or adds pieces (F19-F20: one substitution changed one amino acid).
- PCR's heat is there to kill germs, or to cut the DNA (F21: it parts the strands).
- Short DNA moves farther in a gel because it carries more charge for its size, or because the same pull moves a lighter thing faster (F22: charge per length is uniform; in plain liquid all sizes move alike; the gel's mesh does the sorting).
