# Inertia: Newton's First Law: fact sheet (T3, 7th grade, physical strand)

Researched 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; Wikipedia, NASA (Glenn Research Center, Goddard), OpenStax, the IIHS, and university lecture-demonstration pages (UVA, UCSB, UMD, Iowa, Wisconsin, Illinois) to corroborate. Curriculum line G7.P5 (TEKS 7.7D, NGSS MS-PS2-2).

Neighbours, never re-asked here: T1 `pushes-pulls-and-gravity` (friction stops a kicked ball; rough carpet stops a toy car sooner; balanced pushes on a crate), T2 `forces-and-net-force` (balanced forces at steady speed; the normal force; the skydiver), T2 `action-and-reaction` (third-law pairs; a rocket firing in empty space). T4 `newtons-laws-force-mass-and-acceleration` owns F = ma and the same push on a light and a heavy cart.

Note on a source: Grokipedia's Galileo page calls impetus "Aristotle's impetus theory". That is wrong (impetus was John Philoponus's and Jean Buridan's answer to Aristotle, F7). Grokipedia's Gassendi page links his ship-mast drops to free fall; nothing here relies on either sentence.

## Facts

F1 Newton's first law: an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at a constant speed in a straight line, unless an unbalanced force acts on it. https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/newtons-laws-of-motion/ ("An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion remains in motion at constant speed and in a straight line unless acted on by an unbalanced force."); https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia ("A body at rest tends to remain at rest. A body in motion tends to remain in motion at a constant velocity unless acted on by a net external force."); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion ("Every body continues in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed upon it.")

F2 Isaac Newton set out his laws of motion in his book the Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica, published in 1687; in Andrew Motte's English, Law I reads "Every body perseveres in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a right line". The first law is often called the law of inertia. https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion ("articulated by Isaac Newton in his Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687)"; "every body perseveres in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a right line"); https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia ("Newton incorporated the concept of inertia into his first law, which is often referred to as the law of inertia."); https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/rocket/TRCRocket/rocket_principles.html ("The science of rocketry began with the publishing of a book in 1687 by the great English scientist Sir Isaac Newton." NASA Glenn's beginners' guide says he "presented" the laws in 1686, the year the manuscript went to the Royal Society; the book appeared in 1687.)

F3 Inertia is an object's resistance to any change in its state of motion: to being started, stopped, sped up, slowed or turned. It is a property of matter, not a force. https://grokipedia.com/page/Inertia ("Inertia is the intrinsic property of matter that causes an object to resist changes to its state of motion"); https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/newtons-laws-of-motion/ ("This tendency to resist changes in a state of motion is inertia."); https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia ("Inertia is the tendency for an object at rest to remain at rest", and "for a moving object to remain in motion in a straight line with constant speed")

F4 More mass means more inertia: an object's inertia is proportional to its mass, the amount of matter in it. https://grokipedia.com/page/Inertia ("greater mass implies stronger inertia"; in linear motion "inertia is directly proportional to mass"); https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia ("the inertia of an object is proportional to the mass of the object"; "Mass is a measure of the amount of matter (or stuff) in an object.")

F5 A floating, "weightless" object in orbit keeps all its mass and so all its inertia: an astronaut who tries to push a massive object finds it hard to set moving even though a scale would read nothing for it. Mass does not depend on gravity; weight does. https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/stargaze/ILmass.htm (NASA Goddard, From Stargazers to Starships: "An astronaut in a space suit, in the space shuttle bay, tries to push a one-ton scientific satellite out of the bay, but the satellite proves very hard to move."; "it has no weight, but it has one ton of mass"; "Mass (or inertia) resists acceleration"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_versus_weight (a satellite in orbit is "weightless" yet "still retains its mass and inertia"); https://www.space.com/space-exploration/human-spaceflight/how-do-astronauts-weigh-themselves-in-space ("Your mass is the amount of material your body contains ... regardless of gravity"; the space station's crews measure their mass with spring devices, NASA's Space Linear Acceleration Mass Measurement Device and Russia's Body Mass Measurement Device, because a scale is useless there)

F6 Aristotle divided motion into natural and violent; a violent motion (a thrown stone or javelin) needed a mover acting on it the whole time, and he held that the air around a javelin kept pushing it on. On his view things on Earth come to rest unless a force keeps them moving. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion ("Aristotle divided motion into two types: 'natural' and 'violent'"; "a 'violent' motion requires an immediate cause"; "Aristotle concluded that the air around the javelin must be imparted with the ability to move the javelin forward"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Inertia (violent motion "required the continuous application of an external force"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia ("all moving objects (on Earth) eventually come to rest unless an external power (force) continued to move them"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion (violent motion "requires a continuous applied force to sustain it"; it "ceases immediately upon removal of the agent")

F7 Impetus theory, the medieval answer to Aristotle (John Philoponus, then Jean Buridan): the thrower puts an "impetus" into the body, and the body keeps moving while its impetus lasts. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion (Philoponus "found this absurd: the same medium, air, was somehow responsible both for sustaining motion and for impeding it"; "As long as its impetus was sustained, the body would continue to move."); https://grokipedia.com/page/Inertia (Buridan: "a projectile receives an internal 'impetus' from the initial mover"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia (Buridan rejected the idea that impetus "dissipated spontaneously")

F8 Galileo Galilei (1564-1642) slowed falling motion down by rolling bronze balls along grooves cut in wooden ramps, timing them with a water clock. https://grokipedia.com/page/Galileo_Galilei ("Galileo Galilei (15 February 1564 - 8 January 1642)"; "He rolled bronze balls down grooves cut in wooden ramps, timing their descent with a water clock"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Two_New_Sciences ("By rolling balls down inclined planes and timing their motion with a water clock"); https://ux1.eiu.edu/~addavis/3050/Ch02Inertia/InclinedPlanes.html ("Galileo slowed down motion by rolling objects along inclined planes.")

F9 Galileo's two ramps: a ball that rolls down one ramp and up a second one climbs almost back to the height it started from, whatever the slope of the second ramp; real balls fall a little short because of friction and the air. https://galileoandeinstein.phys.virginia.edu/lectures/gal_accn96.htm (Michael Fowler, UVA: "the ball will roll up the second ramp to a level essentially equal to the level it started at", rolling back and forth and "eventually coming to rest because of friction, air resistance, etc."); https://grokipedia.com/page/Two_New_Sciences (the thought experiment "of a ball rolling down one inclined plane and ascending another", reaching the same height without friction or air resistance)

F10 Galileo's conclusion: if the second ramp were made level and frictionless, the ball could never climb back to its height, so it would roll on for ever at a steady speed. A moving body needs no force to keep moving. https://grokipedia.com/page/Two_New_Sciences (with the second plane horizontal, "the ball would continue in uniform rectilinear motion indefinitely without external impetus"); https://galileoandeinstein.phys.virginia.edu/tns244.htm (Two New Sciences, Fourth Day, Crew and de Salvio translation: "Imagine any particle projected along a horizontal plane without friction; then we know ... that this particle will move along this same plane with a motion which is uniform and perpetual, provided the plane has no limits."); https://www.tau.ac.il/education/muse/museum/galileo/the_law_of_inertia.html (on a level plane with no friction a ball keeps its speed); https://www.pearson.com/channels/physics/study-guides/foundations-of-motion-aristotle-galileo-and-newton ("A moving object does not require a force to keep moving if friction is absent.")

F11 Galileo is credited with first describing inertia; the general law was stated by Galileo's pupils and by Descartes, and Newton stated it clearly in 1687. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia ("This key property of objects was first described by Galileo."); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion ("The modern concept of inertia is credited to Galileo."; Descartes "introduced the concept of inertia by way of his 'laws of nature'"); https://www.tau.ac.il/education/muse/museum/galileo/the_law_of_inertia.html (the general form "devised by Galileo's pupils and by Descartes"; Newton "formulated clearly" the law in 1687)

F12 Everyday moving things slow down and stop because friction and the air push against their motion; a rougher surface gives more friction (a ball on grass stops sooner than one on smooth ice). https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia ("Think of friction as a resistance to motion that slows things down."; with an air-hockey table's air off, "the puck slides only a short distance before friction slows it to a stop"); T1 neighbour `pushes-pulls-and-gravity` (friction is greater on rough surfaces; the carpet and the toy car)

F13 Johannes Kepler coined the word inertia, in his Epitome Astronomiae Copernicanae (1617-1621), from the Latin iners, idle or sluggish. He meant only resistance to being moved, taking rest as natural; Galileo and Newton joined rest and motion in one principle, so that inertia also keeps a moving body moving. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia ("The term 'inertia' was first introduced by Johannes Kepler"; it "comes from the Latin word iners, meaning idle or sluggish"; Kepler "defined inertia only in terms of resistance to movement", on "the presumption that rest was a natural state"; Galileo and Newton "unified rest and motion in one principle")

F14 When a car stops abruptly, its passengers continue forward because of their inertia; unrestrained, they strike the dashboard or windshield. A seat belt holds the body to the car and spreads the stopping force over the strong bones of the pelvis and shoulder. https://grokipedia.com/page/Inertia (when a car "abruptly stops", passengers "continue forward due to their inertia", which can injure them unless restrained); https://grokipedia.com/page/Seat_belt (seat-belt design had to counter "Newton's first law (inertia)"; with no upper-body belt "the unrestrained torso pitched forward violently", with "head impacts with dashboards or windshields"; a belt "distributes deceleration forces across the stronger bony structures of the pelvis and shoulder")

F15 In a rear-end crash the seat shoves the occupant's torso forward while the unsupported head lags behind; a head restraint keeps head and torso moving together. https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/neck-injury-risk-is-lower-if-seats-and-head-restraints-are-rated-good ("its seats accelerate occupants' torsos forward. Unsupported, an occupant's head will lag behind this forward torso movement"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Head_restraint ("the occupant's torso is propelled forward by the seat while the head lags behind")

F16 In a car going round a bend, things and people seem flung toward the outside only because they go on moving in a straight line while the car turns; the outward "centrifugal force" is not a real force. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion (the feeling of "sliding (or being flung ...) away from the center of the turn"; "it only happens because your body wants to continue moving in a straight line"; "Centrifugal force is not a real force"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Centripetal_force ("in inertial frames, no such outward force exists")

F17 A ball whirled on a string is held in its circle by the string's pull; when the string breaks, nothing pulls it toward the centre, and it moves off in a straight line along the circle's edge (a tangent), the way it was heading at that instant, at the same speed. https://www.pearson.com/channels/physics/asset/27068181/a-ball-is-being-whirled-in-a-horizontal-circl ("Since the centripetal force is gone, the ball will move in a straight line tangent to the circle"; "At the same speed as before, in a straight line tangent to the circle"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centripetal_force (with a rope, "the centripetal force on the object is supplied by the tension of the rope"; "objects naturally follow a straight path (due to inertia)")

F18 The tablecloth trick: a smooth cloth yanked out quickly leaves the dishes almost where they were, because friction acts on them for so short a time that it cannot speed them up much and their inertia keeps them in place; a slow pull lets friction drag them along with the cloth. A smooth cloth (silk, rayon) keeps the friction low. https://web.physics.ucsb.edu/~lecturedemonstrations/Composer/Pages/12.12.html ("Your ability to do this arises from the inertia of the objects"; "friction cannot provide sufficient force to accelerate the objects appreciably during the short time it takes for you to pull the tablecloth away"); https://wonders.physics.wisc.edu/beaker-and-cloth/ ("If the tablecloth is pulled slowly, friction (rubbing) between the beaker and tablecloth" drags the beaker along; pulled quickly, it does not move); https://lecdem.physics.umd.edu/highlight-tablecloth1.html (a smooth silk or rayon cloth has low friction; a wrinkle can catch the dishes); https://labdemos.physics.sunysb.edu/c.-kinematics-and-dynamics/c3.-first-law-of-motion/inertia-tablecloth-trick.php ("To dramatically demonstrate inertia of rest")

F19 A ball tossed up or dropped inside a train moving steadily on a straight track keeps the train's forward speed, so it comes straight back down in front of the person, as it would at rest; no push is needed to keep it moving forward. Galileo made the same argument in his Dialogue (Florence, 1632): a cannonball dropped from the top of a smoothly moving ship's mast falls at the foot of the mast, not behind it. https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/12794 (University of Illinois, Ask the Van, on a ball dropped in a bullet train: "Absolutely not. All motion is relative. There is no reason at all for the ball to change its forward velocity"; "No 'push' is needed to maintain motion"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo%27s_ship ("dropping a rock from the mast of a smoothly moving ship and observing whether the rock hit at the base of the mast or behind it"; "a cannonball dropped from the top of the mast would fall directly to the foot"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Dialogue_Concerning_the_Two_Chief_World_Systems ("a dropped cannonball lands at the base of the mast"; printed in Florence in February 1632)

F20 A loose hammer head is tightened by holding the hammer upright and striking the bottom of the handle sharply on a table or floor: the handle stops short and the head, resisting the sudden stop, moves on down the handle. Pounding the head down onto something instead works it off the handle. https://lecdem.physics.umd.edu/c/c3/2436-c3-13.html (UMD demonstration "INERTIA: HAMMERHEAD": "When the hammer is held upright and the handle struck sharply on the table, the head can be seen to move downwards along the handle, resisting the abrupt deceleration of striking the table."); https://instructional-resources.physics.uiowa.edu/1f2060-tighten-hammer-head-knife-potato (University of Iowa: "you don't tighten a hammer head by hitting it onto the handle," but by "slamming the other end of the handle onto the table"; pounding the head "will actually move off the handle")

F21 A spacecraft far from any large source of gravity has its forces in balance and travels in a straight line at a steady speed; it needs a force (a rocket firing, a planet's gravity, drag) only to change its speed or direction. https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/rocket/TRCRocket/rocket_principles.html ("A spacecraft moving through the solar system is in constant motion. The spacecraft will travel in a straight line if the forces on it are in balance."; near a planet the planet's gravity bends its path; an orbit lasts until an unbalanced force such as drag or a rocket firing slows it)

F22 Heavy and light objects fall with the same acceleration when air resistance is small (Galileo). https://grokipedia.com/page/Galileo_Galilei ("no significant difference in acceleration between heavier and lighter objects when air resistance was minimized"); T1/T2 neighbours on gravity

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1 Moving things slow down and stop by themselves, or once nothing pushes them (Aristotle's view, F6). https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia (teacher note: "Students may believe that objects that are in motion tend to slow down and stop.")

M2 A thrown thing carries a stored force from the throw that keeps it going and wears off (impetus, F7); and a thing released from a curved path keeps curving for a while (curvilinear impetus). Michael McCloskey, Alfonso Caramazza and Bert Green, "Curvilinear motion in the absence of external forces: naive beliefs about the motion of objects", Science 210 (1980), 1139-1141: many college students said a ball shot out of a spiral tube would keep curving. https://grokipedia.com/page/Naïve_physics (McCloskey's studies of "a ball released from a curved tube or a cannonball fired horizontally"; adults expected curved paths or a slowing "due to a diminishing 'impetus' force imparted at launch")

M3 Floating, "weightless" things in orbit have no inertia and move at a touch (F5 corrects it). https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/stargaze/ILmass.htm ("If it is weightless, why should it be so?")

M4 An outward force flings passengers toward the outside of a bend (F16 corrects it).

M5 A ball dropped or tossed in a moving train falls behind, since the train moves on while it is in the air (the questioner's belief in F19's Illinois answer; the Aristotelian expectation for the ship's mast).

M6 Inertia is a force that holds a thing still or pushes a moving thing along (F3: it is a property, not a force); or, from the word's Latin, a tendency to come to rest (Kepler's sense, F13).

M7 Heavier things fall faster (F22).

## Not sourced, so not used
- The claim that a flatter second ramp makes the ball travel farther is geometry (the same height reached up a gentler slope), not a quoted source; it appears only as reasoning in a context, never as a key.
- Pierre Gassendi's 1640 ship-mast drops at Marseille: Grokipedia's account is confused (F-note above); left out.
