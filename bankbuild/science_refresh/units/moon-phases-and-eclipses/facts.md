## Facts

F1. The Sun always lights one half of the Moon; at any moment one hemisphere is sunlit and the other is in night. Grokipedia: "The Moon is always half-illuminated by the Sun". NASA: "The Moon is always half-lit by the Sun." https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_phase ; https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/

F2. The phases come from how much of that sunlit half can be seen from Earth, which changes as the Moon travels round its orbit. NASA: "how much we are able to see of that illuminated half changes as the Moon travels through its orbit". https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_phase ; https://www.nsta.org/phases-moon

F3. The phases are NOT caused by Earth's shadow falling on the Moon; Earth's shadow reaches the Moon only during a lunar eclipse. Wikipedia: "The phases are not caused by the Earth's shadow falling on the Moon, as some people believe." Grokipedia: "Earth's shadow only affects the Moon during a lunar eclipse". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_phase

F4. The eight phases in order: new moon, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full moon, waning gibbous, third quarter, waning crescent, then new moon again. https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_phase

F5. Waxing means the lit part is growing (thickening), from new moon to full; waning means it is shrinking (thinning), from full to new. A gibbous Moon is more than half lit ("bulging outwards"); a crescent is a sliver, less than half. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_phase

F6. The whole cycle of phases takes about 29.5 days (the synodic month, 29.53 days); each step between the main phases is about a quarter of that, 7.38 days. https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Orbit_of_the_Moon

F7. At first quarter and third quarter, half of the Moon's disc is lit; "quarter" refers to how far the Moon has gone through its cycle round Earth, not to its shape. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_phase

F8. At new moon the Moon lies between Earth and the Sun, with its sunlit half facing away from Earth; it rises and sets with the Sun and cannot be seen against the daytime sky. Grokipedia: "the Moon lies directly between the Earth and the Sun"; "It rises and sets with the Sun, making it unobservable against the daytime sky." https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_phase ; https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/

F9. At full moon the Moon is on the opposite side of Earth from the Sun, so its whole sunlit half faces Earth; a full moon rises at sunset and sets at sunrise, and is up all night. NASA: "A full Moon rises at sunset and sets at sunrise." https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_phase ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase

F10. The Moon spins on its axis exactly once each time it goes round Earth (about 27.3 days), so the same side always faces Earth. It is "tidally locked". NASA: "it spins on its axis exactly once each time it orbits our planet." https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tidal_locking ; https://www.timeanddate.com/astronomy/moon/far-side.html

F11. MISCONCEPTION: that the Moon does not spin at all. Grokipedia notes the locked Moon does rotate, "countering the common misconception that the Moon does not rotate". A Moon that did not spin would show every side to Earth over one trip round. https://grokipedia.com/page/Tidal_locking

F12. The far side of the Moon is not dark: it gets as much sunlight as the near side. At new moon the near side is dark and the far side fully lit; at full moon the reverse. MISCONCEPTION: the "dark side of the Moon". timeanddate: "At New Moon, the side we see from Earth is in darkness, while the far side of the Moon is fully lit up by the Sun's rays." NASA SVS: "During a full Moon, the near side is in full sunlight while the far side is in full shadow – and vice versa during a new Moon." https://www.timeanddate.com/astronomy/moon/far-side.html ; https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/14992 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Far_side_of_the_Moon

F13. The Moon takes about 27.3 days to go once round Earth, not one day; the daily rising and setting of the Sun, Moon and stars comes from Earth's spin. NASA: orbit 27.322 days. Grokipedia: "Diurnal motion arises from Earth's rotation on its axis". https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Diurnal_motion

F14. A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth, and only at new moon. Grokipedia: "Solar eclipses can only happen during the new moon phase". NASA: "Solar eclipses only occur during the new moon, when the Moon and Sun are aligned on the same side of Earth". https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_eclipse ; https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/geometry

F15. A lunar eclipse happens when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, and only at full moon. Earth's shadow points away from the Sun, toward where the full moon is. NSTA: "the Earth's shadow is cast toward the Moon, which is why lunar eclipses can happen during full Moons." https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_eclipse ; https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/types/ ; https://www.nsta.org/phases-moon

F16. Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5 degrees (5.145) to the plane of Earth's orbit round the Sun. NASA: "During the new moon, the Moon usually passes below or above the Sun, and its shadow misses Earth." Grokipedia: "not every full moon results in an eclipse due to the Moon's orbit being tilted by about 5 degrees." Eclipse seasons come only twice a year. https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/geometry ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Orbit_of_the_Moon ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_eclipse

F17. Solar eclipses of some kind happen between two and five times a year. https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_eclipse

F18. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and also about 400 times farther away, so the two look about the same size in the sky, and the Moon can just cover the Sun's face. NASA: "Even though the Sun is about 400 times bigger than the Moon, it is also about 400 times farther away." https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/geometry ; https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/total-solar-eclipse/en/

F19. When the Moon is farther from Earth than usual it looks a little smaller than the Sun and cannot cover it all, leaving a ring of Sun: an annular eclipse, which is still a solar eclipse. Partial eclipses also count. https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/types/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_eclipse

F20. The Moon's darkest shadow (the umbra) narrows on its way to Earth: the path of a total solar eclipse is only about 100 to 270 km wide (NASA: a typical path "may be less than 50 miles wide"), far narrower than the Moon itself (about 3,475 km across). Totality lasts about 2 to 7 minutes, at most about 7.5. https://grokipedia.com/page/Umbra,_penumbra_and_antumbra ; https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/geometry ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Moon

F21. In a total lunar eclipse the Moon does not vanish but turns a coppery red ("blood moon"): sunlight passing through Earth's atmosphere is bent (refracted) into the shadow toward the Moon, and the air scatters away the blue light and lets the red through, the same effect that makes sunsets red. Grokipedia: "Sunlight grazing the Earth's limb is bent toward the Moon by atmospheric refraction." NASA: "the same phenomenon that makes our sky blue and our sunsets red." https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_eclipse ; https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/skywatching/what-you-need-to-know-about-the-march-2025-total-lunar-eclipse/

F22. Earthshine is sunlight reflected from Earth's surface and clouds that faintly lights the Moon's dark part, seen best a few days from new moon. Earth's phases seen from the Moon are the opposite of the Moon's: at full moon the Moon faces Earth's night side ("new Earth"), so Earth's oceans send it almost no sunlight then. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthlight_(astronomy) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_phase ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_phase

F23. A shadow is a dark area where light from a source is blocked by an object; it gives off no light of its own. Wikipedia: "A shadow is a dark area on a surface where light from a light source is blocked by an object." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow

F24. Earth's air does not magnify the Moon: the Moon looking bigger near the horizon is an illusion of perception, and its measured size stays about half a degree across. Grokipedia: refraction "does not increase the overall angular size". https://grokipedia.com/page/Moon_illusion

F25. The Moon's orbit is an ellipse, so its distance from Earth changes; a full moon at the closest point (a supermoon) happens only a few times a year, so most full moons are not at the Moon's closest point. Grokipedia: supermoons "occur three to four times per year" (new and full together); perigee and the phases "do not align perfectly each month". https://grokipedia.com/page/Supermoon

F26. The Moon makes no light of its own; moonlight is sunlight reflected off its rocks (T1 fact, used here only in context and as a lure). Grokipedia Moon: "a solid, Earth-like rock reflecting sunlight". https://grokipedia.com/page/Moon

F27. A lunar eclipse can be seen from anywhere on Earth's night side and is safe to watch with bare eyes; a solar eclipse is not safe to watch without eclipse glasses, except during totality. https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_eclipse ; https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/types/

F28. The Moon is on average about 384,400 km from Earth; the Sun is about 400 times farther, so the Moon is always much nearer to Earth than the Sun is and never passes behind the Sun. https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/ ; https://science.nasa.gov/eclipses/geometry

## Misconceptions a 6th grader holds (lures)

M1. Earth's shadow causes the phases (F3). NSTA: "Middle-level students most commonly suggest that the phases of the moon are caused by the Earth's shadow" (Driver et al.). https://www.nsta.org/phases-moon
M2. Other planets' shadows, clouds, or the Sun cause the phases (NSTA, citing Dunford and Jones 2019). https://www.nsta.org/phases-moon
M3. The Sun lights only the part of the Moon people see, so the lit amount itself changes (F1 corrects it).
M4. The far side is the "dark side", always unlit (F12).
M5. The Moon does not spin (F11).
M6. The Moon goes round Earth once a day, because it rises and sets daily (F13).
M7. The Moon glows by itself (F26); a shadow is a thing that can give off light (F23).
M8. Earth's air magnifies the Moon (F24).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26; read on the web that day)

F29. The planets' light is far too faint to light the eclipsed Moon: Venus at its brightest is about magnitude -4.6 and the full Moon about -12.6, eight magnitudes apart, and five magnitudes is a factor of 100 in brightness, so Venus is more than a thousand times fainter. The red light on the eclipsed Moon is sunlight bent through Earth's air (F21).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Apparent_magnitude ("Venus at its brightest around -4.6"; the full Moon "reaching an apparent magnitude of -12.6"; "a difference of 5 magnitudes represents a factor of 100 in brightness ratio")

F30. The path of a total solar eclipse is typically 100-160 km wide and at most about 267 km in the most favourable case (Wikipedia); Grokipedia gives a typical 100 to 270 km; NASA says a typical path "may be less than 50 miles wide". So "rarely more than a few hundred kilometres wide" is the safe wording, not a hard range.
