## Galaxies and their shapes

F1. A galaxy is "a gravitationally bound system consisting of stars, interstellar gas, dust, dark matter, and other components" (Grokipedia); "Typical galaxies contain hundreds of billions of stars". NASA: "Galaxies consist of stars, planets, and vast clouds of gas and dust, all bound together by gravity"; the smallest hold a few thousand stars, the largest trillions.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Galaxy ; https://science.nasa.gov/universe/galaxies/

F2. Galaxies are "primarily classified by their morphology into three main categories: spiral, elliptical, and irregular" (lenticulars are a fourth). The system was "developed by Edwin Hubble in 1926" and drawn as his "tuning fork" diagram in The Realm of the Nebulae (1936). NASA: galaxies come "mostly spirals and ellipticals", with "less orderly" ones "dubbed irregular".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Galaxy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Edwin_Hubble ; https://science.nasa.gov/universe/galaxies/

F3. A spiral galaxy has "a rotating disk of stars, interstellar gas, dust, and dark matter, from which prominent spiral arms extend outward", around "a dense central bulge composed primarily of older stars"; young stars are "primarily concentrated along the spiral arms", where star formation is "significantly enhanced". About two-thirds of spirals are barred.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spiral_galaxy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Galaxy

F4. An elliptical galaxy has a "smooth, ellipsoidal" shape, "from nearly spherical to highly elongated", with no spiral arms or prominent disk; it is dominated by "older, redder stellar populations", and ellipticals "typically contain little interstellar gas or dust, resulting in minimal ongoing star formation" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: "very little interstellar matter (neither gas nor dust)"; "Star formation activity in elliptical galaxies is typically minimal". They are thought to form mostly by mergers; "equal-mass (1:1) mergers of spirals produce smooth ellipticals", and starbursts in mergers can expel "up to 50% of the interstellar gas".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Elliptical_galaxy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_galaxy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Galaxy_merger

F5. Irregular galaxies "lack a regular, symmetric structure"; most are small dwarfs, many shaped by "gravitational interactions, mergers, or tidal disruptions", and they hold "significant amounts of interstellar gas and dust, which support high rates of star formation". The Large and Small Magellanic Clouds are "irregular satellites of the Milky Way", about 160,000 and 200,000 light-years away.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Irregular_galaxy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Galaxy

F6. Stars form by "the gravitational collapse of dense, cold regions within giant molecular clouds" of gas and dust; warmer gas has more thermal pressure and resists collapse (sourced by the stars unit, F1-F2 of the-life-cycles-of-stars). Inside a galaxy, "the average distance between stars vastly exceeds their sizes", so stars in merging galaxies almost never collide.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Star_formation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Molecular_cloud ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Galaxy_merger

## The Milky Way and the Sun's place in it

F7. "The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy containing Earth and the Solar System"; it "spans approximately 100,000 light-years in diameter" (Grokipedia; Wikipedia gives a D25 diameter of about 87,400 light-years; NASA "more than 100,000 light-years") and holds "100-400 billion stars" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia gives 100 billion in its introduction, 100 to 400 billion in its body).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Milky_Way ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way ; https://science.nasa.gov/universe/galaxies/

F8. "The Sun is located approximately 26,000 light-years from the galactic center in the Orion Arm (also known as the Local Arm)", a minor arm or spur (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: 25,000-28,000 light-years, on the "inner edge of the Orion Arm"; NASA: "Earth is located along one of the galaxy's spiral arms, about halfway from the center". The Sun takes about 230 to 250 million years to go round the centre once (NASA: "about 240 million years").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Milky_Way ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way ; https://science.nasa.gov/universe/galaxies/

F9. The Milky Way's pale band across the night sky is "the edge-on view of the galaxy's central disk" (Grokipedia); looking into the plane of the flattened disk, many more stars are seen (Wikipedia). "In 1610, Galileo Galilei used his telescope to resolve the Milky Way's hazy band into countless individual stars."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Milky_Way ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way

F10. Harlow Shapley measured the distances of 69 globular clusters (the 1918 paper "The Distances, Distribution in Space, and Dimensions of 69 Globular Clusters"), using the Cepheid and RR Lyrae variables in them. The clusters are concentrated "toward the constellation Sagittarius" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: their "strongly asymmetrical distribution"; assuming the clusters are spread roughly evenly around the galaxy's centre, "He correctly concluded that the Milky Way's center is in the constellation Sagittarius and not near the Solar System". A globular cluster is "a spheroidal conglomeration of stars that is bound together by gravity", "tens of thousands to many millions" of stars, most of them in the halo around the galaxy's core. Jacobus Kapteyn's earlier model "placed the Sun near the core" of a much smaller system (about 30,000 to 40,000 light-years across). Shapley overestimated the size, putting the Milky Way at over 300,000 light-years across.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Harlow_Shapley ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globular_cluster ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Milky_Way

## The light-year

F11. A light-year is "the distance that electromagnetic radiation travels in a vacuum during one Julian year", about 9.46 trillion kilometres (5.88 trillion miles); "a light-year measures distance rather than time" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: "Despite its inclusion of the word 'year', the term is a unit of distance, not a unit of time." Light crosses the Sun-Earth gap in about 8.3 minutes.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Light-year ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-year

F12. Proxima Centauri, the closest known star to the Sun, "lies at 4.24 light-years away". Because light takes time to travel, a distant object is seen as it was when its light left: "the greater the distance, the farther back in time one looks".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Light-year

## Henrietta Leavitt and the Cepheids

F13. Henrietta Swan Leavitt joined the staff of the Harvard College Observatory in 1902 under Edward Pickering, one of his women "computers", at 30 cents an hour (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: "$0.30" an hour, $10.50 a week), to "measure and catalog the brightness of stars" on the observatory's glass photographic plates. Her plates of the Magellanic Clouds came from Harvard's Bruce Astrograph at Arequipa, Peru (Wikipedia). Her 1908 catalogue listed 1,777 variable stars in the Magellanic Clouds and noted "the brighter variables having longer periods".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt

F14. A Cepheid variable is "a type of pulsating variable star that undergoes regular radial expansions and contractions, causing periodic variations in its brightness, diameter, and temperature", with periods typically "between 1 and 50 days". The class is named after Delta Cephei, whose changes John Goodricke first saw in September 1784.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cepheid_variable

F15. Leavitt's study of 25 Cepheids in the Small Magellanic Cloud was published as Harvard Circular 173 in March 1912, signed by Pickering but "prepared by Miss Leavitt". The longer a Cepheid's period, the more luminous it is (the period-luminosity relation, Leavitt's law): "A straight line can be readily drawn among each of the two series of points." She reasoned that the cloud's stars are all at roughly the same distance from Earth, so their apparent brightness reflects their true brightness (Wikipedia: "all its stars must be roughly the same distance from Earth"; Grokipedia: "roughly equivalent distances from Earth").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt

F16. Because the period reveals the true luminosity, the period-luminosity relation "allows astronomers to determine a Cepheid's absolute magnitude from its observed period", and comparing that with how bright the star looks gives its distance; "Cepheid variables serve as standard candles in astronomy" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: a five-day Cepheid in the Small Magellanic Cloud looks about one ten-thousandth as bright as five-day Delta Cephei, so the cloud is about 100 times farther away. One year after Leavitt's report, Ejnar Hertzsprung found the distances of several Milky Way Cepheids and so set the relation's scale. Leavitt died on 12 December 1921, aged 53; Hubble often said she deserved the Nobel Prize, and Gösta Mittag-Leffler tried in 1924 to nominate her, not knowing she had died.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cepheid_variable ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Henrietta_Swan_Leavitt

## The Great Debate and Hubble's Cepheid in Andromeda

F17. The "Great Debate" was held on 26 April 1920 at a National Academy of Sciences meeting at the U.S. National Museum in Washington, D.C. Harlow Shapley argued that the spiral nebulae lay inside the Milky Way, which he put at roughly 300,000 light-years across and took for "the entirety of the Universe" (Wikipedia), with the Sun far from its centre. Heber Curtis argued that the spirals were separate "island universes", galaxies, and put the Milky Way at about 30,000 light-years across with the Sun near its centre. Shapley leaned on Adriaan van Maanen's measured rotation of spirals, later shown to be wrong. Curtis turned out right that the spirals are galaxies; Shapley right that the Sun lies far from the centre and that the Milky Way is large (his size too big).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Great_Debate_(astronomy) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Debate_(astronomy)

F18. Before the debate some astronomers took the spirals for star systems still forming: Isaac Roberts, who photographed Andromeda in 1888, thought it and similar "spiral nebulae" were "star systems being formed" (Wikipedia). Immanuel Kant had earlier argued that such nebulae are galaxies like the Milky Way.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromeda_Galaxy

F19. Edwin Hubble photographed the Andromeda nebula (M31) "with the Hooker 100-inch telescope of the Mount Wilson Observatory" on the night of 5-6 October 1923. He marked stars he took for novae with an N; on the first Cepheid variable the N is crossed out and the star marked "VAR!" (Carnegie). It was later confirmed as a Cepheid with a 31.4-day period, and Hubble put M31 at "approximately 900,000 light-years" (Grokipedia, Edwin Hubble and Great Debate; Grokipedia, Andromeda Galaxy, says 12 Cepheids by late 1924 gave about 860,000). The plate "established beyond any doubt that M31 was a separate galaxy from our own" (Carnegie). The New York Times reported it on 23 November 1924, and Hubble presented it to the American Astronomical Society on 1 January 1925 (Wikipedia).
    https://carnegiescience.edu/about/history/archives/plate-archives/m31var ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Edwin_Hubble ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Great_Debate_(astronomy) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Andromeda_Galaxy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Hubble

F20. The Andromeda Galaxy is a barred spiral "situated approximately 2.5 million light-years away", "the nearest major galaxy to the Milky Way", with "an estimated one trillion stars", "Visible to the naked eye under dark skies as a faint, elongated smudge" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: "approximately 752 kpc (2.5 million light-years)". It "is approaching the Milky Way at about 110 kilometers per second", seen in "its blueshifted light".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Andromeda_Galaxy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromeda_Galaxy

## Red shifts and the galaxies' flight

F21. Redshift: the observed wavelength of an object's light is longer than emitted, shifting it "toward the red end of the spectrum"; "Doppler redshift" is "caused by the relative motion of the source away from the observer", and an approaching source gives "a blueshift (wavelength decrease)". The cosmological redshift is "the expansion of spacetime itself stretching the light's wavelength", "akin to marks on a rubber band being pulled apart".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Redshift

F22. Vesto Slipher, at the Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, made the first Doppler measurement of a spiral nebula in 1912 (Andromeda, at "the quite exceptional velocity of -300 km(/s)", that is, approaching). By 1917 he had measured 25 spiral nebulae, and "all but three of those galaxies were moving away from us, at substantial speeds" (Wikipedia). Grokipedia: Slipher's work showed "light from most galaxies was shifted toward the red end of the spectrum"; "Slipher had compiled velocities for 25 nebulae, noting an average recession of about 570 km/s".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesto_Slipher ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hubble's_law

F23. A star's colour shows its surface temperature: "hotter stars emit predominantly in blue-violet light (shorter wavelengths) and cooler stars in redder light"; O stars exceed 30,000 K and look blue, M stars are about 3,000 K and red.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Stellar_classification

F24. Hubble's 1929 paper, "A Relation between Distance and Radial Velocity among Extra-Galactic Nebulae", done "in collaboration with Milton Humason", used redshifts including Slipher's: galaxies "are receding from Earth at speeds proportional to their distance" (Grokipedia); "the farther a galaxy is from the Earth, the faster it moves away" (Wikipedia). His constant was about 500 km/s per megaparsec, far above today's (about 70). Hubble himself kept calling the speeds "apparent" (1931 letter to de Sitter) and long kept open a universe with no true expansion (Allan Sandage, quoted by Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Edwin_Hubble ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hubble's_law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble%27s_law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Hubble

F25. Georges Lemaître, "a Belgian Catholic priest, astronomer, and theoretical physicist", published in 1927, in French in the Annales de la Société Scientifique de Bruxelles, an expanding universe with the velocity-distance relation and a rate of about 625 km/s per megaparsec, two years before Hubble (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: he "was the first to publish research deriving what is now known as Hubble's law", in "a low-impact journal"; its 1931 English translation dropped the constant. He linked the expansion to Einstein's general relativity (Astronomy). In 1931 he proposed the "hypothesis of the primeval atom", a universe begun from a single dense state. The International Astronomical Union recommended in 2018 that the law be called the Hubble-Lemaître law; 78 percent of the votes were in favour.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Georges_Lemaître ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble%27s_law ; https://www.astronomy.com/science/iau-vote-recommends-changing-the-name-of-hubbles-law/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Edwin_Hubble

## The Big Bang and the expansion

F26. The Big Bang: the universe "emerged from an extraordinarily hot and dense state approximately 13.8 billion years ago"; it "describes not an explosion in preexisting space but the expansion of space itself" (Grokipedia, Big Bang). The age from Planck (2018) is 13.787 ± 0.020 billion years (Grokipedia, Age of the universe); NASA: "Around 13.8 billion years ago". The evidence includes the red shifts of distant galaxies, the cosmic microwave background, and the light elements (about 75 percent hydrogen, 25 percent helium by mass).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Big_Bang ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Age_of_the_universe ; https://science.nasa.gov/universe/overview/

F27. The expansion has no centre: "the Big Bang occurred everywhere in the universe simultaneously" (Grokipedia, Big Bang); expansion occurs "uniformly in all directions with no central point", "From any vantage point, distant galaxies appear to recede", and in the balloon picture "every observer sees all others receding"; "space itself stretches rather than objects moving through fixed space"; galaxies themselves, held by gravity, do not expand (Grokipedia, Expansion of the universe). NASA's raisin-bread model: as the loaf rises "the raisins get further and further apart because there is more space between them", the farther raisins moving away faster; the universe "is not expanding into anything".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Big_Bang ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Expansion_of_the_universe ; https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/features/yba/M31_velocity/hubble_law/hubble_meaning.html

F28. Fred Hoyle introduced the name "big bang" in a BBC Third Programme radio talk on 28 March 1949 (Grokipedia, Big Bang and Fred Hoyle; Kragh 2013). He was the leading champion of the rival steady-state theory.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fred_Hoyle ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Big_Bang ; https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.0219

F29. The steady-state theory was published in 1948 by Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold ("The Steady-State Theory of the Expanding Universe") and, separately, Fred Hoyle ("A New Model for the Expanding Universe"), at Cambridge. It accepts the expansion but keeps the density of matter constant through "the continuous creation of matter" (about one hydrogen atom per cubic metre every few hundred thousand to ten billion years, by the source's various figures), so the universe "has no beginning and no end" and looks the same at all times (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: "the density of matter in the expanding universe remains unchanged due to a continuous creation of matter"; "In this model the universe has no beginning and no end").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Steady-state_model ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady-state_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fred_Hoyle

F30. The steady state "rejected any relic radiation from a hot, dense early phase"; the 1965 discovery of the cosmic microwave background was its "definitive 'smoking gun'" (Grokipedia, CMB), and the steady state "is now rejected by most cosmologists" (Wikipedia). Hoyle never accepted the background as heat from a hot early universe and proposed it came from "interstellar iron whiskers or grains" re-emitting starlight (Grokipedia, Fred Hoyle). Radio counts by Martin Ryle and the quasars also went against the steady state.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_microwave_background ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady-state_model ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fred_Hoyle ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Steady-state_model

## The cosmic microwave background

F31. Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, at Bell Labs in Holmdel, New Jersey, worked with a horn-reflector antenna from May 1964 into early 1965 and found an excess "3.5 ± 1.0 K" of microwave noise at 7.35 cm that persisted "regardless of direction, time of day, or season" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: "evenly spread over the sky, and was present day and night". They checked their electronics and for interference, and found pigeons nesting in the horn; they removed "some pigeons nesting in the antenna" and cleaned "out the accumulated droppings" (Wikipedia), but the noise remained.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_cosmic_microwave_background_radiation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_cosmic_microwave_background_radiation

F32. Robert Dicke's group at Princeton, about 60 km away, had been preparing to look for the Big Bang's leftover radiation; Dicke reportedly said "Boys, we've been scooped". Dicke, Peebles, Roll and Wilkinson "interpreted this radiation as a signature of the Big Bang"; the two papers appeared together in the Astrophysical Journal in July 1965. Penzias and Wilson shared the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics. Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman had predicted a relic glow of about 5 K in 1948.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Discovery_of_cosmic_microwave_background_radiation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_cosmic_microwave_background_radiation

F33. The cosmic microwave background is the "thermal remnant radiation from the Big Bang", released about 380,000 years after it, when the universe had cooled to about 3,000 K and "became neutral and transparent"; the expansion has since stretched it into "microwaves with a blackbody spectrum at 2.725 K", "uniform to within 1 part in 100,000 over the entire sky". Karl Jansky had found in 1932 that the Milky Way gives off radio waves, "strongest when directed toward the constellation Sagittarius" (so the galaxy's own radio glow is not even across the sky).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cosmic_microwave_background ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Karl_Guthe_Jansky

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. A light-year is a unit of time. Grokipedia lists the belief that it "represents a unit of time rather than a unit of distance"; Wikipedia: "Despite its inclusion of the word 'year', the term is a unit of distance, not a unit of time." (F11)
M2. The Sun sits at the centre of the galaxy: the view of Kapteyn's model and of Curtis in 1920 (F10, F17).
M3. A red shift means the object is cooler (a red star is cooler than a blue one, F23); but a shift of a whole galaxy's light toward red shows motion away (F21).
M4. The Big Bang was an explosion at one point in space, so the galaxies fly out from a centre (F26, F27: "not an explosion in preexisting space"; "no central point").
M5. A star that looks fainter gives off less light (it may only be farther away; F15, F16).
M6. The expanding universe means the galaxies themselves swell (F27: bound galaxies "do not expand").

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F34. Shapley's 1918 paper took "the parallaxes of a few clusters" directly from the periods and magnitudes of Cepheid variables; a larger number came from the clusters' brightest stars (the mean absolute magnitude of the 25 brightest stars, fixed by comparing them with the variables in ten clusters), and the survey was completed from the clusters' angular diameters (a curve of parallax against diameter drawn from 30 clusters). W. E. Harris: the estimates were "based almost entirely on cluster diameters and 25 brightest stars" (read through a search summary). So he timed pulsing stars in only a few of the 69 clusters, and the rest were scaled from those.
    https://adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1918ApJ....48..154S ; https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/DE136980C0CA66EE9E38EBA2C8CB5B1F/S0074180900091646a.pdf/the-galactic-distance-scale-globular-clusters.pdf ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Harlow_Shapley

F35. "In 1920, Jacobus Kapteyn proposed his theoretical model of the Milky Way's structure" ("First Attempt at a Theory of the Arrangement and Motion of the Sidereal System"), "derived primarily from extensive star count data"; it put the Sun "about 2,000 light-years from the galactic center", and he revised it in 1922 (Grokipedia). Wikipedia dates the Kapteyn Universe to 1922. Both came after Shapley's 1918 result, so the context says star counts seemed to put the centre near the Sun and Kapteyn's model of 1920 still did.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Jacobus_Kapteyn ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobus_Kapteyn

F36. Proxima Centauri is a "faint red dwarf" (type M5.5Ve) in the southern constellation Centaurus, of apparent magnitude about 11, so it cannot be seen without a telescope; it lies about 4.24 light-years from the Sun.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Proxima_Centauri

F37. Hoyle never accepted the Big Bang: he held its finite beginning "philosophically untenable" and "maintained vigorous defenses of steady-state variants, including QSSC" (the quasi-steady-state model of 1993) "into the late 1990s" (Grokipedia, Fred Hoyle); ESA: he refused to accept its authenticity; Scientific American (John Horgan): his "stubborn refusal to accept the big bang theory". In October 1965 the press reported him dropping the steady state in its original form (Papers Past), so the context says he never accepted the Big Bang, not that he never gave up the steady state.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fred_Hoyle ; https://www.esa.int/About_Us/Corporate_news/Professor_Sir_Fred_Hoyle ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/cross-check/remembering-big-bang-basher-fred-hoyle/ ; https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/CHP19651012.2.153

F38. Ellipticals range widely in mass: giants exceed 10^12 solar masses (up to about 10^13), while dwarf ellipticals hold about 10^7 to 10^9 solar masses of stars (Grokipedia). So the context gives enormous gravity to big ellipticals only.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Elliptical_galaxy
