## The rival ideas

F1. Spontaneous generation is the old, now abandoned theory that living things can arise from non-living matter without parents (maggots from meat, microbes from broth).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-1-spontaneous-generation

F2. Miasma theory held that disease comes from breathing "noxious vapors" (bad air) given off by rotting matter such as sewage, swamps and corpses; the word is Greek for pollution. In the 1840s-1850s it was the leading explanation of cholera, held by men such as Edwin Chadwick and William Farr. It was not foolish: filth, foul smells and disease really did go together, and cleaning up sewage did cut disease, though the reason given was wrong.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Miasma_theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak

F3. Germ theory holds that particular microbes cause particular diseases. By the 1880s it had largely replaced miasma among scientists.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Germ_theory_of_disease ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Miasma_theory ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Louis_Pasteur

## A control group

F4. A control is a baseline used to isolate the effect of one variable: a control group is kept under the same conditions as the test group except for the one thing being tested, so that other explanations (confounding influences) can be ruled out.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_control

## Ignaz Semmelweis and the two clinics (Vienna, 1840s)

F5. Vienna General Hospital had two maternity clinics. The First Clinic taught doctors and medical students; the Second Clinic trained midwives, whose students never touched corpses.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/illustrating/articles/ignaz-phillip-semmelweis-studies-of-death-in-childbirth ; https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2018/209/11/ignaz-philipp-semmelweis-1818-1865-herald-hygienic-medicine

F6. Childbed fever killed far more mothers in the doctors' clinic: Wikipedia gives an average of about 10 percent in the First Clinic against less than 4 percent in the Second; the James Lind Library gives 98.4 against 36.2 deaths per 1,000 births for 1840-1846. (Grokipedia gives 10-18 percent against under 4 percent.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/illustrating/articles/ignaz-phillip-semmelweis-studies-of-death-in-childbirth ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ignaz_Semmelweis

F7. Women were not chosen for a clinic: admissions alternated between the two clinics in 24-hour turns (the First took patients four days a week, the Second three), so the James Lind Library writes there was "no clinical selection of cases for either clinic" and that the rota had produced, "by accident rather than design, a controlled trial." Grokipedia does not describe the rota.
    https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/illustrating/articles/ignaz-phillip-semmelweis-studies-of-death-in-childbirth ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/semmelweis-i-1861/

F8. Women begged to be sent to the Second Clinic, and some gave birth in the street rather than enter the First; Semmelweis noticed that the street births rarely brought childbed fever. He set aside overcrowding (the Second Clinic was always the more crowded) and climate (the two clinics stood close together); he also changed the position in which mothers gave birth, and tested his own guess that the priest walking through the ward with the last rites frightened the women into the fever.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ignaz_Semmelweis (miasma judged inconsistent; crowding did not explain the gap)

F9. Jakob Kolletschka, a professor of forensic medicine and Semmelweis's friend, was pricked in the finger by a medical student's scalpel during an autopsy and died on 13 March 1847. His autopsy showed the same findings as the mothers dead of childbed fever. Semmelweis concluded that "cadaverous particles" carried on the hands of doctors and students from the autopsy room caused the fever. He did not know about bacteria and could not say what the particles were.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2018/209/11/ignaz-philipp-semmelweis-1818-1865-herald-hygienic-medicine

F10. In May 1847 Semmelweis made the staff wash their hands in a solution of chlorinated lime between autopsy work and examining patients; he chose it because it removed the smell of the corpses that soap left behind. The First Clinic's monthly death rate was 18.3 percent in April 1847, then 2.2 percent in June, 1.2 percent in July and 1.9 percent in August; for 1848 it was about 1.3 percent (Grokipedia 1.27; MJA "11.4% in 1846 to 1.3% in 1848").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2018/209/11/ignaz-philipp-semmelweis-1818-1865-herald-hygienic-medicine

F11. Why he was rejected: his observations conflicted with the medical opinion of the day; he had "no theoretical explanation" for why washing worked (Wikipedia); Johann Klein, head of the First Clinic, "demanded a mechanism beyond statistics", critics called the result a post hoc fallacy (deaths falling after the washing does not by itself prove the washing caused it), and his model "lacked a microbial mechanism, which was a major objection" (Grokipedia); some doctors were offended at being told they carried the disease. Klein never accepted the theory (MJA) and ended Semmelweis's post on 20 March 1849 (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Germ_theory_of_disease ; https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2018/209/11/ignaz-philipp-semmelweis-1818-1865-herald-hygienic-medicine

F12. Semmelweis was committed to an asylum near Vienna on 30 July 1865, beaten by the attendants, and died on 13 August 1865, aged 47, of blood poisoning (pyaemia, sepsis) from a gangrenous wound on his right hand. His findings won wide acceptance only after his death, once Pasteur's germ theory explained them and Lister's antiseptic surgery spread.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis

## John Snow and the Broad Street pump (London, 1854)

F13. John Snow (1813-1858) was a London doctor who doubted the miasma theory; from 1849 he argued that cholera is swallowed in water, because its symptoms begin in the gut ("alimentary canal"), not the lungs.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/John_Snow ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak

F14. The Soho outbreak began on 31 August 1854 and killed hundreds (sources give 420, 578, 616 and other totals, so no total is used). Snow marked each death at its address on a map, and the deaths clustered around the public pump on Broad Street. Of ten deaths in houses nearer another pump, five were in families who always sent to the Broad Street pump because they preferred its water.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak ; https://epi-snow.ph.ucla.edu/Stream2_BSPoutbreak_c.html (Snow 1855)

F15. The brewery on Broad Street, near the pump, had "above seventy workmen", and none of them suffered from cholera, "at least in a severe form"; the men were allowed a ration of malt liquor and "never obtained water from the pump in the street" (Snow 1855, quoting the owner Mr Huggins). Wikipedia: none of the brewery workers contracted cholera. (Grokipedia's pages give conflicting brewery numbers, 18 and "over 500"; Snow's own figure is used.) The Poland Street workhouse, almost surrounded by deaths, lost 5 of 535 inmates; it had its own well and never sent to Broad Street for water.
    https://epi-snow.ph.ucla.edu/Stream2_BSPoutbreak_c.html ; https://epi-snow.ph.ucla.edu/Stream2_BSPoutbreak_d.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak

F16. A widow of 59 at West End, Hampstead, far from Soho, had a large bottle of Broad Street pump water brought to her by cart because she preferred it; she drank it, caught cholera and died on 2 September 1854. Her visiting niece drank it too and died at home in Islington; there was no cholera in either district at the time.
    https://epi-snow.ph.ucla.edu/Stream1_introduction_d9.html (Snow 1855)

F17. Snow told the Board of Guardians of St James's parish his findings on the evening of 7 September 1854, and the pump's handle was removed the next day, 8 September. Fatal attacks had already fallen from 143 on 1 September to 12 on 8 September, partly because many people fled the district. Snow wrote that the attacks "had so far diminished before the use of the water was stopped, that it is impossible to decide whether the well still contained the cholera poison." The pump was back in use about six weeks later. MISCONCEPTION: the popular story that taking off the handle ended the outbreak.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak ; https://grokipedia.com/page/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak ; https://epi-snow.ph.ucla.edu/Stream2_BSPoutbreak_e.html

F18. Snow never saw the cholera germ; no one could detect it then. Robert Koch isolated Vibrio cholerae in 1883-1884.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Koch

## Pasteur's swan-neck flasks (Paris, 1859-1864)

F19. Earlier tests: John Needham (1745) boiled broth briefly and sealed it, and microbes grew; Lazzaro Spallanzani (1760s) boiled sealed flasks far longer and nothing grew. Needham answered that the long boil had destroyed a "vegetative force" and altered the air inside; with the air shut out, whether air was essential to life arising was left open. Theodor Schwann (1837) kept boiled meat broth from spoiling with air that had been heated or otherwise treated, which left room to say the treated air had been changed.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_generation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swan_neck_flask

F20. Félix Pouchet published a book defending spontaneous generation in 1859, and he and Louis Pasteur argued in public from 1859 to 1864. Pasteur won the French Academy of Sciences' Alhumbert Prize in 1862.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swan_neck_flask ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Louis_Pasteur

F21. Pasteur boiled broth in a flask whose neck he had drawn out into a long S-shaped (swan-neck) bend, open at the tip. Air moves in and out freely, but dust and the microbes on it settle on the moist glass of the bend, so the broth stays clear for a long time with no stopper. This answered Pouchet's claim that the oxygen in air was needed for life to arise, because the broth had free access to air, yet stayed unspoiled. MISCONCEPTION: that the bent neck seals the flask or keeps the air out.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swan_neck_flask ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-1-spontaneous-generation

F22. When the neck was broken off close to the body of the flask, so that the air and its dust reached the broth directly, the broth soon turned cloudy with microbes; this showed that the heating had not destroyed the broth's ability to support life (Pouchet had argued that the long heating had destroyed it). When an unbroken flask was tilted so the broth washed over the dust in the bend, it clouded too, showing the source of the life was outside the broth. Pasteur concluded that particles carried in the air, not the air itself or a "vital force" in it, bring the microbes.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swan_neck_flask ; https://mun.ca/biology/scarr/4270_Pasteur_experiment.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Louis_Pasteur

F23. Honest caveat: the objections to Pasteur were "not always unreasonable"; some bacterial spores (for example in hay) survive boiling, which confused the results of the day. MISCONCEPTION: that the swan-neck flasks proved germs cause disease; they showed where the life in spoiled broth comes from, and the disease link came later.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_generation

## Robert Koch's proof (Wollstein and Berlin, 1876-1890)

F24. Robert Koch was a country doctor in Wollstein (district medical officer). The anthrax rod had already been seen by Pollender, Rayer and Davaine, and Koch "set himself to prove scientifically that this bacillus is, in fact, the cause of the disease." With home-made slivers of wood he inoculated mice with anthrax bacilli from the spleens of farm animals that had died of anthrax, and they all died, "whereas mice inoculated at the same time with blood from the spleens of healthy animals did not suffer from the disease." Grokipedia: healthy animals got anthrax only from the isolated microbe, "while controls remained unaffected."
    https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1905/koch/biographical/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Robert_Koch

F25. That did not satisfy Koch: he wanted to know whether bacilli that had never been in contact with any animal could cause the disease. He grew pure cultures in the aqueous humour (the fluid) of an ox's eye for several generations, and they still caused anthrax. He also saw the rods form tough spores when conditions turned against them. He showed his work to Ferdinand Cohn in Breslau, who published it in 1876.
    https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1905/koch/biographical/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Robert_Koch

F26. Koch's postulates, the steps for proving that a particular microbe causes a particular disease: (1) the microbe is found in abundance in all who have the disease and is absent from the healthy; (2) it is isolated and grown in pure culture; (3) the pure culture should cause the disease when given to a healthy, susceptible host; (4) the microbe is isolated again from that newly sick host and is the same. Formulated by Koch and Friedrich Loeffler in 1884 on earlier ideas of Jakob Henle, and published by Koch in 1890 (Wikipedia; sources differ on who wrote them down first).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Koch's_postulates ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koch%27s_postulates

F27. Limits: Koch "later abandoned the universalist requirement of the first postulate" when he found people carrying cholera, and later typhoid, without symptoms (Wikipedia). Most cholera infections are mild or have no symptoms, and such carriers conflict with the first postulate (Grokipedia). Viruses cannot be grown in pure culture outside living cells. Step 3 says "should" because not everyone exposed to a germ falls ill.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koch%27s_postulates ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Koch's_postulates

## Joseph Lister and carbolic acid (Glasgow, 1865-1870)

F28. Before Lister, the oxygen of the air was "universally regarded as the agent by which putrefaction was effected," and it "seemed hopeless" to keep it out of a wound. After Pasteur showed that the rotting power of the air depended "not on the oxygen or any gaseous constituent, but on minute organisms suspended in it," Lister saw that decomposition "might be avoided without excluding the air, by applying as a dressing some material capable of destroying the life of the floating particles." He chose carbolic acid (phenol). (Lister, 1867.) The chemist Thomas Anderson drew his attention to Pasteur's work around 1864-1865.
    https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_Antiseptic_Principle_of_the_Practice_of_Surgery ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Joseph_Lister ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Lister

F29. Lister took the idea of carbolic acid from its use on the sewage of Carlisle. On 12 August 1865 he dressed the compound fracture (bone through the skin) of James Greenlees, an 11-year-old boy run over by a cart, with carbolic acid at the Glasgow Royal Infirmary; no infection followed and the bones knit in about six weeks. Amputation was the usual treatment for such a wound.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Joseph_Lister ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Lister

F30. Lister's own amputation figures (Lancet, 1870): 16 deaths in 35 cases (about 45 percent) before antisepsis, against 6 in 40 (about 15 percent) in his three antiseptic years. He admitted the numbers were "too small for a satisfactory statistical comparison." Critics questioned whether antisepsis caused the drop (Grokipedia); William Savory (1879) complained that Lister still gave no comparative statistics, and The Lancet urged that one ward be set aside for antisepsis so the two could be compared directly, side by side. (Later historians asked whether his comparison with earlier years was fair, since other things, such as which cases were treated, had changed.)
    https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/statistics-and-the-british-controversy-about-the-effects-of-joseph-listers-system-of-antisepsis-for-surgery-1867-1890/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Joseph_Lister

## Rival explanations of childbed fever

F31. Carl Braun, Semmelweis's successor as assistant in the First Clinic, listed 30 causes of childbed fever in his textbook; cadaverous infection was only the 28th. Others included "emotional traumata", "mistakes in diet", "chilling" and "atmospheric epidemic influences". Virtually every doctor rejected Semmelweis's central claim that the disease had one cause, a lack of cleanliness, even where some tried the washing.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- Removing the Broad Street pump handle ended the outbreak (F17: deaths were already falling).
- Pasteur's swan neck kept the air out (F21: air moved freely; only dust was caught).
- Boiling kills everything, so any later growth must be survivors or new life (F22, F23).
- Semmelweis knew about germs (F9: he spoke of "cadaverous particles" and did not know about bacteria).
- A control group "gets nothing" or is a spare (F4, F24: it gets the same treatment except the one thing tested).
- The fall in deaths after a change proves the change caused it (F11, F17, F30: post hoc; a decline already under way; other changes at the same time).
- Beer cured the brewery workers (F15: they simply never drank the pump water).

## Added in the revision (sources read 2026-11-25)

F32. Semmelweis tested other differences between the two clinics. In the Second Clinic women gave birth lying on their sides; he changed the birthing position in the First Clinic, and it "had no effect on the mortality rate." Whenever a patient was dying of childbed fever, a priest brought the last rites ringing a bell, and in the First Clinic he had to walk through five wards to reach her, while the Second Clinic's priest reached the sickroom directly. Semmelweis had the priest change his route and stop ringing the bell; "the adjustment also failed to influence the death rates in the clinic." Wikipedia: "He altered the position in which mothers gave birth, and proposed that the giving of last rites by priests in the clinic was terrifying women." (Grokipedia does not describe either test.)
    https://series.publisso.de/de/journals/hic/volume20/dgkh000608 (Ravel, Pandiyan and Chandran, GMS Hygiene and Infection Control 20, 2025) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis

F33. Chance: with 42,795 births and 2,977 maternal deaths in the two clinics from 1840 to 1846, the James Lind Library writes that chance "could confidently be excluded" as an explanation of the difference. It was the size of the counts, not the alternate-day admissions, that did this; the admissions ruled out choosing which women went where. (Wikipedia's table: 4,010 births in the First Clinic and 3,754 in the Second in 1846 alone.)
    https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/illustrating/articles/ignaz-phillip-semmelweis-studies-of-death-in-childbirth ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis

F34. Where Semmelweis was committed in 1865: Grokipedia names the "Niederösterreichische Landesirrenanstalt in Döbling, Vienna"; Wikipedia says he was committed to the Landesirrenanstalt Döbling and that Hebra lured him to "a Viennese insane asylum located in Lazarettgasse". Either way it was an asylum in Vienna, so the questions say "in Vienna", not "near Vienna".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ignaz_Semmelweis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Semmelweis

F35. Pasteur's swan-neck broth stayed clear "for extended periods, even after months of incubation", sterile "indefinitely unless the neck was broken or tilted" (Grokipedia); it "would remain sterile as long as the swan necks remained intact" (OpenStax). "Air can still move in and out of the flask," and dust and bacteria-bearing particles "settle or adhere to the moist glass of the bend" (Wikipedia). Pasteur concluded that particles in the air, not the air itself or a "vital force" in it, bring the microbes (F22); the vital force was the old answer of Needham and the spontaneous-generation side (F19).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spontaneous_generation ; https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-1-spontaneous-generation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swan_neck_flask

F36. Koch's cholera work, 1883-1884: he found a comma-shaped bacillus "in the intestinal walls and contents of cholera victims during autopsies" (Grokipedia); he "performed autopsies of almost 100 bodies" and found the bacterial infection in each one, and people who died of cholera "always had bacterial infection" (Wikipedia). He held the bacillus to be the cause of cholera, though his pure cultures did not bring the disease on in animals (Wikipedia). Later he found people carrying the germ with no symptoms and "abandoned the universalist requirement of the first postulate" (F27).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Robert_Koch ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Koch ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koch%27s_postulates

F37. How the postulates are written: "formulated by Robert Koch and Friedrich Loeffler in 1884", based on Jakob Henle's ideas, "refined and published by Koch in 1890", and "The fourth postulate was added later" (Wikipedia). They are now taught as four steps named after Koch. The first: the microbe "must be found in abundance in all organisms suffering from the disease" but "should not be found in healthy organisms" (Wikipedia); Grokipedia: "must be found in abundance in all organisms suffering from the disease, but not in healthy ones."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koch%27s_postulates ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Robert_Koch

F38. Koch's way of inoculating a mouse: he soaked a wood splinter in an infected animal's blood, "made a small incision at the base of a mouse's tail and inserted the splinter into the body" (National Geographic). The Nobel biography (F24) says mice inoculated "at the same time" with healthy spleen blood did not fall ill.
    https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/history-magazine/article/robert-koch-bacteria-tuberculosis-anthrax-cholera ; https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1905/koch/biographical/

F39. Anthrax is caught three main ways: spores "entering through breaks in the skin", "inhalation" of spores and "ingestion" of spores; grazing animals such as cattle and sheep are infected mainly "through ingestion of spores" from the soil. Rayer and Davaine saw the rods "in the blood of infected sheep" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Anthrax
