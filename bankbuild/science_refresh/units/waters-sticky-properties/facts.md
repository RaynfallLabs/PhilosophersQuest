## Why water is sticky: the polar molecule

F1. A water molecule is one oxygen atom bonded to two hydrogen atoms; each bond is a pair of shared electrons. Oxygen pulls on the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does (electronegativity 3.44 against 2.20), so the oxygen end carries a slight (partial) negative charge and each hydrogen end a slight positive charge. Grokipedia, Chemical polarity: "The higher electronegativity of oxygen (3.44) compared to hydrogen (2.20) creates partial negative charge on oxygen." Wikipedia: "making the oxygen partially negative and each hydrogen partially positive". Hawaii (Exploring Our Fluid Earth): "the hydrogen atoms do not pull as strongly on the shared electrons as the N, O, or F atoms."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_polarity ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_of_water ; https://manoa.hawaii.edu/exploringourfluidearth/chemical/properties-water/hydrogen-bonds-make-water-sticky

F2. Electrons carry a negative charge (protons positive, neutrons none), so the atom that holds shared electrons closer becomes slightly negative.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atom ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_polarity

F3. The molecule is bent, the H-O-H angle about 104.5 degrees (Wikipedia: 104.45 to 104.48); the bent shape keeps the charges from cancelling, whereas in straight (linear) carbon dioxide they "cancel exactly". Not asked (beyond 8th grade); background only.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_polarity ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_of_water

F4. Neighbouring water molecules attract where the slightly positive hydrogen of one meets the slightly negative oxygen of another: a hydrogen bond. Hawaii: "hydrogen bonds form between neighboring hydrogen and oxygen atoms of adjacent water molecules." Grokipedia (Chemical polarity): "the positive end of one molecule is attracted to the negative end of another." Grokipedia (Hydrogen bond): it "arises primarily from electrostatic attraction between the partially positive hydrogen and the lone pair on the acceptor."
    https://manoa.hawaii.edu/exploringourfluidearth/chemical/properties-water/hydrogen-bonds-make-water-sticky ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_polarity ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_bond

F5. A hydrogen bond is far weaker than the bond inside a molecule and is not a shared pair of electrons: Hawaii, "Hydrogen bonds are not true bonds like covalent bonds or ionic bonds." Grokipedia: typical strengths 4 to 50 kJ/mol, against covalent bonds above 150 kJ/mol.
    https://manoa.hawaii.edu/exploringourfluidearth/chemical/properties-water/hydrogen-bonds-make-water-sticky ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_bond

F6. A water molecule can take part in at most four hydrogen bonds (Wikipedia); in liquid water at room temperature each averages about 3.2 to 3.5 (Grokipedia), and in ordinary ice (ice Ih) "each water molecule participates in exactly four hydrogen bonds" (Grokipedia, Hydrogen bond).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_of_water ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_bond

## Cohesion: water clings to water

F7. Cohesion is the attraction between molecules of the same substance; water's comes from its hydrogen bonds (Grokipedia, Properties of water: "Cohesion refers to the internal attractive forces between water molecules, driven by hydrogen bonding"). USGS: "Cohesion: Water is attracted to water". Cohesion gives "the formation of spherical droplets" and makes water "form beads on non-adhesive surfaces" (Grokipedia, Cohesion).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cohesion_(chemistry) ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/capillary-action-and-water

F8. A free drop is round because surface tension, from cohesion, pulls its surface as small as it can be, and a sphere has the least surface for a given volume. Grokipedia (Drop): drops are nearly spherical "due to surface tension, which acts to minimize the surface area for a given volume"; where gravity is negligible a sphere "minimizes the surface free energy"; on Earth larger drops show "gravitational flattening"; a drop hanging from a tip grows from "a spherical cap to an elongated teardrop shape" before it falls. Grokipedia (Surface tension): "spheres minimize surface area for a given volume". USGS: "droplets of water tend to be pulled into a spherical shape by the cohesive forces".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Drop_(liquid) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Surface_tension ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/surface-tension-and-water

F9. In the weightlessness of space, water forms near-perfect balls. NASA image iss072e742508 (2 March 2025): Don Pettit "stares at a ball of water shaped by microgravity and attached to research hardware by surface tension", in the International Space Station's Kibo laboratory module. Hawaii: "In space, water is able to form perfectly round spheres".
    https://www.nasa.gov/image-detail/amf-iss072e742508 ; https://manoa.hawaii.edu/exploringourfluidearth/chemical/properties-water/hydrogen-bonds-make-water-sticky

F10. A body's own gravity rounds it only if it is huge: the icy moon Proteus, 420 km across, still has "an obviously non-equilibrium shape" (is not round), so a drop's own gravity is far too weak to shape it.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic_equilibrium

## Adhesion: water clings to other things, and climbs narrow tubes

F11. Adhesion is the attraction between water and other substances (Grokipedia: "the attraction between water and other surfaces, often mediated by polar interactions"; USGS: "Adhesion: Water is attracted to other substances"). Water's polar molecules give it strong adhesion to glass (Wikipedia: "Water also has high adhesion properties because of its polar nature").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/capillary-action-and-water ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_of_water

F12. Capillary action is "the spontaneous movement of a liquid through narrow spaces", set by cohesive forces within the liquid against adhesive forces between liquid and solid. Water wets glass (adhesion wins): "the meniscus is concave, leading to an upward rise". Mercury does not wet glass (cohesion wins): "for non-wetting liquids like mercury, it is convex, causing depression", that is, mercury in a thin glass tube stands below the level outside. Grokipedia (Cohesion): "high cohesion relative to adhesion causes liquids to bead up, as seen in mercury on glass"; water in a glass tube "wets the surface" because adhesion dominates.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Capillary_action ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cohesion_(chemistry)

F13. The narrower the tube, the higher the water climbs: the rise "is inversely proportional to the tube radius" (Grokipedia, Capillary action and Surface tension). USGS: "Smaller diameter tubes have more relative surface area inside the tube", "allowing capillary action to pull water up higher than in the larger diameter tubes". Figures (Wikipedia, Jurin's law; water at 20 degrees C in glass): a tube of 0.2 mm radius lifts water 70 mm; one of 2 cm radius, 0.7 mm; one of 2 m radius, 0.007 mm (so in a wide vessel water stands level). Grokipedia: a 0.1 mm radius tube lifts water about 15 cm.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Capillary_action ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Surface_tension ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/capillary-action-and-water ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurin%27s_law

F14. James Jurin (1684-1750), an English physician and scientist, used "glass tubes of varying diameters filled with water to measure the height" and "confirmed the inverse proportionality between the height of capillary rise" and the radius; his paper "An Account of Some Experiments Shown before the Royal Society" appeared in Philosophical Transactions in 1718 (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: he "discovered it between 1718 and 1719" and the law "was named in his honour". Earlier, Francis Hauksbee (1713) "showed that there was no dependence on the thickness of the tube walls".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Jurin%27s_law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurin%27s_law

## Surface tension, and soap breaking it

F15. Surface tension comes from cohesion: a molecule inside the water is pulled by neighbours on every side, but one at the surface has "no neighboring molecules above" (USGS), so there is "a net inward force since there will be no attractive force acting from above" (USGS). Grokipedia: surface molecules "experience a net attractive force directed inward toward the bulk liquid"; they "lack adjacent molecules on the vapor side". The surface then behaves like a stretched skin.
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/surface-tension-and-water ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Surface_tension

F16. Pure water's surface tension is about 72 mN/m (72.8 at 20 degrees C; 71.99 at 25 degrees C), high because of its hydrogen bonds; ethanol's is 22.3; mercury's about 465. USGS: "other than mercury, water has the greatest surface tension of any liquid." So the skin belongs to clean water itself, not to a film on it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Surface_tension ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/surface-tension-and-water

F17. A steel paper clip "can indeed float on the water surface" (USGS), as can a carefully placed needle or a steel razor blade, "despite its density being over seven times that of water", if it is laid on with "careful placement that avoids breaking the surface" (Grokipedia). Steel's density is 7.75 to 8.05 g/cm3, nearly eight times water's. USGS: "If the surface is agitated to break up the surface tension, the needle will quickly sink."
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/surface-tension-and-water ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Surface_tension ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Steel

F18. A water strider stands on water with its legs "creating small dimples without breaking the film" (Grokipedia, Gerridae), using the water's surface tension; USGS adds its long water-repellent legs. (The strider's own legs are the animal bank's; here it only sets the scene.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gerridae ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/surface-tension-and-water

F19. Soap is a surfactant: its molecules have "a hydrophilic (water-attracting) head and a hydrophobic (water-repelling) tail"; they gather at the surface, tails "extending into the vapor phase" (into the air), and this "disrupts cohesive forces in liquids like water", cutting surface tension "from about 72 mN/m for pure water to as low as 20-30 mN/m" (Grokipedia, Surfactant), "by 50–90%" (Grokipedia, Surface tension). USGS: detergents work by "lowering the surface tension of the water". A weakened surface can no longer hold up a floating clip, which sinks, as when the surface is broken (F17).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Surfactant ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Surface_tension ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/surface-tension-and-water

## Water up a plant's thin tubes

F20. The cohesion-tension explanation: water evaporating from the leaves (transpiration) "creates negative hydrostatic pressure (tension) in the leaf xylem" (Grokipedia, Xylem), so "water is being pulled up from the roots into the leaves" (Wikipedia, Xylem); "cohesive forces between water molecules" hold the column together and "adhesive forces between water and hydrophilic xylem cell walls" help (Grokipedia). Eugen Askenasy (1895): "cohesion allows the water to be pulled up and adhesion to the walls of the conduits holds it in place." Wikipedia (Properties of water): "Strong cohesion from hydrogen bonding and adhesion allows trees to transport water more than 100 m upward."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Xylem ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylem ; https://theplantconnection.substack.com/p/how-do-plants-move-water ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_of_water

F21. Henry Dixon and John Joly proposed it in 1894 (Grokipedia: "Proposed by Henry H. Dixon and John Joly in their 1894 paper"; Wikipedia: "proposed in 1894 by John Joly and Henry Horatio Dixon"); it is "the most widely accepted theory for the transport of water through a plant's vascular system" (Wikipedia). Their 1894 abstract: "evaporation at capillary water-surfaces is mainly responsible for the elevation of sap" (quoted by The Plant Connection). In 1895 they sealed a cut maple branch in a thick glass tube, its end in a vial of water outside, and it went on drawing water up with the air round its leaves at two and then three times atmospheric pressure (The Scientist). Not asked; background.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Xylem ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylem ; https://theplantconnection.substack.com/p/how-do-plants-move-water ; https://www.the-scientist.com/19th-century-experiments-explained-how-trees-lift-water-32093

F22. Coast redwoods "reach heights exceeding 100 m" (Grokipedia, Xylem), and trees carry water "more than 100 m upward" (Wikipedia, F20).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Xylem ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_of_water

F23. Capillary action alone cannot do it: USGS, "Capillary action can only "pull" water up a small distance, after which it cannot overcome gravity"; Nehemiah Grew reckoned capillary action in the xylem "would raise the sap by only a few inches" (Wikipedia, Xylem).
    https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/capillary-action-and-water ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylem

F24. Air pressure cannot push water that high: "a standard pressure of 1 atm supports a column approximately 10.3 m tall" (Grokipedia, Atmospheric pressure).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmospheric_pressure

F25. No living pump is needed: Eduard Strasburger "had shown that the transport of water in plants did not require the xylem cells to be alive" (Wikipedia, Xylem); plants "could transport water to great heights even when all the living cells in their stems and leaves had been poisoned" (The Plant Connection, citing his work of 1891 and 1893); his work "proved that the process was physical and not physiological" (Wikipedia, Eduard Strasburger).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylem ; https://theplantconnection.substack.com/p/how-do-plants-move-water ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduard_Strasburger

## Why ice floats: the open pattern

F26. Ordinary ice (ice Ih) is hexagonal: each molecule is "hydrogen-bonded to four neighbors in a tetrahedral arrangement" (Grokipedia, Ice), in "a rigid, open tetrahedral network that results in a hexagonal crystal lattice" (Grokipedia, Hydrogen bond); "the bonds enforce an expanded structure with void spaces" (ibid.). Its lower density "results from the open hexagonal lattice structure in ice Ih" (Grokipedia, Properties of water) and "the expansive, cage-like voids in the hydrogen-bond network", the liquid being "closer-packed" (Grokipedia, Ice). Wikipedia: ice has an "open lattice structure"; on melting, "a denser molecular packing in which some of the lattice cavities are filled by water molecules". Wikipedia (Ice): "a packing of molecules less compact in the solid".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_bond ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_of_water ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice

F27. Ice's density is about 0.917 g/cm3 against about 1.00 for liquid water (Grokipedia, Ice: "approximately 0.917 g/cm³ compared to 1 g/cm³"; Grokipedia, Properties: 0.9167 at 0 degrees C), about 8.3 percent less dense; "When water freezes, it increases in volume (about 9% for fresh water)" (Wikipedia, Ice; Grokipedia, Ice: "water expands by about 9% in volume").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice

F28. Cooling lowers particles' kinetic energy, so they move more slowly; temperature is "a measure of the average kinetic energy of the microscopic particles" (Grokipedia). Water freezing is water cooled to 0 degrees C, so its molecules are slowing, not speeding up.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Temperature ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_expansion

F29. Liquid water is densest at about 3.98 degrees C (Grokipedia, Properties). Not asked.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Particles themselves grow when a substance expands (or are pictured with the bulk's properties): the RSC's research summary reports some 16-year-olds thinking "that particles expand when they are heated", learners who "ascribe bulk properties to particles themselves", and about half of 18-year-olds in one small study thinking water molecules in steam are larger than those in ice. Here: that ice's molecules swell as it freezes (wrong: the molecules are unchanged; the pattern is open, F26).
    https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-the-particulate-nature-of-matter-beyond-appearances/4017763.article

M2. A hydrogen bond is taken for a true shared-electron bond between molecules (Hawaii warns against it, F5).

M3. Cohesion and adhesion are swapped (water-to-water against water-to-other): classroom quiz items on TEKS 8.6C have been seen with the two reversed (author's note from search results), so a reversed choice is an honest wrong choice.

M4. A heavy liquid cannot climb because it is heavy: mercury's depression in a glass tube is set by its cohesion beating adhesion (F12), not by weight alone; without surface effects a liquid stands level with the dish, as water does in a wide vessel (F13).

M5. A free drop or bubble is shaped by air pressing on it, or by its own gravity: the shape comes from surface tension (F8); a drop's own gravity is negligible (F10).

M6. Living cells pump sap up a tree: the respectable older idea, disproved by Strasburger's poisoned plants (F25). Air pressure pushing sap up from the soil fails above about 10 m (F24). Capillary wicking alone lifts water only a few inches (F23).

M7. The ice-floats-because-of-air-bubbles idea: bubbles in cloudy ice play only a small part; the main cause is the open molecular pattern (search-result extracts only, MSU Extension and Home Science Tools; no question rests on them). The wrong choice used here is narrower and plainly false: air filling the spaces between the molecules (the lattice's gaps are empty, F26).
    https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/exploring_our_world_why_does_ice_float

## Left out, or not sure

- The charged-comb-bends-a-water-stream demonstration: Grokipedia (Chemical polarity) says the bending is due to "dielectrophoretic forces" and that "permanent dipole orientation alone does not cause deflection in uncharged streams", so the usual classroom telling (polarity alone) is a half-truth. Not used.
- Kepler's six-cornered snowflake (1611) and the X-ray finding of ice's hexagonal lattice: the X-ray date differs by source (Bragg 1921 or 1922). Not used.
- "Wax repels water": Grokipedia's definition of a hydrophobe says "repel or avoid", but the same page says the effect "is not a true attractive force" and that water simply "cannot form hydrogen bonds with nonpolar surfaces". Too ambiguous for a wrong choice; not used.
- Root pressure is real but small (Wikipedia: up to 145 kPa in a grapevine, about zero in some plants), so it is never a wrong choice here.
- Water bond counts: Grokipedia gives liquid water 3.5 bonds a molecule on average, its Hydrogen bond page 3.2 to 3.4, Hawaii "up to three"; only ice's "exactly four" is used.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F30. Things aboard the space station float because they are falling: "The answer is that they are in free fall." "The spacecraft, its crew and everything aboard are all falling around Earth." "Since they are all falling together, the crew and objects appear to float." (NASA, What Is Microgravity?) The bank's gravity-and-orbits unit teaches the same.
    https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-k-4/

F31. Electric charge on a drop pushes it apart, not together: as a charged droplet shrinks to its Rayleigh limit, the repulsion of its like charges "becomes more powerful than the surface tension holding the droplet together" (Wikipedia, Electrospray ionization). So static electricity is not what rounds a floating drop.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrospray_ionization

F32. A water molecule is neutral overall though its ends carry slight charges: ACS Middle School Chemistry (chapter 5, lesson 1) notes that the water molecule as a whole has 10 protons and 10 electrons, so it is neutral, and defines a polar molecule as one in which "a neutral molecule has a positive area at one end and a negative area at the other" (read through the search summary). No electron leaves or joins it; its shared electrons only sit nearer the oxygen (F1, F2).
    https://www.acs.org/middleschoolchemistry/lessonplans/chapter5/lesson1.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_polarity

F33. A hydrogen bond is mainly an electric attraction but has "partial covalent character due to charge transfer and orbital overlap" (Grokipedia, Hydrogen bond, via the fact-check), so the context says it is not a shared pair of electrons rather than that it shares none.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_bond

F34. Water's surface tension against other liquids: water about 72.8 mN/m at 20 C, ethanol 22.3 (Grokipedia, Surface tension), so water's is about three times alcohol's. It is not second only to mercury, as USGS says: pure hydrogen peroxide is 80.4 mN/m at 20 C (Evonik data sheet) and room-temperature liquid metals such as Galinstan reach 535-718 (Wikipedia). So the context says "stronger than almost any other everyday liquid". Mercury's is about 465, over six times water's (F16).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Surface_tension ; https://active-oxygens.evonik.com/en/products-and-services/hydrogen-peroxide/general-information/physico-chemical-properties ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galinstan
