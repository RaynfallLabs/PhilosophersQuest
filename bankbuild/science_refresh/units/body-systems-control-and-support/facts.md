# Body Systems: Bones, Muscles, Nerves and Glands: fact sheet (T3, 7th grade)

Researched 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; KidsHealth (Nemours), OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology 2e, LibreTexts, Cleveland Clinic, Stanford Blood Center, NHS Blood and Transplant and Wikipedia to corroborate. Lane: clinical and brief; nothing about the reproductive system. Neighbours not re-asked: T1 `the-bodys-main-organs` (skull guards the brain, ribs the heart and lungs, a muscle pulls and never pushes, nerves carry messages, the skin is the largest organ), T1 `born-knowing-or-learned` (a newborn's grasp; jerking a hand back from a hot pan as an inborn behaviour), T2 `one-cell-or-many` (a red blood cell loses its nucleus as it matures; nerve cells up to a metre long), T5 `the-cell-cycle` (red blood cells live about four months).

## Facts

F1 The skeleton supports the body, protects organs, acts as levers for movement, makes blood cells in the red bone marrow and stores minerals (calcium, phosphorus). An adult skeleton typically has 206 bones. https://grokipedia.com/page/Human_skeleton ("The adult human skeleton typically consists of 206 discrete bones"; "provides structural support by forming a rigid framework"; hematopoiesis "primarily occurs within the red bone marrow of the human skeleton"; "the body's principal reservoir for essential minerals"); https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/bones-muscles-joints.html (marrow: "where most of the body's blood cells are made")

F2 A newborn has roughly 270 to 300 bones (Cleveland Clinic: 275 to 300); many start as cartilage and, as the child grows, harden (ossify) and fuse into single bones, so most adults have 206. Examples: the skull's plates close over the soft spots (fontanelles) by about 18 to 24 months; the five sacral vertebrae fuse into one sacrum; each hip bone forms from three bones (ilium, ischium, pubis) fusing in adolescence. https://grokipedia.com/page/Human_skeleton ("the 5 sacral vertebrae fuse into a single sacrum"; hip bone "formed by the fusion of the ilium, ischium, and pubis during adolescence"; "the gradual closing of skull fontanelles"); https://health.clevelandclinic.org/how-many-bones-does-a-baby-have ("A newborn has 275 to 300 bones"; "As a child grows, their bones fuse and harden through a process called ossification"; extra flexibility for birth)

F3 The change runs from cartilage to bone (ossification), not from bone back to cartilage; the bones are not lost, they join. https://health.clevelandclinic.org/how-many-bones-does-a-baby-have (many baby bones start as cartilage; "bones fuse and harden through a process called ossification")

F4 Red bone marrow makes red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets. In adults the red marrow lies mainly in the vertebrae, pelvis (hip bones), ribs, breastbone (sternum) and skull; yellow (fatty) marrow fills the shafts of long bones. The marrow makes over 500 billion blood cells a day, about 200 billion of them red. https://grokipedia.com/page/Bone_marrow ("red bone marrow, which actively generates red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets"; "predominantly in the vertebrae, pelvis, ribs, sternum, skull"; yellow marrow "dominates the shafts of long bones"; "over 500 billion blood cells daily"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Red_blood_cell ("approximately 200 billion red blood cells are produced daily"; erythropoiesis "primarily occurs in the red bone marrow of flat bones")

F5 Before birth, blood is made first in the yolk sac, then in the spleen, liver and lymph nodes (the liver is the main blood-forming organ of the fetus); the bone marrow takes over. In adults, old red cells are cleared mainly by cells of the spleen and liver. The heart makes no blood cells. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haematopoiesis ("blood formation occurs in the spleen, liver and lymph nodes"; "When bone marrow develops, it eventually assumes the task of forming most of the blood cells"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Red_blood_cell (clearance "primarily occurs through phagocytosis by macrophages in the spleen and liver")

F6 A whole-blood donation is capped at one pint; the bone marrow then makes new red cells, and replacing all the red cells given takes about 4 to 8 weeks. https://stanfordbloodcenter.org/pulse-spring23-what-really-happens-to-your-body-after-you-donate-blood-effects-risks-and-recovery/ ("capped at one pint per person"; "typically takes 4-8 weeks to replace all the red cells"; erythropoietin "signaling to the bone marrow to generate red cells"); https://www.blood.co.uk/the-donation-process/after-your-donation/how-your-body-replaces-blood/ ("The bone marrow produces stem cells"; red cell stores rebuilt in "a number of weeks")

F7 Skeletal muscles move bones by contracting and pulling; they cannot push, so they work in opposing pairs on either side of a joint. The biceps, at the front of the upper arm, bends (flexes) the elbow; the triceps, at the back, straightens (extends) it, as in pushing. While one contracts, its partner (the antagonist) relaxes and is lengthened. https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/bones-muscles-joints.html ("work in pairs of flexors and extensors"; "the biceps muscle, in the front of the upper arm, is a flexor"; "the triceps, at the back of the upper arm, is an extensor"; "Muscles can pull bones, but they can't push them"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Anatomical_terms_of_muscle (the antagonist is "typically relaxing or lengthening to allow the movement to occur"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Triceps ("primarily function[s] to extend the elbow joint"; "such as pushing and straightening the arm"; "the chief antagonist to the biceps brachii")

F8 The central nervous system is the brain and the spinal cord; the peripheral nervous system is the nerves linking them to the muscles, organs and sense organs. Sensory neurons carry signals in to the central nervous system; motor neurons carry signals out to muscles and glands. https://grokipedia.com/page/Nervous_system ("the central nervous system (CNS), which includes the brain and spinal cord"; "nerves and ganglia that connect the CNS to muscles, organs, and sensory receptors"; "sensory neurons (afferent, carrying signals from periphery to CNS)"; "motor neurons (efferent, to muscles and glands)")

F9 The cornea and lens focus light into a real, inverted (upside-down) image on the retina at the back of the eye. Light-sensing cells (photoreceptors) in the retina turn light into electrical signals, which the optic nerve carries to the brain; they reach the visual cortex in the occipital lobe, at the back of the brain, which is mainly responsible for vision. https://grokipedia.com/page/Human_eye (cornea and lens form "a real, inverted, and reversed image on the retina"; photoreceptors "converts light energy into electrical signals"; signals "reach the visual cortex in the occipital lobe"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Retina (light "converted into electrical signals"; "transmitted to the brain via the optic nerve"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Occipital_lobe ("most posterior portion of the cerebrum"; "primarily responsible for visual processing")

F10 A spinal reflex arc runs from a sensory receptor along a sensory neuron to the spinal cord, then out along a motor neuron to a muscle. It bypasses the brain for speed; the brain receives the signal while the reflex is carried out and analyses it afterwards. The withdrawal reflex pulls a body part away from heat or a sharp object (stepping on glass or a tack lifts the foot). https://grokipedia.com/page/Reflex_arc ("integration center, typically in the spinal cord"; "often bypassing higher brain centers for speed and efficiency"; "without the delays associated with cortical processing"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflex_arc ("an action potential can bypass the brain"; "The brain will receive the input while the reflex is being carried out"; "the analysis of the signal takes place after the reflex action"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Withdrawal_reflex ("stepping on a sharp object, like a piece of glass or a tack"; "without conscious awareness or higher brain involvement")

F11 The spinal cord carries signals between the brain and the rest of the body: sensory signals up and motor signals down. A complete spinal cord injury is the total loss of feeling (sensory function) and movement (motor function) below the level of the injury; above it, function remains. An injury in the middle of the back (thoracic) causes paraplegia and spares the arms. https://grokipedia.com/page/Spinal_cord_injury ("transmits signals between the brain and the rest of the body"; "disrupts the ascending sensory pathways and descending motor pathways"; "the total loss of sensory and motor function in all segments below the neurological level"; "Above the injury level, motor function remains intact"; "Thoracic injuries (T1-T12) result in paraplegia, sparing the upper limbs")

F12 Endocrine glands release hormones straight into the bloodstream, which carries them to cells in other parts of the body. https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/endocrine.html ("Endocrine glands release hormones into the bloodstream"; this "lets the hormones travel to cells in other parts of the body"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Endocrine_system (hormones "secreted directly into the bloodstream"); https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/17-1-an-overview-of-the-endocrine-system (hormones "transported primarily via the bloodstream")

F13 The pituitary gland is pea-sized and sits at the base of the brain. Its growth hormone stimulates the growth of bone and other tissues (too little in childhood causes short stature), and its hormones control many other glands (TSH sets the thyroid working; ACTH the adrenal glands), so it is often called the master gland. https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/endocrine.html ("is at the base of the brain, and is no bigger than a pea"; growth hormone "stimulates the growth of bone and other body tissues"; "The hormones it makes control many other endocrine glands"; "often called the 'master gland'"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Pituitary_gland ("pea-sized"; "situated at the base of the brain"; "master gland"; deficiency "leads to short stature in children")

F14 Robert Wadlow (1918-1940), of Alton, Illinois, was 2.72 m (8 ft 11.1 in) tall at his death, the tallest person in recorded history. His height came from pituitary gigantism: an enlarged (hyperplastic) pituitary made too much growth hormone, and he was still growing when he died. https://grokipedia.com/page/Robert_Wadlow ("8 ft 11.1 in (2.72 m) tall at the time of his death"; "widely recognized as the tallest person in recorded history"; "pituitary gigantism caused by hyperplasia of his pituitary gland"; "confirming his ongoing growth")

F15 The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland at the front of the neck. Its hormones control the rate at which cells burn fuel from food to make energy: more hormone, faster reactions. Too much (hyperthyroidism) brings weight loss despite a bigger appetite, a fast heartbeat, heat intolerance and sweating; too little (hypothyroidism) brings weight gain despite a smaller appetite, feeling cold, a slow heart rate and tiredness. https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/endocrine.html ("control the rate at which cells burn fuels from food to make energy"; "The more thyroid hormone there is in the bloodstream, the faster chemical reactions happen"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Thyroid ("butterfly-shaped endocrine organ"; "located in the anterior neck"; "These hormones increase basal metabolic rate"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Hyperthyroidism ("unintentional weight loss despite increased appetite"; "tachycardia"; "heat intolerance"; "Cold intolerance is characteristic of hypothyroidism, not hyperthyroidism"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Hypothyroidism ("unexplained weight gain despite often reduced appetite"; "cold intolerance"; "bradycardia (low heart rate)"; "Persistent fatigue")

F16 Insulin is made by the beta cells of the pancreas's islets. It lowers blood sugar by letting cells (muscle, fat, liver) take glucose in from the blood and store it; glucose needs insulin to move from the bloodstream into cells. Glucagon, the pancreas's other hormone, does the opposite: it makes the liver release stored sugar. https://grokipedia.com/page/Insulin ("produced and secreted by the beta cells of the pancreatic islets of Langerhans"; "promoting the uptake and storage of glucose in cells"; "glucagon primarily stimulates hepatic glycogenolysis to rapidly increase blood glucose"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Type_1_diabetes (insulin is needed for glucose to move from the bloodstream into cells); https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/endocrine.html (insulin and glucagon "control the level of glucose, or sugar, in the blood")

F17 In type 1 diabetes the immune system destroys the insulin-making beta cells, so the body has almost no insulin and blood sugar rises dangerously; it is not linked to lifestyle. When blood sugar is above the kidneys' limit (about 180 mg/dL) sugar spills into the urine (glycosuria); a lack of insulin causes this, not insulin. https://grokipedia.com/page/Type_1_diabetes ("the body's immune system mistakenly attacks and destroys the insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas"; "blood glucose levels rise to dangerous heights if untreated"; "is not linked to lifestyle factors like obesity"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Glycosuria ("the abnormal presence of glucose in the urine"; "exceeds the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, typically around 180 mg/dL"; "absolute insulin deficiency, causing uncontrolled hyperglycemia and subsequent glycosuria")

F18 Leonard Thompson, aged 13, dying of diabetes at Toronto General Hospital, was given the first insulin injection on 11 January 1922, an extract made by Frederick Banting and Charles Best; it caused an allergic reaction. On 23 January 1922 he was given James Collip's purer extract, his blood sugar fell from 520 to 120 mg/dL within about 24 hours, and daily injections went on. He lived about 13 more years and died in 1935, aged 27. The extracts came from animal pancreases (dog pancreases in the discovery experiments; the page names no animal for Thompson's doses). https://grokipedia.com/page/Leonard_Thompson_(diabetic) (11 January 1922; Toronto General Hospital; "severe local allergic reaction"; Collip's extract "far better tolerated"; 520 to 120 mg/dL; died 20 April 1935); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Thompson_(diabetic) ("at 13 years of age"; "11 January 1922"; "on 23 January, he began a two-week series of daily injections"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Insulin ("Discovered in 1921 through experiments led by Frederick Banting and Charles Best at the University of Toronto")

F19 Adrenaline (epinephrine) is made in the adrenal medulla; the adrenal glands sit on top of the kidneys. Released rapidly in fright or stress, it raises the heart rate and the force of each beat, widens the airways, raises blood sugar, sends more blood to the muscles and slows digestion. It travels in the blood and is broken down quickly: its half-life in the blood is about 2 to 3 minutes. https://grokipedia.com/page/Adrenaline ("primarily secreted by the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla"; adrenal glands "located atop the kidneys"; "heart rate"; "bronchodilation"; glycogenolysis raising blood glucose; "redirecting blood flow to muscles"; reduces "non-essential functions like digestion"; "plasma half-life of approximately 2-3 minutes"); https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/endocrine.html ("Also called adrenaline, epinephrine increases blood pressure and heart rate when the body is under stress")

F20 The nervous and endocrine systems differ in speed and duration: a nerve response comes within milliseconds and stops very quickly once the signalling ends; hormones act more slowly and their effects last longer. In fright the nerves themselves set the adrenal glands releasing their hormones within seconds. https://med.libretexts.org/Courses/James_Madison_University/A_and_P_for_STEM_Educators/13%3A_Endocrine_System/13.05%3A_Overview_of_the_Endocrine_System/13.5B%3A_Comparing_the_Nervous_and_Endocrine_Systems ("near instantaneous, although the effects are often short lived"; endocrine responses "typically slow but long lasting"); https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/17-1-an-overview-of-the-endocrine-system ("The target cell responds within milliseconds"; the response "ceases very quickly once the neural signaling ends"; adrenal hormones released "within seconds"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Endocrine_system ("the endocrine system uses slower, longer-lasting communication through hormones")

F21 Hair grows from the hair bulb in its follicle, where living cells divide and push outward; the hair itself is made mainly of dead, keratinized cells. The follicle's bulb is wrapped in sensory nerve fibres, so pulling a hair is felt. https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/5-2-accessory-structures-of-the-skin (hair is "primarily made of dead, keratinized cells"; "basal cells of the hair bulb divide and push cells outward"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Hair ("tightly packed, keratinized dead cells"; sensory nerve fibers "densely innervating the hair follicle bulb")

F22 The nail body (plate) is made of densely packed dead keratinocytes and has no blood vessels; it grows from the living nail root (matrix), and the nail bed beneath it has dense nerve endings. Nails are made of keratin, a hard protein also found in hair and skin, not of calcium. https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/5-2-accessory-structures-of-the-skin ("composed of densely packed dead keratinocytes"; "The nail body forms at the nail root"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Nail_(anatomy) ("compacted keratinized cells called onychocytes"; plate "avascular"; "The nail bed contains dense free nerve endings"); https://ummhealth.org/simply-well/healthy-nails-are-at-your-fingertips ("Our nails are made of keratin, a hard, strong protein also found in hair and skin")

F23 The integumentary system is the skin with its accessory structures: hair, nails, sweat glands and sebaceous (oil) glands. https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/5-2-accessory-structures-of-the-skin (accessory structures "include hair, nails, sweat glands, and sebaceous glands")

## Misconceptions a 7th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Blood is made in the heart. Ozgur (2013), International Journal of Environmental and Science Education, via SCIMAP: https://wordpressua.uark.edu/scimap/?p=722 (grades 3-5: "Blood is produced in the heart"). Corrected by F4, F5.

M2 A muscle can push as well as pull, so one muscle could straighten the arm by lengthening. KidsHealth corrects it ("Muscles can pull bones, but they can't push them", F7). Carried from the T1 sheet.

M3 The eye sends a picture (perhaps flipped the right way up) to the brain, or the light itself travels up the optic nerve. Author's judgment; corrected by F9 (photoreceptors turn light into electrical signals).

M4 Every quick movement is decided in the brain, or the muscles and skin react by themselves. Author's judgment; corrected by F10.

M5 The brain runs everything by nerves, so a gland in the head would reach the legs along nerves or the spinal cord. Author's judgment; corrected by F12.

M6 Nails are made of calcium, like bone (nail-care sources call this a common misconception, e.g. https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/tx-nail-technician/nail-anatomy-physiology/nail-growth-composition), or hair and nails are living tissue without nerves. Corrected by F21, F22.

M7 Diabetes comes from eating too much sugar, or insulin gets rid of sugar through the urine. Author's judgment; corrected by F16, F17.

M8 A fright strains the heart, or nerve signals linger. Author's judgment; corrected by F19, F20.

## Notes and cautions

- A muscle can also pull while it lengthens (lowering a load slowly), and partners may tighten together to steady a joint. The muscle-pair question fixes the case: the arm straightens to push open a door, the triceps' own job (F7), with the biceps relaxing.
- Bone counts at birth differ by source (270 to 300): say "somewhere near 300", never an exact number.
- Do not say the liver or spleen never makes blood: the fetal liver does (F5). The marrow question is about a healthy adult.
- Thompson's age: Wikipedia and Grokipedia's birth date give 13 at the first injection (some accounts say 14). The blood-sugar fall came after Collip's purer extract (23 January), so the stem says "early in 1922" and does not tie the fall to the first injection. No animal is named for his doses.
- Adrenaline's half-life is short (2 to 3 minutes), so the stem says the pounding lasts "minutes", not hours.
- Growth hormone acts partly through IGF-1 made in the liver, but the hormone itself does reach bone through the blood; the Wadlow question asks only how the hormone travels.
- The adrenal glands' fact that the nerves set them working (F20) means the fright response is both systems at once; the firework question asks why the pounding outlasts the jump, which is the hormone's lasting effect.

F24 Yellow marrow is made mostly of fat cells (adipocytes) and fills the shafts (medullary cavities) of the long bones in adults; at birth nearly all marrow is red, yellow marrow spreads from about age 7 until about 25, and by adulthood red marrow is about half the total. In adults red marrow remains in the vertebrae, pelvis, ribs, sternum, skull and the upper ends of the humerus and femur, and the blood cells are made in the red marrow, not the yellow (which can turn red again under severe demand). https://grokipedia.com/page/Bone_marrow (yellow marrow mostly adipocytes; "dominates the shafts" of long bones; nearly all marrow red at birth; red marrow about half of total volume in adults)

F25 The femur, "commonly known as the thigh bone", is "the longest, heaviest, and strongest bone in the human body"; its shaft surrounds a "central medullary cavity filled with marrow". https://grokipedia.com/page/Femur

F26 Air-filled cavities in the skeleton, linked to the air sacs, are found in birds: "PSP [postcranial skeletal pneumaticity] is unique to birds" among living tetrapods, and "mammals lack postcranial skeletal pneumaticity" (a person's limb bones hold no air). https://grokipedia.com/page/Skeletal_pneumaticity

F27 The quadriceps is "located on the front of the thigh" and is "the most powerful extensor of the knee joint, primarily extending (straightening) the knee"; it acts "as an antagonist to the hamstring muscles that flex the knee"; strains of its rectus femoris are common "in sports like soccer where kicking is prevalent". https://grokipedia.com/page/Quadriceps ; the pair rule (one contracts while its partner relaxes) is F7.

F28 The eye's parts: the cornea, the clear front layer, is "the primary refractive surface" and gives "roughly two-thirds of the eye's total focusing ability"; the lens sits behind the iris and changes shape to focus; the iris, the coloured part, "controls the size of the central pupil to regulate light entry"; the retina "lines the inner surface" at the back of the eye and holds the photoreceptors (rods and cones), where light is turned into electrical signals. https://grokipedia.com/page/Human_eye

F29 Cartilage has "firmness, flexibility, and rubbery consistency"; hyaline cartilage is found in the "rib cage, nose, and trachea" and elastic cartilage in the "external ear (pinna)"; it "lacks blood vessels and nerves". https://grokipedia.com/page/Cartilage

F30 Leonard Thompson's age at his first injection is given as 13 by Wikipedia (birth 17 July 1908) and as 14 by the University of Toronto's insulin archive ("in January 1922, at age 14, Thompson was the first diabetic to receive injections of pancreatic extract"); either way he was a teenage boy. https://collections.library.utoronto.ca/explore/insulin/about/patients ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Thompson_(diabetic)

M9 (revision) A grown-up's long bones are full of red, blood-making marrow all the way through (the T1 and T2 units teach only that blood is made in the marrow). Corrected by F24.
