# Action and Reaction: Newton's Third Law: fact sheet (T2, 6th grade, physical strand)

Researched 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; NASA Glenn, OpenStax, NGSS, Texas TEKS, a Virginia (WHRO) physics course, Alloprof, BSCS and physics-education research to corroborate and to fix the grade.

Note on a source: Grokipedia's Reaction (physics) page says the normal force "forms an action-reaction pair with the gravitational force between Earth and the object". That is wrong (both forces act on the same object; Wikipedia's Newton's laws page gives the correct pairing, F4). Nothing here relies on that sentence.

## Facts

F1 Newton's third law: when one object pushes or pulls on a second, the second pushes or pulls back on the first, equally hard and in the opposite direction. Isaac Newton stated it in his Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687): "to every action there is always opposed an equal reaction". https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion ("articulated by Isaac Newton in his Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687)"; "to every action there is always opposed an equal reaction"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion ("the mutual actions of two bodies upon each other are always equal, and directed to contrary parts"); https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/newtons-laws-of-motion/ ("Whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object exerts an equal and opposite force on the first.")

F2 The two forces of a pair are equal in size, opposite in direction and act at the same moment: the push back does not come later. https://grokipedia.com/page/Reaction_(physics) ("the second body simultaneously exerts a force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction on the first body"); https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-26 (TEKS 6.7C: "identify simultaneous force pairs that are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction that result from the interactions between objects using Newton's Third Law of Motion"); WHRO Virginia physics course, https://digitallearning.whro.org/courses/physics/mod5/top2/documents/mod5top2_content_newtonsthird.pdf (each force is matched with one of "equal magnitude", "opposite direction", that acts "on 2 different objects", "same type of force", "same period of time")

F3 The two forces of a pair act on different objects, so they never cancel each other out: each one acts on a different object. https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion ("These forces act on different bodies and thus do not cancel each other out within a single body"); https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-5-newtons-third-law (the forces act on different systems; "Thus, they do not cancel each other."); WHRO horse-and-cart brainteaser, same PDF as F2 ("while force pairs are equal and opposite, they are acting on different objects")

F4 Forces cancel only when they act on the same object: balanced forces on one object add up to zero and leave its motion unchanged, as in a tug-of-war that does not move. Two equal, opposite forces on one object (a book's weight and the table's support) are balanced forces, not a third-law pair; the partner of Earth's pull on the book is the book's pull on Earth. https://grokipedia.com/page/Net_force ("A net force of zero results in no change in the object's velocity"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion (book on a table: the reaction to Earth's pull on the book is the book's gravitational pull on Earth, not the table's support force); T1 neighbour `pushes-pulls-and-gravity` F4-F5 (tug-of-war)

F5 A force pair is always the same two objects acting on each other: object A on object B, and B back on A. When a hammer drives a nail, the nail pushes back on the hammer, equal and opposite to the hammer's push on the nail. A force from a third object (the board on the nail, the hand on the hammer) belongs to a different pair. https://www.pearson.com/channels/physics/asset/47121665/when-a-hammer-strikes-a-nail-and-drives-it-in ("The nail exerts an equal and opposite force on the hammer as the hammer exerts on the nail"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion ("if one body exerts a force on a second body, the second body is also exerting a force on the first body"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps2-1-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions (PS2.A: "For any pair of interacting objects, the force exerted by the first object on the second object is equal in strength to the force that the second object exerts on the first, but in the opposite direction")

F6 Still, solid things push back too: a wall pushed on pushes back on the one pushing. A rider on a skateboard (or skates) who shoves a wall rolls away from it, because the wall pushes on him. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-5-newtons-third-law ("When you push on a wall, the wall pushes back on you."); https://www.alloprof.qc.ca/en/students/vl/physics/newton-s-third-law-of-motion-p1090 (a person on a skateboard: "If he pushes on the wall, he will move away from it, because the wall has exerted a force on him.")

F7 A swimmer's arms and hands push the water backward, and the water pushes the swimmer forward just as hard; that forward push from the water moves her. https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion (the swimmer pushes water backward and "the water exerts an equal forward force on the swimmer (reaction)"); https://www.alloprof.qc.ca/en/students/vl/physics/newton-s-third-law-of-motion-p1090 (to move, a swimmer "must exert a thrust with his arms and legs in the opposite sense"); https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-5-newtons-third-law (a swimmer pushing off a pool wall "accelerates in the direction opposite that of her push")

F8 A rocket pushes hot gas out of its engine, and the gas pushes the rocket the opposite way: out the back, so the rocket is pushed forward (or up from the launch pad). https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/rocket/TRCRocket/rocket_principles.html ("A rocket can lift off from a launch pad only when it expels gas out of its engine"; "The rocket pushes on the gas, and the gas in turn pushes on the rocket."); https://grokipedia.com/page/Rocket ("the force generated by accelerating the exhaust gases rearward creates an equal and opposite thrust forward")

F9 A rocket does not push against the air or the ground. It works in the vacuum of space, and works better there than in air, because its exhaust escapes freely. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-5-newtons-third-law ("It is a common misconception that rockets propel themselves by pushing on the ground or on the air behind them. They actually work better in a vacuum, where they can more readily expel the exhaust gases."); https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/rocket/TRCRocket/rocket_principles.html ("rockets actually work better in space than they do in air"; "In space, the exhaust gases can escape freely."); https://grokipedia.com/page/Rocket (rockets carry fuel and oxidizer, "enabling operation in both atmospheric and vacuum environments")

F10 An unsigned New York Times editorial of 13 January 1920 mocked the rocket pioneer Robert Goddard, saying a rocket needed "something better than a vacuum against which to react". During the Apollo 11 mission in July 1969 the Times printed a correction: "it is now definitely established that a rocket can function in a vacuum. The 'Times' regrets the error." (Grokipedia gives the correction's date as 17 July in one place and 20 July in another; both fall during the flight.) https://grokipedia.com/page/Robert_H._Goddard

F11 Air is squeezed inside a blown-up balloon by its rubber walls; when the neck is let go, air escapes through it and the balloon is pushed the opposite way. https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/rocket/TRCRocket/rocket_principles.html ("Air inside a balloon is compressed by the balloon's rubber walls."; "When the nozzle is released, air escapes through it and the balloon is propelled in the opposite direction.")

F12 A balloon blown up with a person's breath does not float, because breath is not lighter than the room's air; a helium balloon floats because helium is less dense than air. https://childrensmuseumatlanta.org/?p=3052 ("a balloon that you fill up with your own breath will not float"); https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-heavy-is-your-air/ ("The air we exhale is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, and only about 4 to 5 percent carbon dioxide"; "helium is much lighter (or less dense) than the air in our environment"); https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/2129 (a helium balloon "will float high up, because it has a lower density than air does")

F13 The two forces are equal however different the objects are: when a bug hits the windshield of a speeding car, the bug and the windshield feel exactly the same size of force, in opposite directions; a bat and a ball push on each other equally hard. https://digitallearning.whro.org/courses/physics/mod5/top2/documents/mod5top2_content_newtonsthird.pdf (bug on a Ferrari's windshield: "they experience exactly the same magnitude force, in opposite directions"; bat and ball: "the magnitude of the force of the baseball bat on the ball is exactly the same as the magnitude of the force of the ball on the bat"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps2-1-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions (PS2.A: "equal in strength")

F14 Equal forces can have very different effects: the same force changes the motion of a light object a lot and of a heavy object only a little. A cannon and its ball are pushed equally hard, but the heavy cannon only rolls back a metre or two; a bug is squashed while a truck barely slows. https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/rocket/TRCRocket/rocket_principles.html (cannon "pushed backward a meter or two"; same force, the cannon's large mass gives a small acceleration); https://www.cpalms.org/PreviewStandard/PrintStandard/1740 (Florida SC.5.P.13.3: "the more mass an object has, the less effect a given force will have on the object's motion")

F15 Gravity comes in pairs too: Earth pulls a falling apple down, and the apple pulls Earth up with an equal force. Earth's mass is vastly larger than the apple's, so Earth's motion toward the apple is far too small to notice. https://grokipedia.com/page/Reaction_(physics) ("Earth exerts a downward gravitational force on the apple (the action)"; "the apple exerts an equal and opposite upward gravitational force on Earth (the reaction)"; Earth's tiny acceleration is due to its mass, "vastly larger than the apple's"); https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-5-newtons-third-law (a boy whom Earth pulls down "pulls upward on Earth" with an equal and opposite force)

F16 Walking and driving use the same pair: a walker's feet push backward on the floor and the floor pushes the walker forward; a car's tyres push backward on the road and the road pushes the car forward. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-4-newtons-third-law-of-motion-symmetry-in-forces ("the ground pushes forward on the drive wheels in reaction to the drive wheels pushing backward on the ground"; a professor walking "exerts a force backward on the floor")

F17 Helicopters push air down and are pushed up; two skaters who push on each other both roll apart. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-4-newtons-third-law-of-motion-symmetry-in-forces ("Helicopters similarly create lift by pushing air down, thereby experiencing an upward reaction force."); https://grokipedia.com/page/Reaction_(physics) (two ice skaters pushing each other: "resulting in mutual acceleration away from the point of contact")

F18 Objects do not store up force: a force is an interaction between two objects that lasts only while they act on each other. https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_3rd/forces/gr3.forces_common_student_ideas.pdf (CPP and BSCS RESPeCT: "Objects don't contain force, so they can't run out of force. All forces are interactions (a push or pull, a touch or tug) between two objects.")

F19 Earth's spin does not hold objects in place or set their weight (a belief recorded among students; weight is gravity's pull). https://wordpressua.uark.edu/scimap/?p=778 (Stover and Saunders 2000 list the student idea "The weight of an object is related to the Earth's rate of rotation" as a misconception); https://grokipedia.com/page/Weight ("weight is the force exerted on an object by the gravitational attraction of a massive body")

F20 The grade: Texas puts Newton's third law at grade 6 (TEKS 6.7C, quoted at F2); NGSS teaches it in middle school (MS-PS2-1: "Apply Newton's Third Law to design a solution to a problem involving the motion of two colliding objects"); the curriculum map's G6.P7 names the swimmer, a rocket's exhaust and a book on a table, and that the two forces act on different objects and do not cancel. A year earlier, TEKS 5.7B names "a balloon rocket on a string" as a force investigation (quoted in the T1 neighbour's fact sheet, F11, from https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-7). https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-26; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps2-1-motion-and-stability-forces-and-interactions; bankbuild/science_refresh/curriculum_map.json (G6.P7)

## Misconceptions a 6th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 The two forces of a pair cancel each other out. Grokipedia: "A common misconception is that the action and reaction forces cancel one another." https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion ; Grokipedia Reaction (physics): "A common misconception among students is that the equal and opposite forces" are cancelled. https://grokipedia.com/page/Reaction_(physics)

M2 The heavier object, or the more active (faster) one, pushes harder in a collision. Wells, Henderson, Traxler, Miller and Stewart, "Exploring the Structure of Misconceptions in the Force and Motion Conceptual Evaluation with Modified Module Analysis" (2020): "greater mass implies greater force" and "the most active agent produces the greatest force", the misconceptions that "the heavy truck would exert a greater force on the small car" and "the object that is moving the fastest would produce the greatest force". https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00967

M3 Still, lifeless objects (a wall, a table) cannot push. CPP and BSCS RESPeCT: "Some students believe that inert objects can alter another object's motion, such as a wall stopping a ball thrown at it, but they can't exert a force"; "Inanimate or inert objects (like a table or chair) don't exert force." https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_3rd/forces/gr3.forces_common_student_ideas.pdf

M4 A rocket moves by pushing on the ground or the air behind it, so it could not work in empty space. OpenStax: "It is a common misconception that rockets propel themselves by pushing on the ground or on the air behind them." https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-5-newtons-third-law ; the 1920 Times editorial (F10) made the same mistake.

M5 A push is stored up in the thing pushed and carries it along. RESPeCT: "An object stops because it runs out of force or energy" is listed as a common student idea; "Objects don't contain force". https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_3rd/forces/gr3.forces_common_student_ideas.pdf

M6 Earth's spin holds things in place (F19, Stover and Saunders via SCIMAP).

M7 A blown-up balloon rises because the air inside is light (author's reading: a helium balloon does float, F12; no survey found that quotes this belief for breath-filled balloons).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F21 When one of two people on skates or skateboards pushes the other, both move apart; when one pulls on a rope the other only holds, both move toward each other, because the other must apply an equal and opposite force at the same moment. https://web.physics.ucsb.edu/~lecturedemonstrations/Composer/Pages/12.57.html (UCSB demo 12.57, "Skateboard push-off and pull-together": "Have one student push against the other, and they both move apart"; with a rope, one student pulls and both move toward each other while the other keeps holding on; the other student "must simultaneously apply an equal and opposite force"); https://www.alloprof.qc.ca/en/students/vl/physics/newton-s-third-law-of-motion-p1090 ("The left skater (Skater A) pushes the right skater (Skater B) to the right." "Both skaters will start moving.")

F22 The two forces of a pair are perfectly simultaneous; the words "action" and "reaction" wrongly suggest that one comes after the other. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_(physics) ("The terms 'action' and 'reaction' have the misleading suggestion of causality"; "It is therefore easy to think of the second force as being there because of the first, and even happening some time after the first. This is incorrect; the forces are perfectly simultaneous"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Reaction_(physics) ("the second body simultaneously exerts a force"; modern interpretations reject "any notion of one preceding or dominating the other")

F23 Forces between the parts of one body (a system) cancel, so a swimmer's arms cannot move her by pulling on her own body; what moves a swimmer is the water's push on her, the partner of her push on the water. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-4-newtons-third-law-of-motion-symmetry-in-forces ("Thus internal forces (between components of a system) cancel."; a swimmer pushing off the pool wall: "The wall has exerted an equal and opposite force back on the swimmer."); F7

M8 The reaction comes after the action, in answer to it (F22: Wikipedia names it; a search also turned up PMT Education's note that students think the reaction acts after the action has taken place).

M9 A body moves itself with its own muscles, without needing anything outside it to push on (the judge's reading of the classic belief; no survey quoted). F23 answers it.

M10 Two equal pulls on a rope balance, so nothing moves (M1 carried over from the T1 tug-of-war, `pushes-pulls-and-gravity`, where balanced pulls leave the rope still). F21 answers it: the pulls act on two different skaters.
