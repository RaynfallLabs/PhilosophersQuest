## The giant planets

F1. Gas giants (Jupiter and Saturn) are made mostly of hydrogen and helium, more than 90 percent of their mass, and have no solid surface. The term "gas giant" was coined in 1952 by the science-fiction writer James Blish.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gas_giant ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_giant

F2. Ice giants (Uranus and Neptune) are only about 20 percent hydrogen and helium by mass. Most of their mass (NASA: "80% or more" for Uranus) is a hot, dense fluid of "icy" materials, water, methane and ammonia, above a small rocky core. They have no true solid surface: NASA says Uranus "doesn't have a true surface" and is "mostly swirling fluids".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice_giant ; https://science.nasa.gov/uranus/facts/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_giant

F3. Why "ice": in planetary science "ices" are compounds such as water, ammonia and methane, with freezing points above about 100 K, which were solids when the planets formed. The name is about what the planets are made of, not their temperature: deep inside, the ices are a hot fluid near 5,000 K at the core-mantle boundary.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_giant ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice_giant

F4. Jupiter and Saturn have far more moons than Uranus and Neptune: NASA says "dozens of moons swarm around" Jupiter and Saturn "has more moons than any other planet", while Uranus has 28 known moons and Neptune 16. (Counts change as moons are found; not keyed.)
    https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/moons/ ; https://science.nasa.gov/uranus/facts/

## Moons beyond our own

F5. Galileo Galilei found Jupiter's four largest moons in January 1610 with a telescope of about 20 times magnification. On 7 January he took three of them for fixed stars near Jupiter; within days he saw they moved and concluded they circled Jupiter. They were the first moons found going round a planet other than Earth, a blow to the Earth-centred (Ptolemaic) system. Simon Marius proposed the names Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto in 1614.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Galilean_moons ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_moons ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Europa_(moon)

F6. Of the four Galilean moons, Ganymede is the largest moon in the solar system (5,268 km across, larger than Mercury), and Io is the most volcanically active body in the solar system, with hot melted rock erupting from over 400 active volcanoes.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Galilean_moons

F7. Europa's surface is a smooth, bright crust of water ice crossed by dark reddish-brown lines (cracks) and has few craters, a sign of a young surface. Evidence strongly suggests a global ocean of salty liquid water beneath the ice shell; NASA says it may hold about twice as much water as all Earth's oceans.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Europa_(moon) ; https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/ ; https://science.nasa.gov/mission/europa-clipper/why-europa-evidence-for-an-ocean/

F8. The evidence for Europa's ocean came largely from the Galileo spacecraft: on a flyby in January 2000 its magnetometer measured changes in Europa's magnetic field that fit a layer of electrically conducting material, such as salty water, near the surface. The ocean is not yet directly confirmed. NASA calls Europa one of the best places to look for environments where life could exist beyond Earth, because liquid water is one of life's needs.
    https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/galileo-findings-boost-idea-of-other-worldly-ocean/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Europa_(moon) ; https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/

F9. Titan, Saturn's largest moon (found by Christiaan Huygens in 1655, larger than Mercury), is the only moon with a thick atmosphere: about 95 percent nitrogen and 5 percent methane, pressing on the ground about 1.5 times as hard as Earth's air. The Huygens probe, built by the European Space Agency, landed on Titan on 14 January 2005 after a descent of 2 hours 27 minutes, under parachutes for most of it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Titan_(moon) ; https://science.nasa.gov/saturn/moons/titan/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Huygens_(spacecraft)

F10. Titan is the only place besides Earth known to have liquids on its surface: clouds, rain, rivers, lakes and seas of liquid methane and ethane. Its surface averages about -179 degrees Celsius; its "rock" is water ice, and methane, a gas on Earth, is liquid in the cold.
    https://science.nasa.gov/saturn/moons/titan/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Titan_(moon)

## Asteroids and the belt

F11. Asteroids are rocky leftovers from the formation of the solar system about 4.6 billion years ago, made mainly of rock (clay and silicate rocks) and metal (nickel-iron). Most circle the Sun in the main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.
    https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/asteroids/facts/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Asteroid_belt

F12. Giuseppe Piazzi, director of the observatory at Palermo in Sicily, found Ceres on 1 January 1801 while charting stars: a moving, starlike dot. He first took it for a comet, but it showed no fuzzy head or tail. Astronomers were then hunting a "missing planet" in the gap between Mars and Jupiter that the Titius-Bode rule predicted (a group nicknamed the Celestial Police split the sky into zones to search).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ceres_(dwarf_planet) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceres_(dwarf_planet)

F13. Ceres was lost in the Sun's glare after Piazzi had followed it for about 40 nights. Carl Friedrich Gauss, then 24, worked out a new mathematical way to compute its path from the few observations, and astronomers found it again near his predicted place at the end of 1801 (Grokipedia: Franz Xaver von Zach on 7 December; Wikipedia: von Zach and Heinrich Olbers on 31 December). Not keyed by date.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ceres_(dwarf_planet) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceres_(dwarf_planet)

F14. Ceres was listed as a planet for over half a century, then classed as an asteroid as more such bodies were found, and in 2006 the IAU made it a dwarf planet. It is the largest object in the asteroid belt, about 950 km across, holding about a third of the belt's mass (Grokipedia "about one-third"; Wikipedia 39 percent).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ceres_(dwarf_planet) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceres_(dwarf_planet) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt

F15. More small bodies soon turned up in the same gap: Pallas (found by Heinrich Olbers, 28 March 1802), Juno (Karl Harding, 1 September 1804) and Vesta (Olbers, 29 March 1807); then no more were found for 38 years. After Pallas was found, Olbers proposed that Ceres and Pallas were fragments of a planet that had been destroyed (the disrupted-planet idea), and it gained a following. It has been superseded: the belt is now understood as leftover planetesimals that never came together into a planet, because Jupiter's gravity stirred them up. The belt's low total mass and the asteroids' differing chemistry also tell against one shattered parent planet.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaeton_(hypothetical_planet) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Asteroid_belt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt ; https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/march-28-1802-heinrich-olbers-discovers-pallas/ ; https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/sept-1-1804-karl-harding-spots-3-juno/ ; https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/march-29-1807-heinrich-olbers-discovers-vesta/

F16. The belt holds hundreds of thousands of asteroids at least 1 km across (Wikipedia: 700,000 to 1.7 million), yet all of them together have only about 3 to 4 percent of the mass of Earth's Moon; NASA: "The total mass of all the asteroids combined is less than that of Earth's Moon."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Asteroid_belt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt ; https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/asteroids/facts/

F17. The belt is mostly empty: on average the asteroids are about 965,600 km (600,000 miles) apart. Pioneer 10 was the first spacecraft to cross it (July 1972 to February 1973) and met no fragment larger than a millimetre; many craft have crossed since without incident.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pioneer_10 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pioneer_10

F18. William Herschel proposed the word "asteroid", from Greek for "star-like", in 1802, because Ceres and Pallas looked like points of light, like stars, in a telescope.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt

## Comets

F19. Comets are small icy bodies: NASA calls them "cosmic snowballs of frozen gases, rock, and dust". Unlike asteroids, which are mainly rock and metal with little ice, comets are mostly ice.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Comet ; https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/

F20. Far from the Sun a comet is just its small solid nucleus. As it nears the Sun (typically within about 2 AU), the Sun's heat makes its ices sublimate, turning straight from solid to gas, and the gas carries dust out with it; together they form the glowing head (coma) and the tails. NASA: "it heats up and spews dust and gases into a giant glowing head larger than most planets." The comet is not burning: the glow is sunlight on the dust and light given off by the gas.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Comet ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Comet_tail ; https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/

F21. A comet's tails point away from the Sun because they are pushed: sunlight's radiation pressure pushes the dust (a broad, curved dust tail), and the solar wind, a stream of charged particles from the Sun, sweeps the ionized gas into a narrow, straight ion tail. Tails can stretch for millions of kilometres.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Comet_tail ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet_tail ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Comet

F22. The tail points away from the Sun whichever way the comet is moving, so a comet heading back out from the Sun travels tail first. NASA StarChild: "When a comet approaches the Sun, its head leads the way, but as it travels away from the Sun, the tail is in front." astro4edu (IAU): "If the comet itself is moving away from the Sun, the ion tail precedes the comet."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Comet_tail ; https://starchild.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/StarChild/solar_system_level2/comets_answer.html ; https://www.astro4edu.org/resources/glossary/term/64/

F23. Where comets come from: short-period comets (orbits under 200 years) mostly come from the Kuiper belt, a flat band of icy bodies beyond Neptune, from about 30 AU out to about 50 AU; long-period comets come from the Oort cloud, a vast, roughly spherical shell of icy bodies thousands to perhaps 100,000 AU from the Sun. Jan Oort proposed the cloud in 1950 from long-period comets' paths; it has never been seen directly.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Comet ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kuiper_belt ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Oort_cloud ; https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/comets/

F24. Older ideas of comets: Aristotle taught they were "windy exhalations" set alight high in Earth's air, and this stayed the usual view for centuries; Tycho Brahe showed by measurement in 1577 that comets lie beyond the Moon. Even in Halley's day many people doubted that comets went round the Sun and assumed they followed straight paths through the solar system, passing only once.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Comet ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halley%27s_Comet

F25. Edmond Halley, an English astronomer, found in 1705 that the comets seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682 had nearly the same paths, and concluded they were one comet returning about every 76 years. He predicted its return in 1758 and died in 1742, before it came back. Johann Georg Palitzsch, a German farmer and amateur astronomer, saw it on Christmas night, 25 December 1758. It was the first comet known to return, the first successful forecast of a comet's return, and the first time anything but planets was shown to go round the Sun. Its next return is expected in 2061.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Halley's_Comet ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halley%27s_Comet

F26. Comets can also break apart, and their fragments then follow nearly the same path: the Kreutz sungrazers, with very similar orbits, are thought to descend from one large parent comet that broke up. (A respectable rival reading of comets on a shared path; Halley's comet is one body.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kreutz_sungrazer

## Meteoroids, meteors and meteorites

F27. A meteoroid is a small rocky or metallic body in space, from a dust grain up to about 1 metre across, much smaller than an asteroid; many are chips of asteroids or dust shed by comets. A meteor is the streak of light seen when a meteoroid plunges into Earth's air at high speed (11 to 72 km a second) and burns up; people call it a "shooting star", though it is no star. A meteorite is a piece that survives the fall and reaches the ground.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Meteoroid ; https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/meteors-meteorites/

F28. Almost all incoming material is vaporized in the air, so meteorites are only the rare survivors. A meteor shower comes when Earth passes through the trail of dusty debris a comet has left; every Perseid meteor in August is a tiny piece of comet Swift-Tuttle.
    https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/meteors-meteorites/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Meteoroid

## Dwarf planets and Pluto

F29. Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto at Lowell Observatory in Arizona on 18 February 1930, and it was counted as the ninth planet until 2006. Pluto orbits the Sun at an average of about 39 AU, beyond Neptune, in the Kuiper belt; it is round, about two-thirds as wide as Earth's Moon, and has five moons, the largest Charon.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pluto ; https://science.nasa.gov/dwarf-planets/pluto/

F30. In 2005 Mike Brown, Chad Trujillo and David Rabinowitz announced Eris, a body beyond Neptune about 27 percent more massive than Pluto, which NASA at first called the tenth planet. Its discovery pushed the International Astronomical Union (IAU) to define "planet" for the first time.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eris_(dwarf_planet) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/IAU_definition_of_planet

F31. On 24 August 2006 in Prague, the IAU ruled that a planet must (1) orbit the Sun, (2) have enough mass for its own gravity to pull it nearly round, and (3) have cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. Pluto passes the first two but fails the third: it shares its zone with many other icy Kuiper belt bodies. A dwarf planet meets (1) and (2) but not (3); the IAU recognizes five: Ceres, Pluto, Haumea, Makemake and Eris. There is no rule about distance from the Sun.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/IAU_definition_of_planet ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pluto ; https://science.nasa.gov/dwarf-planets/

## Misconceptions a 7th-grader brings (honest wrong choices)

M1. The asteroid belt is a crowded field of tumbling rocks that a ship must dodge (film imagery). Wikipedia: "Contrary to popular imagery, the asteroid belt is mostly empty." (F16, F17)
M2. A comet's tail streams out behind it like smoke or a jet's trail, set by its motion; it is set by the Sun (F21, F22).
M3. A comet glows because it is on fire, burning in the Sun's heat; its ice is turning to gas (F20).
M4. A shooting star is a star falling; a meteor is a meteoroid burning up in Earth's air (F27). Students also mix up meteor and meteorite.
M5. Ice giants are frozen solid or covered by a crust of ice; their "ices" are a hot fluid and they have no solid surface (F2, F3).
M6. Pluto was demoted because it is too small to be round or too far from the Sun; it is round, there is no distance rule, and it fails only the "cleared its neighbourhood" rule (F29, F31).
M7. The asteroid belt is the wreck of an exploded planet (Olbers, 1802): a respectable theory of its day, now superseded (F15).
M8. Comets are weather high in Earth's air (Aristotle), or pass through once on straight paths (F24): the older views Halley's comet overturned.
