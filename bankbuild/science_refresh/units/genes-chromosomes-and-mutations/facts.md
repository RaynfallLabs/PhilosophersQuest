## Where genes sit

F1. A chromosome is "a thread-like structure composed of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and proteins"; chromosomes "reside in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells", "encapsulating genes along their linear DNA molecules". The nucleus houses "the cell's genetic material in the form of DNA organized into chromosomes".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chromosome ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_nucleus

F2. A gene is "a region of DNA that codes for a functional product, such as a protein"; "Genes provide the instructions for building and maintaining an organism's cells." NHGRI: a gene is "the unit of DNA that carries the instructions for making a specific protein"; humans have roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes (Grokipedia: about 19,400 to 20,000, "distributed across 23 pairs of chromosomes"; NHGRI: an estimated 20,000 to 25,000).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gene ; https://www.genome.gov/about-genomics/fact-sheets/A-Brief-Guide-to-Genomics

F3. "Each human chromosome carries hundreds to thousands of genes" (the X carries about 900). NGSS LS3.A (grades 6-8): "Genes are located in the chromosomes of cells, with each chromosome pair containing two variants of each of many distinct genes."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chromosome ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ls3-1-heredity-inheritance-and-variation-traits

F4. Human body cells hold 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes, XX in females and XY in males. Each parent contributes half of the set. NGSS LS3.B: "Individuals have two of each chromosome and hence two alleles of each gene, one acquired from each parent. These versions may be identical or may differ from each other."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chromosome ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Karyotype ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ls3-2-heredity-inheritance-and-variation-traits

F5. "Virtually every single cell in the body contains a complete copy" of the human genome (NHGRI). Mature red blood cells and platelets have no nucleus (Grokipedia, Cell nucleus); mammalian red blood cells carry no genome. A buccal smear collects cells from the inside of the cheek, chiefly for chromosome or DNA analysis (MedlinePlus).
    https://www.genome.gov/about-genomics/fact-sheets/A-Brief-Guide-to-Genomics ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cell_nucleus ; https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003414.htm

F6. Eye colour "is primarily determined by the amount and distribution of melanin pigments in the iris"; it is "a polygenic trait influenced by multiple genes", with OCA2 and HERC2 on chromosome 15 playing key roles; blue eyes "contain minimal melanin".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Eye_color

## Walter Sutton (and Theodor Boveri): genes on chromosomes

F7. Walter Sutton (1877-1916) went to Columbia University in 1901 to work under Edmund Beecher Wilson; he studied the lubber grasshopper Brachystola magna, "whose large chromosomes facilitated detailed observation" of meiosis. In spring 1902, aged 25, he concluded that chromosomes are the basis of heredity (DNA from the Beginning). He saw that chromosomes keep "a distinct morphological individuality", with "consistent sizes and shapes", in homologous pairs, "one from each parent", and that the reduction division distributes them to the gametes (sex cells), one of each pair to each; this "paralleled the behavior of Mendel's hypothetical factors". His paper "The Chromosomes in Heredity" (Biological Bulletin, 1903) set out the chromosome theory. Theodor Boveri reached the theory independently in 1902; his sea urchins showed "all the chromosomes had to be present for proper embryonic development".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Walter_Sutton ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Boveri%E2%80%93Sutton_chromosome_theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boveri%E2%80%93Sutton_chromosome_theory ; https://dnaftb.org/8/bio-2.html ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/walter-stanborough-sutton-1877-1916

F8. Sutton argued in 1903 that a single chromosome must carry many factors: otherwise "the number of distinct characters possessed by an individual could not exceed the number of chromosomes in the germ-products; which is undoubtedly contrary to fact. We must, therefore, assume that some chromosomes at least are related to a number of different allelomorphs" (allelomorphs: his word for Mendel's paired factors). Russell (1930), quoting Wilson's account, gives the same sentence, "some chromosomes at least are related to a number of different allelomorphs", and "all the allelomorphs represented by any one chromosome must be inherited together".
    Sutton 1903 (scan): https://www.nature.com/scitable/content/16712/Sutton_1903.pdf ; https://embryology.med.unsw.edu.au/embryology/index.php/Russell1930_5 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Boveri%E2%80%93Sutton_chromosome_theory

F9. Thomas Hunt Morgan doubted Mendelism and doubted "that chromosomes served as the physical bearers of hereditary determinants" in the early 1900s. In his Columbia fly room ("a compact 16-by-23-foot space cluttered with hundreds of milk bottles"), he found in 1910 "a spontaneous white-eyed mutation in a single male" fruit fly; the white-eye allele proved to ride on the X chromosome, "empirical support for genes residing on chromosomes". Fruit flies have "a short generation time of approximately 10 days" and four chromosome pairs (2n = 8). Morgan won the 1933 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Hermann Muller joined his group in 1912.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thomas_Hunt_Morgan ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Karyotype ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Joseph_Muller

## The human count: Tjio and Levan

F10. Theophilus Painter reported 48 chromosomes in human cells in the early 1920s (Grokipedia: 1923; Wikipedia: 1922). He had been unsure, "at first favoring 46", then "revised his opinion from 46 to 48"; he counted 46 on some slides and 48 on others. The accepted number, "given for some 30 years, was 48" (NHGRI). Earlier counts were hard because chromosomes "clump together", overlap and tangle, and Painter's thin slices cut some into pieces (Science History Institute). The other great apes have 48 chromosomes; human chromosome 2 formed from the fusion of two ancestral ape chromosomes.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Karyotype ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karyotype ; https://www.genome.gov/25520285/online-education-kit-1955-a-46-human-chromosomes ; https://sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/joe-hin-tjio-counts-chromosomes

F11. Joe Hin Tjio, a visiting scientist in Albert Levan's laboratory at the Institute of Genetics of the University of Lund in Sweden, made the count on 22 December 1955. His cells were human cells "grown in tissue culture" (embryonic lung fibroblasts, NHGRI). The method: pretreatment "in a hypotonic solution, which swells the cells and spreads the chromosomes"; colchicine "to arrest mitosis at metaphase" (stopping dividing cells when the chromosomes are most compact); squashing so the chromosomes lie in one plane. "Every intact cell he examined had 46 chromosomes" (NHGRI); he counted more than 250 cells. Tjio and Levan published "The chromosome number of man" in Hereditas 42: 1-6 (1956).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Hin_Tjio ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karyotype ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Karyotype ; https://www.genome.gov/25520285/online-education-kit-1955-a-46-human-chromosomes ; https://sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/joe-hin-tjio-counts-chromosomes

## One set from each parent; why siblings differ

F12. NGSS LS3.B (grades 6-8): "In sexually reproducing organisms, each parent contributes half of the genes acquired (at random) by the offspring." LS3.A: "Variations of inherited traits between parent and offspring arise from genetic differences that result from the subset of chromosomes (and therefore genes) inherited."
    https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ls3-2-heredity-inheritance-and-variation-traits

F13. Full siblings inherit half their DNA from each parent and share about 50 percent of their DNA on average (one study: 49.8 percent, range 37.4 to 61.7), because each gets a different combination of the parents' DNA; identical twins are the exception and share all of their DNA.
    https://www.thetech.org/ask-a-geneticist/siblings-are-around-fifty-percent-related ; https://www.thetech.org/ask-a-geneticist/ask138

## X and Y: Nettie Stevens

F14. Nettie Stevens, at Bryn Mawr College, published Studies in Spermatogenesis (Carnegie Institution, 1905) on the mealworm Tenebrio. A female mealworm's body cells hold 20 large chromosomes; a male's hold 19 large and one small one. She concluded that sex is settled at the start of life by the chromosomes received; the small chromosome is the one now called the Y. Before her work, "the prevailing view" was that an animal's sex "is not determined at the time of fertilization" but is set later by the environment in which the embryo develops (Linda Hall Library); Grokipedia speaks of "environmental or cytoplasmic theories of sex determination prevalent at the time". Edmund Wilson found similar chromosomes in other insects at the same time.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nettie_Stevens ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nettie_Stevens ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/nettie-maria-stevens ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/nettie-maria-stevens-1861-1912

F15. In humans, females are XX and males XY. The mother always passes on an X; the father passes on an X or a Y, so "the father determines the sex of the child". A single gene on the Y chromosome, SRY, "acts as a signal to trigger male" development.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XY_sex-determination_system ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chromosome

## Mutations

F16. A mutation is "a permanent alteration in the nucleotide sequence of the genome" (a change in the DNA). Most mutations "are neutral or deleterious rather than beneficial"; mutations are "the ultimate source of genetic novelty in evolution". Causes include errors in DNA copying, ionizing radiation such as X-rays, ultraviolet light and some chemicals. Germline mutations are heritable; somatic ones, in body cells, are not passed on. NGSS LS3.B: mutations add variation beyond what sexual reproduction makes, and "Some changes are beneficial, others harmful, and some neutral to the organism."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mutation ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ls3-1-heredity-inheritance-and-variation-traits

F17. Hermann Muller began exposing fruit flies to X-rays in 1926 at the University of Texas in Austin, announced the results at the Fifth International Congress of Genetics in Berlin in 1927, and published "The Artificial Transmutation of the Gene" in Science (22 July 1927). He bred the X-rayed flies and examined their offspring against untreated controls: 88 lethal mutations in 758 cultures, against 1 in 947 in the controls; across experiments, lethal mutations about 150 times the spontaneous rate. The visible mutations X-rays caused "were the same as visible mutations that occurred spontaneously", such as white eyes, small wings and bobbed bristles (ASU Embryo Project); Grokipedia: effects resembling natural processes but much faster, and "predominantly deleterious". In his Nobel lecture Muller called "the great majority of mutations" undesirable. Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 1946, "for the production of mutations by means of X-ray irradiation".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hermann_Joseph_Muller ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/hermann-joseph-mullers-study-x-rays-mutagen-1926-1927 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Joseph_Muller

## Sickle-cell disease: harmful, and a helpful side

F18. Sickle-cell disease is caused by "a single nucleotide substitution" in the beta-globin gene (HBB), a gene for haemoglobin; a person has the disease on inheriting the changed gene from both parents (two copies); one copy gives sickle-cell trait, usually without symptoms. The cells are "rigid, sickle-shaped cells" that "obstruct small vessels", causing anaemia and bouts of severe pain. Where there is little health care, "between 50% and 90% of children born with the disease die before the age of 5 years" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sickle_cell_disease ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle_cell_disease ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle_cell_trait

F19. James Herrick of Chicago published the first description in 1910 ("Peculiar elongated and sickle-shaped red blood corpuscles in a case of severe anemia", Archives of Internal Medicine). His intern Ernest Irons examined the blood of the patient, Walter Clement Noel, "a 20-year-old first-year dental student from Grenada". In 1949 E. A. Beet and J. V. Neel worked out how the disease is inherited.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sickle_cell_disease ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle_cell_disease ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_B._Herrick

F20. Carriers of one copy (sickle-cell trait) have "a survival advantage against severe Plasmodium falciparum malaria" (Grokipedia; studies estimate 50-90 percent protection against severe outcomes). Anthony Allison tested nearly 5,000 East Africans and reported in 1954 (British Medical Journal, "Protection afforded by sickle-cell trait against subtertian malareal infection"): the trait among people of coastal areas was "higher than 20%", and "children with heterozygous trait had significantly low number of parasites in their blood"; he concluded the trait "confers resistance to malaria". The gene's distribution in Africa and malaria's "virtually overlap". People with two copies are less often infected with malaria but, "once infected, they are more likely to develop severe and life-threatening anaemia" (so no question says two copies protect).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sickle_cell_trait ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Clifford_Allison ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle_cell_trait ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle_cell_disease

## A new variety from one changed gene: the nectarine

F21. Nectarines "belong to the same species as peaches" (Prunus persica) and differ in a smooth, fuzzless skin, "a recessive gene mutation inhibiting fuzz development" (Grokipedia, Peach). Vendramin and colleagues (PLoS ONE, 2014): the peach/nectarine trait is monogenic; an inserted retroelement in the gene PpeMYB25 causes "the recessive glabrous phenotype"; "a unique mutational event gave rise to the nectarine trait"; nectarines have been known in China "for over 2,000 years". Nectarines are "often erroneously believed to be a crossbreed between peaches and plums" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Peach ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nectarine ; https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0090574

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Genes are found only in certain cells, "commonly in the blood, brain or reproductive system"; different cells contain different genes (Science Learning Hub). In fact every cell but the mature red blood cells and the gametes holds the same DNA, and cells differ by the genes they use.
    https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/216-alternative-conceptions-about-genetics
M2. Genes and chromosomes are taken to be unrelated, or the same thing (Lewis and Wood-Robinson 2000, as reported by later reviews).
    https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/216-alternative-conceptions-about-genetics
M3. Inheritance is an averaging or blending of the parents' genes (Science Learning Hub).
M4. Traits travel in the blood: Darwin's pangenesis (1868) had "every unit or cell of an organism's body" throw off gemmules that carry its traits; Francis Galton's blood transfusions between rabbits in 1870-71 "failed to detect circulating gemmules", and the idea was supplanted by the 1890s.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pangenesis
M5. The two chromosomes of a pair are taken for a chromosome and its copy; wrong by F4 (one from each parent).
M6. The nectarine as a peach-plum cross (F21).
M7. Traits gained in life are inherited (the T3 blacksmith); mutations are made to order by the surroundings that would favour them (malaria "making" the sickle-cell gene); wrong by F16 and F20 (the gene passes from parent to child, and mutation is a change in DNA from copying errors, radiation or chemicals).

- Painter's year: 1922 (Wikipedia) or 1923 (Grokipedia). No question gives the year; the context says "the early 1920s".
- The tissue Tjio used (embryonic lung cells) is left out of the questions; "human cells grown in the laboratory" is enough.
- Stevens's mealworm conclusion is told through sex cells and fertilization in her sources; the question keeps to the human pair and the parents' chromosomes, and the context gives her counts.
