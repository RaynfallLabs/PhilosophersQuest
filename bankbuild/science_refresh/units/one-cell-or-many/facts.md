## One cell that does everything

F1. A unicellular (one-celled) organism is a single cell that does every job of life by itself: taking in food and energy, growing, reproducing, getting rid of wastes and responding to its surroundings. Examples: bacteria, the amoeba, the paramecium, one-celled algae such as diatoms, and yeast. Most are microscopic. Grokipedia: "a living entity composed of a single cell that independently performs all essential functions of life"; CK-12: "Single-celled organisms are able to carry out all the processes of life without help from other cells."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Unicellular_organism ; https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Introductory_and_General_Biology/Introductory_Biology_(CK-12)/02%3A_Cell_Biology/2.12%3A_Organization_of_Cells ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/unicellular-vs-multicellular/

## Many cells, each with a job

F2. In a multicellular (many-celled) organism, groups of cells specialise to do particular jobs (nerve cells, skin cells, muscle cells, blood cells), and the cells depend on one another: CK-12 says "the cells of a multicellular organism would not survive as independent cells" (its example is liver cells). The heart and blood vessels carry oxygen and nutrients to the body's cells.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Multicellular_organism ("groups of cells differentiate to carry out specific functions"; "the heart and blood vessels distribute oxygen and nutrients throughout the body") ; https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Introductory_and_General_Biology/Introductory_Biology_(CK-12)/02%3A_Cell_Biology/2.12%3A_Organization_of_Cells ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/unicellular-vs-multicellular/ ("nerve cells, skin cells, muscle cells, blood cells")

F3. A skeletal muscle cell (a muscle fibre) is a long cell with several nuclei; it contracts (shortens) to make movement, pulling on the bones through tendons. Grokipedia says fibres reach several centimetres; OpenStax gives the longest as up to 30 cm, in the sartorius of the thigh, so no muscle cell is near a metre long. Like every cell, it has a cell membrane.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Skeletal_muscle ("elongated, multinucleated cells"; "can extend up to several centimeters in length"; "produce movement by contracting and relaxing"; "attached to bones via tendons") ; https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-2-skeletal-muscle ("lengths up to 30 cm (11.8 in) in the Sartorius of the upper leg") ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-2-prokaryotic-cells (every cell has a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, DNA and ribosomes)

F4. Mitochondria are found in most eukaryotic cells, and muscle fibres, which need much energy, hold thousands of them.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mitochondrion ("found in the cytoplasm of most eukaryotic cells"; "In energy-demanding cells like oocytes or muscle fibers, thousands of mitochondria are present")

F5. A nerve cell (neuron) is a cell specialised to carry signals: it receives, processes and passes on electrical and chemical signals, and its long axon carries impulses away from the cell body. The longest axons, in the sciatic nerve, run from the base of the spinal cord (in the lower back) down the leg to the foot, up to a metre long or more.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Neuron ("specialized for communication"; axons "up to 1 meter in projection neurons such as those in the human sciatic nerve", running "from the spinal cord to the foot") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axon ("run from the base of the spinal cord to the big toe of each foot"; "up to one meter or more")

F6. A ligament is a band of tough fibrous tissue that joins one bone to another; blood flows in blood vessels, tubes built of many cells. (Lures for F5.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ligament ("connects one bone to another bone") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Multicellular_organism

F7. A mature human red blood cell has no nucleus and few other parts: they are pushed out as it matures in the bone marrow, which leaves the most room for haemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen from the lungs to the body. With no nucleus it cannot divide and can barely repair itself, and it lives about 100 to 120 days. It has no cell wall: it is a soft, flexible disc that squeezes through capillaries as narrow as 3 micrometres.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Red_blood_cell ("maximizes intracellular space for hemoglobin, enhancing oxygen-carrying capacity"; "approximately 120 days"; capillaries "as narrow as 3 um") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_blood_cell ("to accommodate maximum space for hemoglobin"; "cannot divide and have limited repair capabilities"; "about 100-120 days"; "develop in the bone marrow"; "flexible and deformable so as to squeeze through tiny capillaries") ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-3-eukaryotic-cells (animal cells have no cell wall)

## Making food or taking it in

F8. An autotroph makes its own food from simple substances, using the energy of light (most plants, algae and cyanobacteria) or of chemicals; "auto" means self. Autotrophs are the producers at the base of food webs.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Autotroph ("capable of synthesizing its own food molecules from inorganic substances"; "Photoautotrophs, including most plants, algae, and cyanobacteria")

F9. A heterotroph cannot make its own food and takes in food made by other living things; the word is from the Greek heteros (other) and trophe (nourishment), "other feeder". Heterotrophs include animals, fungi, many bacteria and some protists.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Heterotroph ("consuming organic matter produced by other organisms"; "major groups such as animals, fungi, many bacteria, and certain protists")

F10. Animals are multicellular: no animal is a single cell. They are heterotrophs that ingest (eat) their food.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Animal ("Animals are multicellular, eukaryotic organisms"; "ingesting organic matter from external sources")

## Fungi are not plants

F11. Fungi have no chlorophyll and cannot photosynthesise. They feed by absorption: they pour digestive enzymes out onto their food, break it down outside their bodies, and absorb the dissolved nutrients through the walls of their threads (hyphae), which together form the mycelium. Fungal cell walls are mainly chitin, not the cellulose of plant walls.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fungus ("fungi lack chlorophyll and cannot perform photosynthesis"; "secrete digestive enzymes externally to break down organic matter"; "absorptive nutrition"; "a rigid cell wall primarily composed of chitin") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Heterotroph ("secreting hydrolytic enzymes onto organic matter in their environment"; absorbed "through their hyphal walls")

F12. Linnaeus counted fungi among the plants, as non-flowering plants; Robert H. Whittaker proposed in 1969 that fungi be a kingdom of their own. Fungi are now known to be the sister group of the animals, closer kin to animals than to plants.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fungus ("treating them as non-flowering plants"; "recognizing Fungi as a separate kingdom"; "they form a sister group to animals")

F13. Yeasts are single-celled fungi, about 3 to 4 micrometres across, with a nucleus; they are heterotrophs that live on sugars, and they live in soil and water and on the surfaces of plants and fruits. Mushrooms and moulds are many-celled fungi.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Yeast ("eukaryotic, single-celled microorganisms classified as members of the fungus kingdom"; "about 3-4 micrometers"; "possess a defined nucleus"; "heterotrophic organisms"; "the surfaces of plants and fruits") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fungus ("unicellular yeasts, multicellular molds") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mold ("multicellular fungi")

F14. Black bread mould (Rhizopus stolonifer) grows on bread from spores carried in the air that germinate in damp conditions; its threads secrete enzymes (such as amylases) that break the food down into simpler compounds, which it absorbs. Moisture is the most critical requirement for mould growth, but a mould feeds on organic matter, not on water. A spore grows germ tubes that lengthen into hyphae.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rhizopus_stolonifer ("black bread mold"; "spores germinate in moist conditions"; "secretes extracellular enzymes such as amylases, proteases, and pectinases"; "saprophytic heterotroph"; spores "dispersed by air currents") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mold (moisture "the most critical requirement"; "the spore produces germ tubes that elongate into hyphae"; moulds obtain nutrients from dead material)

## A plant that eats flies is still an autotroph

F15. Carnivorous plants remain photosynthetic autotrophs that get most of their carbon from carbon dioxide in the air; the prey they catch supplies nitrogen, phosphorus and trace elements that are scarce in their boggy, sandy, nutrient-poor soils; it is "a nutritional supplement rather than a primary energy source".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carnivorous_plant

F16. The Venus flytrap grows in nutrient-poor, acidic, boggy wetlands; its traps shut in about a tenth of a second (Grokipedia gives under 100 milliseconds, and 100 to 300 milliseconds); digestive glands pour out enzymes and digest the prey over 5 to 12 days. It gets up to 30 to 50 percent of its nitrogen from prey, takes most of its energy from photosynthesis, and does not need to be fed to survive.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Venus_flytrap ("nutrient-poor, acidic subtropical wetlands"; "supplemental nitrogen and phosphorus"; "most energy from photosynthesis"; "do not require feeding to survive") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Carnivorous_plant

## How single cells move and eat

F17. The paramecium is a one-celled ciliate of pond water, slipper- or cigar-shaped, 50 to 350 micrometres long. It swims by beating thousands of short, hair-like cilia in coordinated waves, at about 1 millimetre a second; when it meets an obstacle it reverses the beat of its cilia and backs away (the avoidance reaction). It is a heterotroph that eats bacteria, algae and yeasts, swept by cilia down an oral groove. (One species, P. bursaria, also houses green algae; no question says every paramecium is the same.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Paramecium ("thousands of short, hair-like cilia"; "rapidly reversing the direction of ciliary beating to back away") ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/unicellular-vs-multicellular/ ("a slipper-shaped, unicellular organism found in pond water")

F18. An amoeba is a single cell bounded by a thin membrane. It crawls by pushing out pseudopodia ("false feet", from the Greek for false and foot) and eats by phagocytosis: its pseudopodia reach around the prey, forming a cup that closes and seals the food inside a compartment (a food vacuole), where it is digested. It eats bacteria, algae and other protists. Amoeba proteus is 0.2 to 0.5 millimetres across.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Amoeba ("engulfing food particles via phagocytosis"; the membrane "surrounds the prey and forms a food vacuole where digestion occurs") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pseudopodia ("pseudo" false, "podium" foot; "forming a phagocytic cup that envelops the particle")

F19. Euglena is a one-celled protist that swims with one whip-like flagellum. Its green chloroplasts let it make its own food from light (autotrophy). A red eyespot shades a light-sensitive swelling at the base of the flagellum, so the cell senses which way the light lies; it swims toward light (phototaxis), which "optimizes positioning for photosynthesis". In the dark it can absorb dissolved food instead (a mixotroph); a few strains can engulf prey, less commonly.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Euglena

F20. The word protozoa was coined by Georg August Goldfuss in 1818 for "first animals", and Carl Theodor von Siebold in 1845 defined protozoa as unicellular animals; today the protozoa are an informal grouping spread across many branches of life, not animals (and animals are all multicellular, F10).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Protozoa

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Fungi are plants. Naturalists held it for centuries (F12), and pupils still do: Gul Seyda's study of 5th graders (2021) recorded "Fungus is a type of plant", "Fungi reproduce similarly to plants", and that yeast and mould are not alive because they do not move.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fungus ; https://wordpressua.uark.edu/scimap/2023/06/09/5th-grade-students-misunderstandings-and-misconceptions-about-fungi-2 (ERIC: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1321978.pdf)

M2. All microbes are one kind of thing: a review of school microbiology misconceptions lists the belief that bacteria, viruses and one-celled fungi are all the same, so yeast is taken for a bacterium. (Search-result summary of the secondary microbiology studies; Byrne 2011, International Journal of Science Education 33(14), studied the same ideas in children aged 7 to 14; full text not read.) Treated as author's judgment for the yeast lure.

M3. Food means energy, so a plant that eats flies must be eating them for energy (or plants get their food from the soil). The T1 photosynthesis unit corrects the soil version; F15 and F16 correct the flytrap version.

M4. Author's notes on the lures: swimming toward light misread as seeking warmth, as seeing with the eyespot, or as following the crowd (F19 gives the real reason); "it has no nucleus" for a body cell carries over from the red blood cell (F7), though a muscle cell has several nuclei (F3).

CAUTION, not used: the claim that big animals have more cells but cells no bigger than a mouse's holds only for some cell types (Savage and others, PNAS 2007: neurons and fat cells are larger in big mammals), so no question uses it.
    https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/03/070308220603.htm

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F21. The longest human muscle fibres fall far short of a metre, though OpenStax's "up to 30 cm" is too low: in the sartorius, the longest muscle ("up to 600 mm in length"), some fibres "can be >500 mm in length and reach from tendon to tendon" (Harris and others, Journal of Neuroscience 2005, "Muscle fiber and motor unit behavior in the longest human skeletal muscle"). Used in the context of the long-nerve-cell question.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6725658

F22. Each Venus flytrap trap is ONE leaf with two halves: "The traps of Dionaea muscipula consist of modified terminal leaves divided into two hinged lobes", "a bilobed leaf structure with a midrib hinge" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia (the fact-checker's source): "a pair of terminal lobes hinged at the midrib, forming the trap which is the true leaf".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Venus_flytrap ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_flytrap

F23. Every fungus is a heterotroph: "fungi lack chlorophyll and cannot perform photosynthesis; instead, they are heterotrophs". Some live by "saprotrophy, where they decompose dead organic matter"; others are parasites that feed on living plant or animal hosts ("Parasitic fungi adopt a nutrient-acquiring strategy involving host penetration and absorption"). So "feeds only on dead remains" is false of fungi, and the word heterotroph does not mean it. Heterotrophs include many bacteria (F9), which are single cells (F1), so "built of many kinds of cells" is not what the word means either.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fungus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Heterotroph
