## The reaction

F1. The overall equation of photosynthesis is 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6O2: six carbon dioxide molecules and six water molecules, with light energy, become one glucose molecule and six oxygen molecules. Glucose "serves as the primary energy-storing molecule" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ; bankbuild/science_refresh/curriculum_map.json (G8.P2) ; https://texasgateway.org/teks/s86e/alignments (TEKS 8.6E)

F2. Reading the formulas in F1: a carbon dioxide molecule (CO2) holds one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms; a water molecule (H2O) two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom; a glucose molecule (C6H12O6) six carbon, twelve hydrogen and six oxygen atoms. So the six carbon atoms of one glucose come from six carbon dioxide molecules, and two glucose molecules take twelve. Carbon is carried in only by carbon dioxide, and hydrogen only by water.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis (the equation) ; bankbuild/science_refresh/curriculum_map.json (G7.P1: "C6H12O6 is glucose")

F3. Where: chloroplasts are "membrane-bound organelles found primarily in the cells of plants and algae", "converting solar energy into chemical energy stored in glucose and other carbohydrates". Chlorophyll sits in the thylakoid membranes inside them; the light-dependent phase "takes place in the thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chloroplast ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Light-dependent_reactions

F4. Chlorophyll absorbs light "particularly in the blue and red wavelengths" (Grokipedia); "Chlorophylls absorb light most strongly in the blue and red portions of the electromagnetic spectrum", and chlorophyll "is a poor absorber of green and near-green portions of the spectrum", so chlorophyll-containing tissues look green (Wikipedia). The action spectrum of photosynthesis "closely parallels the absorption spectrum of chlorophyll", peaking in the blue and the red.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chlorophyll ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll

F5. The energy: photosynthesis converts light (solar) energy into chemical energy, stored in glucose (Grokipedia: "converting solar energy into chemical energy"; Chloroplast: "chemical energy stored in glucose and other carbohydrates"). Light is written into the equation (F1) with no formula: it brings energy and no atoms, and every atom of the products comes from the carbon dioxide and the water (F2). NGSS LS1.C: plants "use the energy from light to make sugars (food) from carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and water".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chloroplast ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ls1-6-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes

## Theodor Engelmann: the chloroplast and the colours

F6. Theodor Wilhelm Engelmann (German physiologist and botanist). In 1881 he watched oxygen-seeking bacteria gather around the chloroplasts of a strand of the green alga Spirogyra and concluded they were drawn by the oxygen the active chloroplasts gave off (Wikipedia). In 1882, with a microscope prism device built by Carl Zeiss, he spread the visible spectrum of sunlight along a strand of green alga (Cladophora, by Wikipedia's account) among oxygen-seeking bacteria; the bacteria clustered "primarily in the red and blue regions" (Grokipedia) ("red and violet", Wikipedia). Crops Review puts the two together: Spirogyra lit through a prism, the bacteria "gathered around the parts of the chloroplast that were illuminated by red and blue light". The result introduced the action spectrum; he is credited with showing the chloroplast is the site of photosynthesis.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Wilhelm_Engelmann ; https://www.cropsreview.com/?p=749

## Where the oxygen comes from

F7. The oxygen given off comes from water: water splitting "oxidizes two water molecules to produce one dioxygen molecule" (Grokipedia); "The source of electrons for photosynthesis in green plants and cyanobacteria is water" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Light-dependent_reactions ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis

F8. The older view: the oxygen came from carbon dioxide. Jean Senebier "concluded (erroneously as it turned out) that in photosynthesis carbon dioxide is decomposed" (Kimball); van Niel's idea was that plant oxygen came from split water "and not from carbon dioxide, as previously thought" (Encyclopedia.com).
    https://www.biology-pages.info/P/Photosynthesis_history.html ; https://encyclopedia.com/science/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/van-niel-c-b

F9. Cornelis Bernardus van Niel (1897-1985) moved to Stanford University's Hopkins Marine Station in California in 1929 and studied purple and green sulfur bacteria. Purple sulfur bacteria use light to build food from carbon dioxide, but "use hydrogen sulfide (H2S)" where plants use water; "no oxygen is liberated during this photosynthesis but rather elemental sulfur" (Kimball); sulfide oxidation leads to "the accumulation of elemental sulfur globules", "without evolving oxygen" (Grokipedia). Van Niel was "the first scientist to demonstrate, in 1931, that photosynthesis is a light-dependent redox reaction" (his 1931 paper with F. M. Muller); his work "predicted that H2O is the hydrogen donor in green plant photosynthesis and is oxidized to O2" (Wikipedia): all the oxygen a plant gives off comes from water "just as all the sulfur produced by the purple sulfur bacteria comes from H2S" (Kimball).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._B._van_Niel ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Purple_sulfur_bacteria ; https://www.biology-pages.info/P/Photosynthesis_history.html ; https://encyclopedia.com/science/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/van-niel-c-b

F10. Samuel Ruben, Merle Randall, Martin Kamen and J. L. Hyde, "Heavy oxygen (18O) as a tracer in the study of photosynthesis", Journal of the American Chemical Society 63 (1941): 877-879, at the University of California (Berkeley). They gave photosynthesizing algae water in which 0.85 percent of the molecules held oxygen-18; the oxygen gas given off held 0.86 percent, "the same as that of the water supplied". They also gave algae carbon dioxide (as bicarbonate) enriched in oxygen-18 (0.68 percent) with ordinary water (0.20 percent); "the oxygen that was given off was not enriched in 18O" (0.20 percent). Wikipedia: Ruben's heavy water "H2 18O" yielded 18O2 gas, showing the oxygen gas comes from water; "Kamen confirmed in 1941 that all of the oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from water, not carbon dioxide."
    https://www.biology-pages.info/P/Photosynthesis_history.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Ruben ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Kamen

F11. Oxygen-18 is a stable (not radioactive) isotope of oxygen, about 0.2 percent of natural oxygen atoms. An oxygen atom has 8 protons, so oxygen-18 has 10 neutrons against the 8 of ordinary oxygen-16: two extra neutrons, two units heavier. (Wikipedia's Photosynthesis article calls the tracers "radioactive isotopes"; for the oxygen work that is wrong, so it is not used.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Oxygen-18

F12. The textbook equation (F1) is a net count. Wikipedia gives the water-splitting form as CO2 + 2H2O + light energy → [CH2O] + O2 + H2O: for each carbon fixed, two water molecules go in and one comes out. Six times over, a plant splits twelve water molecules and makes six new ones to free the six O2 molecules (twelve oxygen atoms); six water molecules alone hold only six oxygen atoms. Kimball notes a small exchange of oxygen between the water and the bicarbonate in Ruben's first test (the carbon dioxide's 18O rose from 0.20 to 0.61 percent), "a non-biochemical exchange".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis ; https://www.biology-pages.info/P/Photosynthesis_history.html

## Where the carbon (and the hydrogen) come from

F13. Dry wood "has an elemental composition of about 50% carbon, 6% hydrogen, 44% oxygen, and trace amounts of inorganics" (Rowell, Pettersen and Tshabalala, USDA Forest Service, Cell Wall Chemistry). Dry wood is mostly cellulose (40-50 percent), with hemicelluloses and lignin (Grokipedia); cellulose is "a linear polysaccharide composed of β(1→4)-linked D-glucose units", the main structural part of plant cell walls.
    https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/42245 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Wood ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cellulose

F14. Nicolas-Théodore de Saussure (1767-1845), a Swiss (Genevan) chemist, in Recherches chimiques sur la végétation (1804) showed that "plants obtain their carbon from the carbon dioxide in the atmosphere, not through uptake from humus in the soil"; that plants grown with their roots in water gained more dry weight than the carbon dioxide could explain ("the weight increase had come from the water"); and that plant minerals come from the soil. He based his conclusions "on extensive quantitative data that he had collected".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas-Th%C3%A9odore_de_Saussure

F15. Hydrogen: in F1 only water holds hydrogen (F2), and water is the source of the electrons (hydrogen) in green-plant photosynthesis (F7, F9: water is "the hydrogen donor in green plant photosynthesis"). So the hydrogen atoms in a plant's glucose, and in the cellulose of its wood built from glucose (F13), came from water. De Saussure found water adds to a plant's dry weight (F14).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._B._van_Niel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas-Th%C3%A9odore_de_Saussure

F16. Soil gives a plant water and small amounts of minerals, not most of its matter; the willow's 74 kg "while the weight of the soil had barely changed at all" (IFLScience, after van Helmont); Feynman: "almost all of the tree" came out of the air. Wood ash, "the inorganic residue remaining after the complete combustion of wood", is mostly calcium ("typically comprising 15-20% by weight, potassium up to 5%", with magnesium and phosphorus); its metals "originate primarily from the soil absorbed by trees during growth" (Grokipedia). So a tree's calcium came from the soil, not from rain or distilled water.
    https://www.iflscience.com/where-do-trees-get-most-of-their-mass-from-because-it-isnt-the-ground-77039 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas-Th%C3%A9odore_de_Saussure ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Wood_ash

## The first clues

F17. Jan Baptist van Helmont (1579-1644), Flemish chemist and physician. His own account (as translated): "I took an earthen pot and in it placed 200 pounds of earth which had been dried out in an oven"; he moistened it with rain water and planted "a shoot of willow which weighed five pounds"; a "tin plated iron lid with many holes punched in it" covered the pot (to keep out dust); "When five years had passed the tree ... weighed 169 pounds and about three ounces"; "I dried out the earth in the pot once more, and found the same 200 pounds, less about 2 ounces"; "Thus, 164 pounds of wood, bark, and roots had arisen from water alone." Metric: the shoot about 2.3 kg, the soil 91 kg, the gain about 74 kg (74.5 kg, Kimball), the soil loss 57 g. He watered it "with rain water or distilled water" (Kimball; Grokipedia: "exclusively with rain or distilled water"). Kimball: "He did not consider the possibility that gases in the air might be involved." Grokipedia dates the start to about 1600. (Irony, not used: van Helmont coined the word "gas", and his gas sylvestre from burning charcoal was carbon dioxide.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Jan_Baptist_van_Helmont ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Baptist_van_Helmont ; https://www.biology-pages.info/P/Photosynthesis_history.html ; https://www.iflscience.com/jan-baptista-van-helmonts-16th-century-willow-tree-experiment-was-one-of-the-first-in-modern-biology-82153

F18. Joseph Priestley, in his own words: "on the 17th of August 1771, I put a sprig of mint into a quantity of air, in which a candle had burned out, and found that, on the 27th of the same month, another candle burned perfectly well in it" (ten days). "Several times I divided the quantity of air in which the candle had burned out, into two parts", a plant in one and none in the other: a candle burned in the first but not the second. His finding: "Plants restore air that has been injured by respiration and burning." Grokipedia: in 1771 he "enclosed a burning candle in a glass vessel until the flame extinguished" "and then introduced a sprig of mint", and describes "the survival of a mouse placed inside". Historynet: in 1771 "A mouse placed inside the jar with the plant could survive happily for ten minutes"; his mouse-and-mint trials ran in 1771 and 1772. Kimball: Priestley "thought (erroneously) that it was simply the growth of the plant that accounted for this."
    https://revolutionaryplayers.org.uk/?p=3387 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ; https://historynet.com/green-ben/ ; https://wellcomecollection.org/stories/what-is-air--and-how-do-we-know- ; https://www.biology-pages.info/P/Photosynthesis_history.html

F19. Jan Ingenhousz, a "Dutch-born physician, plant physiologist, and chemist", in 1779 "established the essential role of light" (Grokipedia) with "over 500 experiments" in the summer of 1779, at a country house he rented near London (Southall Green, Wikipedia), published as Experiments upon Vegetables (London, 1779). Leaves of mint and other plants sunk in water-filled jars formed bubbles only in sunlight; production stopped in darkness or shade; "only the green portions of plants—such as leaves—released the restorative gas". He wrote that "no dephlogisticated air is obtained in a warm room, if the sun does not shine upon the jar containing the leaves": light, not mere warmth. Plants correct foul air "only in clear day light, or in the sun shine" (Wikipedia). (Kimball dates the light finding to 1778; the book is 1779.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Jan_Ingenhousz ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Ingenhousz ; https://www.biology-pages.info/P/Photosynthesis_history.html

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Plants take their food from the soil, as animals take theirs from outside ("a widely held misconception", OpenLearn; "Soil provides a support structure and food for plants", Ohio State). Its form for this unit: a tree's matter (or its carbon) comes from the soil, or from rotting humus (the humus theory de Saussure overturned, F14).
    https://www.open.edu/openlearncreate/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=64820&section=9.2 ; https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290
M2. "Part of the plant's food is sunlight" (OpenLearn): light taken for matter, as if it became the sugar's atoms (wrong by F5).
M3. "Sunlight helps plants grow by keeping them warm" (Ohio State): warmth taken for the light's work (wrong by F19).
M4. The oxygen a plant gives off comes from the carbon dioxide: the chemists' own belief before 1941 (F8), and the natural reading of the equation, since carbon dioxide holds more oxygen than water.
M5. A plant that grows on water and air alone still gets its carbon from the water (the T2 lesson "from air and water" read as "carbon from both"); water holds no carbon (F2).
M6. Leaves are green, so green light must serve them best: no survey was read for this one; it is wrong by F4 and F6, and is used only as a wrong choice that those facts settle.
