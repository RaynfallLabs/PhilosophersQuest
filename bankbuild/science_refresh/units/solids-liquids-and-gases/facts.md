# Solids, Liquids and Gases: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-25. Grokipedia first; NIST, NSTA, USGS, Florida and England curriculum pages to corroborate and to place the grade.

## Facts

F1 Matter comes in three common states: solid, liquid and gas. (Plasma, "often called the fourth state of matter", is not taught in this unit.) https://grokipedia.com/page/State_of_matter ("melting (solid to liquid), freezing (liquid to solid), vaporization (liquid to gas)"; "Plasma, often called the fourth state of matter")

F2 A solid has its own shape and its own volume, whatever container it is in. https://grokipedia.com/page/State_of_matter ("a definite shape and volume independent of the container"); https://floridascience.squarespace.com/physical-science-1 (grade 5, SC.5.P.8.1: solids "do not change shape, volume, or mass when moved to a new container")

F3 A liquid has its own volume but no shape of its own: it takes the shape of its container and forms a pool, not a pile. https://grokipedia.com/page/State_of_matter ("a definite volume but no fixed shape"); https://floridascience.squarespace.com/physical-science-1 (liquids "will change shape but not volume or mass when moved to a new container"); https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study ("liquids form a pool not a pile")

F4 A gas has neither its own shape nor its own volume: it spreads out to fill the whole of its container. Gases can be squeezed into a much smaller space (they are highly compressible). https://grokipedia.com/page/State_of_matter ("neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume"; "expand and fill the entire space of its container"; "gases are highly compressible"); https://floridascience.squarespace.com/physical-science-1 ("Gases will expand to fill the container they are in")

F5 Water exists as a solid (ice), a liquid (water) and a gas (water vapour). https://grokipedia.com/page/Water ("Water exists in solid, liquid, and gaseous states depending on temperature and pressure"); https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study ("Pupils should observe water as a solid, a liquid and a gas")

F6 Water vapour is invisible. The white "steam" or mist that can be seen is tiny drops of liquid water, formed when the vapour cools and condenses. https://grokipedia.com/page/Steam (pure steam is "an invisible gas"; the visible white cloud is "aerosols of microscopic liquid water droplets formed by condensation in cooler air"); https://floridascience.squarespace.com/physical-science-1 (water vapor "is invisible"; "Steam is liquid water that is made up of droplets")

F7 Breathed-out air is full of water vapour. On a cold day some of that vapour condenses into tiny drops of liquid water, which make a visible fog; the vapour itself cannot be seen. https://grokipedia.com/page/Exhalation (water vapor "constitutes a significant portion" of exhaled air); https://www.nsta.org/science-and-children/science-and-children-january-2020/q-why-can-i-see-my-breath-cold-days ("the water in your moist breath changes from a gas, water vapor, to liquid droplets, which form a fog"; "Note that water vapor is invisible"); https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/738 ("tiny liquid water droplets. These water droplets are what you see")

F8 Fog made of ice crystals (ice fog) generally needs temperatures at or below about -35 degrees Celsius, so a puff of breath on an ordinary chilly morning is liquid drops, not ice. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_fog ("Generally, this requires temperatures at or below -35 °C (-31 °F)")

F9 Pure water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius (32 degrees Fahrenheit) at ordinary air pressure, and ice melts at the same temperature, 0 degrees Celsius. https://grokipedia.com/page/Freezing ("For pure water, this equilibrium temperature is 0 °C (32 °F) at 1 atm"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Melting_point ("its precise melting point of 0°C"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Celsius ("the freezing point of water under standard atmospheric pressure is defined as 0 °C"; Fahrenheit "32 °F as the freezing point of pure water")

F10 A mixture of ice and water stays at about 0 degrees Celsius while the ice melts: the heat taken in goes into melting, without a rise in temperature. https://www.nist.gov/pml/mercury-thermometer-alternatives/mercury-thermometer-alternatives-verification-methods ("When ice and water are packed together into an insulated container, the mixture has a temperature of nearly 0 °C (32 °F)"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Melting_point (melting absorbs latent heat "without a rise in temperature")

F11 At sea level (1 atmosphere of pressure) pure water boils at 100 degrees Celsius (212 degrees Fahrenheit). Where the air pressure is lower, high on a mountain, water boils below 100 degrees Celsius (about 95 degrees Celsius at 1,524 m). https://grokipedia.com/page/Boiling ("the boiling point is precisely 100 °C (212 °F)"; "it decreases at lower pressures, as seen in high-altitude locations where water boils below 100 °C"; about 95 °C at about 1,524 m); https://grokipedia.com/page/Boiling_point ("the normal boiling point of water is 100 °C"); https://www.nist.gov/pml/mercury-thermometer-alternatives/mercury-thermometer-alternatives-verification-methods ("The water boiling point, however, is NOT necessarily 100 °C (212 °F)", corrected for elevation)

F12 Once water is boiling, adding more heat turns more of it into vapour but does not raise its temperature above the boiling point. https://grokipedia.com/page/Boiling_point (at the boiling point "the liquid can vaporize without further temperature increase")

F13 The changes of state have names: melting (solid to liquid), freezing (liquid to solid), boiling or vaporization (liquid to gas, with evaporation as the slow change at the surface) and condensation (gas to liquid). https://grokipedia.com/page/State_of_matter; https://grokipedia.com/page/Freezing ("Freezing is the conversion of a liquid into a solid phase"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Condensation ("the phase transition in which a gas or vapor transforms into a liquid"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Boiling (evaporation "a surface-only phenomenon")

F14 Adding heat melts a solid and boils a liquid; taking heat away freezes a liquid and condenses a gas. https://floridascience.squarespace.com/sc5p91-temperatures-affect-in-physical-and-chemical-change (grade 5, SC.5.P.9.1: changes of state of water are "caused by adding or removing heat"; "Boiling is when heat is added to water to cause it to change into a gas"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Freezing (freezing occurs "upon the removal of thermal energy"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Condensation (condensation typically occurs "when the temperature decreases or pressure increases")

F15 Gallium is a soft, silvery metal that melts at about 29.8 degrees Celsius (29.76), just above room temperature, so it turns to liquid when held against human skin. Skin is about 33-34 degrees Celsius and the body's core about 37 degrees Celsius. https://grokipedia.com/page/Gallium ("soft, silvery-white metal that melts at approximately 29.8 °C, just above room temperature"; can "liquefy upon contact with human skin at typical body temperature"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Human_body_temperature (core "approximately 37°C"; skin "typically ranges from 33°C to 34°C")

F16 Every substance melts and freezes at its own temperature: water at 0 degrees Celsius, mercury at about -38.8 degrees Celsius, iron at about 1,538 degrees Celsius. England's primary curriculum has pupils research such temperatures, "for example, when iron melts". https://grokipedia.com/page/Freezing (table: mercury -38.83 °C, iron 1538 °C); https://grokipedia.com/page/Melting_point (mercury -38.8 °C); https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study

F17 Lava is molten rock that erupts onto Earth's surface (magma is molten rock below the surface). It erupts at about 650-1,250 degrees Celsius (basalt lava 1,000-1,200; Kilauea's about 1,150) and cools and solidifies into igneous rock such as basalt. https://grokipedia.com/page/Lava ("molten rock that erupts onto the Earth's surface"; "generally ranging from 650°C to 1250°C"; "cooling and solidifying to form extrusive igneous rock"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Magma ("molten or partially molten rock"; "Magma forms through partial melting of existing rocks")

F18 Lava turns solid while it is still very hot: USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory scientists treat Kilauea's lava crust as solid below about 850 degrees Celsius. Since freezing is any liquid turning into a solid as heat is removed, lava hardening into rock is freezing (solidification). https://bigislandnow.com/2019/11/01/volcano-watch-why-2018-lava-is-still-hot/ (USGS HVO "Volcano Watch": "Lava erupts from Kilauea at a temperature of 1150° C"; "crust is considered solid when it is below 850° C"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Freezing ("the conversion of a liquid into a solid phase"; iron's freezing point 1538 °C)

F19 Air holds water vapour, an invisible gas (from nearly 0 up to about 4 percent of it). When that vapour touches a cold surface, such as a cold glass, it condenses into drops of liquid water on the outside. https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_vapor (water vapor "invisibly dispersed among other atmospheric gases"; "nearly 0%" to "about 4% by volume"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Condensation ("the familiar transformation of water vapor into liquid droplets on a cold glass surface")

F20 In boiling, bubbles of vapour form inside the liquid, all through it, not only at the surface. The big bubbles in boiling water are water vapour (steam). https://grokipedia.com/page/Boiling ("discrete vapor bubbles at nucleation sites on the heating surface or within the liquid"); https://archive.bigelow.org/virtual/handson/diss_air.html ("The second set of bubbles that form near the boiling point are water vapor bubbles"); https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-changes-of-state-beyond-appearances/4017768.article ("steam" is the correct answer to "What are the bubbles made of?")

F21 The very small bubbles that first appear as water is warmed (from about 35 degrees Celsius) are air that was dissolved in the water, not water vapour. https://archive.bigelow.org/virtual/handson/diss_air.html ("Very small dissolved air bubbles will begin to form on the bottom at about 35°C (95°F)")

F22 A change of state is a physical change: no new substance is made. Ice, liquid water and water vapour are all the same substance, and the change can be undone (melted ice freezes again; vapour condenses back to water). https://grokipedia.com/page/Physical_change (changes "without affecting its chemical composition or identity"; boiling water: "the H2O molecules remain intact"; "Physical changes are often reversible", refreezing melted ice); https://grokipedia.com/page/State_of_matter (matter changes form "without altering its chemical composition")

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 The bubbles in boiling water are heat, air, or oxygen and hydrogen split from the water. Osborne and Cosgrove (1983), students aged 8-17 asked "What are the bubbles made of?": heat (30% at 12), air (30%), oxygen and hydrogen (25% rising to 40%), steam (correct, 15% rising to 30%). https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-changes-of-state-beyond-appearances/4017768.article

M2 The water on the outside of a cold, sealed jar of ice came through the glass, or the cold came through the glass; younger children call a surface with condensation "sweaty". RSC (Osborne and Cosgrove): "water comes through the glass" (age 8-15), "coldness comes through the glass" (age 12-17); "Many 10-13-year-olds said the plate had become 'sweaty' or simply 'wet'". Science Learning Hub: "Condensation on the outside of a container is water that seeps out of the container walls." https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-changes-of-state-beyond-appearances/4017768.article; https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/616-alternative-conceptions-about-water-s-states-of-matter

M3 Steam (the visible white puff) is hot air, or is water vapour itself. Science Learning Hub: "Steam is hot air." The "visible mist is water vapour" belief is what NSTA's breath note ("Note that water vapor is invisible") and Florida's grade 5 page correct. https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/616-alternative-conceptions-about-water-s-states-of-matter; https://www.nsta.org/science-and-children/science-and-children-january-2020/q-why-can-i-see-my-breath-cold-days

M4 Mixing up the Fahrenheit and Celsius numbers (32 and 212 are Fahrenheit's freezing and boiling points, F9, F11). Author's judgment for a US 5th grader; no survey found.

M5 Freezing only means getting cold or turning into ice, and hot things only melt, so hot lava hardening "cannot be freezing". Author's judgment; no survey found. Corrected by F13, F16, F18.

M6 Breath fog is frozen breath (ice). Author's judgment; corrected by F7, F8.

M7 A melting ice-water mix gets colder than 0 degrees, or warms steadily while it melts; a hotter flame makes boiling water hotter. Author's judgment; corrected by F10, F12.

## Notes and cautions

- Say "at sea level" (or ordinary air pressure) for 100 degrees Celsius: the boiling point is lower on a mountain (F11).
- Say "pure water": salt water freezes lower (Grokipedia Freezing table: 23.3% salt solution at -21.1 °C).
- The ice-water mix is "nearly 0 °C" (NIST); at this grade "0 degrees Celsius" is the true reading.
- Do not say all the bubbles while heating are water vapour: the first tiny ones are dissolved air (F21). Ask about the big bubbles of hard boiling.
- Lava freezing: Grokipedia's Lava page says "solidifies", not "freezes"; the word freezing follows from the definition (F13, F18). Say the solid crust forms below about 850 °C (USGS HVO), not a single freezing point for all lava.
- Ice-water mix, melting gallium and condensation questions use lab, rock and body examples, not kitchen ones (lane note).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-25)

F23 Heat flows from a warmer thing to a cooler one, so heat flows from a warm hand into a cooler lump of metal; cold is not a thing that flows. "Cold is transferred from one object to another" is a known grades 3-5 misconception. https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_conduction ("from regions of higher temperature to regions of lower temperature"); https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2919 (Ohio State, grades 3-5: "heat moves from warmer objects to cooler ones"; misconception "Cold is transferred from one object to another")

F24 Gallium is not dissolved by water or sweat and does not react with air or water at room temperature: a thin, protective oxide layer forms on it. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallium ("is not reactive with air and water because it forms a passive, protective oxide layer"; it "will melt in a person's hands"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Gallium (it "readily tarnishes in moist air to develop a thin oxide layer")

F25 Glass does not let liquids or gases through, so water inside a sealed glass jar cannot seep out through its walls. https://grokipedia.com/page/Glass ("The impermeability of glass to gases, liquids, and contaminants")
