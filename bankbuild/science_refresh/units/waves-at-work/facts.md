## Wave speed

F1. For any wave in a given medium, wave speed equals frequency times wavelength (v = f x wavelength). When a wave passes into a medium where it travels more slowly, its frequency stays the same and its wavelength shortens; that change of speed is what bends (refracts) it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wavelength

F2. A classroom measurement of light's speed with a microwave oven: take the turntable out, heat a bar or layer of chocolate for a few seconds, and melted spots appear; "each spot is half a wavelength" apart (typically about 6 cm, so the waves are about 12 cm long). The oven's frequency, usually printed on the back, is 2,450 MHz (2,450 million waves a second). "The speed of light is frequency x wavelength": 2,450,000,000 x 0.12 = 294,000,000 m/s. CAVEAT: hot spots in real ovens are unevenly spread, so the spacing (and the result) varies; theory gives 6.1 cm between peaks; one home attempt measured 9 cm and got 73 percent of the true value.
    https://wp.optics.arizona.edu/oscoutreach/measure-the-speed-of-light-with-a-microwave ; https://uwaterloo.ca/chem13-news-magazine/node/579 ; https://uwaterloo.ca/chem13-news-magazine/node/619

F3. The speed of light in empty space is exactly 299,792,458 metres a second (about 300,000 km a second); c = f x wavelength holds for all electromagnetic waves, so microwaves and radio waves travel at the same speed in empty space.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed_of_light

## Interference

F4. Where waves overlap, their displacements add (superposition). In phase (crest meets crest; paths differing by a whole number of wavelengths) the amplitudes add: constructive interference. Out of phase by half a wave (crest meets trough; 180 degrees; paths differing by an odd number of half wavelengths) they subtract, cancelling completely if the amplitudes are equal: destructive interference.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Wave_interference ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%27s_interference_experiment

F5. Thomas Young set out his principle of interference in a Bakerian Lecture to the Royal Society in November 1801. In his lecture of 24 November 1803 (published 1804) he described placing a card about 0.85 mm (about a thirtieth of an inch) wide in a beam of light from a single opening in a window, and seeing fringes of colour in its shadow and to its sides. Placing another card so as to stop the light reaching one edge "caused the fringes to disappear." Grokipedia: the card, inserted edgewise, splits the beam into two portions, giving "alternating bright and dark fringes"; dark fringes where the two are out of phase. CAVEAT: it is not clear which slit experiments Young actually performed and which he only described (Wikipedia); sources give 1801, 1803 and 1807 for different versions, so the questions say "in 1803" only for the card.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Young_(scientist) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Young's_interference_experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thomas_Young_(scientist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%27s_interference_experiment

F6. Young aimed "to challenge Isaac Newton's prevailing corpuscular theory of light" (Grokipedia). In Newton's corpuscular theory, light is a stream of particles, and colours come from particles of different kinds ("red light consists of the least refrangible corpuscles"). Explaining the dark fringes with particles would need ad hoc assumptions such as forces between corpuscles. Britannica: "the observation of interference effects definitively indicates the presence of overlapping waves." Compadre: in some places adding more light (opening another slit) makes the result darker, not brighter.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Young's_interference_experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Corpuscular_theory_of_light ; https://www.britannica.com/science/light/Youngs-double-slit-experiment ; https://www.compadre.org/nexusph/course/The_wave_model_of_light

F7. Reception: Young met scepticism in Britain, where Newton's authority dominated, and in France (Laplace, Biot); unsigned attacks in the Edinburgh Review, later attributed to Henry Brougham, hurt his credibility. Fresnel's diffraction memoir won the French Academy's prize (1819), and Arago's observation of the bright spot Poisson had predicted "convinced most scientists of the wave-nature of light."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%27s_interference_experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Young's_interference_experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thomas_Young_(scientist)

F8. Honest modern note: "neither wave nor particle explanations alone can explain the behaviour of light" (Wikipedia, Thomas Young); the double-slit experiment also shows light and matter meeting both classical definitions, of waves and of particles.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Young_(scientist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-slit_experiment

F9. Active noise control (noise-cancelling headphones): microphones pick up the noise and a loudspeaker emits "a secondary sound wave of equal amplitude but opposite phase" (Grokipedia); "the same amplitude but with an inverted phase", so the waves cancel by destructive interference (Wikipedia). It works best on "steady, low-frequency disturbances, such as engine hums in vehicles or aircraft cabins"; "High-frequency waves are difficult to reduce in three dimensions due to their relatively short audio wavelength in air"; consumer ANC can fail on sudden peaks. Where the waves do not line up they can make zones that reduce noise in some spots "while doubling noise in others", and a badly matched wave can be "reinforced ... making the noise louder, not softer" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Active_noise_control ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control

F10. Paul Lueg filed his noise-cancelling patents in 1933; the patent (US 2,043,416) was granted in 1936, "the first patent for a noise control system."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Active_noise_control ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_noise_control

## Reflection at work: sonar

F11. Sonar is "originally an acronym for SOund Navigation And Ranging." The idea of finding things under water by echo "seems to have been prompted by the Titanic disaster of 1912" (Wikipedia); the Titanic struck an iceberg and sank on 15 April 1912 with over 1,500 dead (Grokipedia). Lewis Fry Richardson filed the world's first patent for underwater echo-ranging about a month later. Reginald Fessenden detected an iceberg about 2 miles (3.2 km) away by echo in 1914, off Newfoundland.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sonar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar

F12. Paul Langevin, with Constantin Chilowsky, began work on ultrasonic echo-ranging to detect submarines in 1915. By February 1917 he used quartz crystals (the piezoelectric effect found by the Curie brothers) as receivers, and by April 1917 as transmitters; he settled on 40 kHz (ultrasound). His system first detected underwater echoes in November 1917, and in 1918 found submarines up to about 1,300 m away (Grokipedia's sonar page: about 1,500 m). Wikipedia credits Boyle and Wood (Britain, 1917) with the first practical quartz apparatus.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Paul_Langevin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sonar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar

F13. Active sonar times the echo of a pulse; sound travels in sea water at about 1,500 m/s, and since the pulse goes out and back, distance = (speed x time) / 2. MISCONCEPTION: forgetting to halve the round trip.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sonar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar

## Reflection at work: ultrasound scans

F14. Ian Donald, Regius Professor of Midwifery at Glasgow (from 1954), visited the Babcock & Wilcox boilermaking works at Renfrew on 21 July 1955, where ultrasound flaw detectors tested steel, to see whether they could tell tissue types apart. He brought fibroids and a large ovarian cyst removed from patients, and tested them beside a large steak the company supplied as a control. In Donald's words, "a cyst produced echoes only at depth from the near and far walls," whereas "a solid tumour progressively attenuated echoes at increasing depths of penetration" (quoted by Wikipedia). The Embryo Project: a technician, Bernard Donnelly, first bounced the beam off the bone of Donald's own thumb.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Donald ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/ian-donald-1910-1987 ; https://heritage.rcpsg.ac.uk/exhibits/show/our-science-and-art/clinical-ultrasound ; https://www.gla.ac.uk/schools/medicine/mus/ourfacilities/history/ourfamousscholars/iandonald

F15. Donald's team scanned a woman diagnosed with inoperable stomach cancer (a barium X-ray had seemed to confirm it); the scan showed a cyst, and surgery removed a large, benign ovarian cyst. Wikipedia places it in 1956, the Embryo Project in 1957, so no year is used. Donald, John MacVicar and Tom Brown published "Investigation of Abdominal Masses by Pulsed Ultrasound" in The Lancet in June 1958, with the first published ultrasound image of a fetus.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Donald ; https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/ian-donald-1910-1987 ; https://hekint.org/?p=42970

F16. A scanner maps echo strength to brightness and echo return time to depth, assuming sound travels 1,540 m/s in soft tissue; it uses 1-20 MHz. Ultrasound "avoids ionizing radiation", which matters for pregnant patients. A tissue-air boundary reflects nearly all the sound, so gel is spread on the skin; gas and bone block the view.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Medical_ultrasound

## Reflection at work: radar

F17. The Air Ministry asked Robert Watson-Watt early in 1935 whether a radio "death ray" could work. His assistant Arnold Wilkins calculated the radio energy needed to heat 8 pints of water 5 km away and showed it was "impossible given the state of the art"; Wilkins, knowing that shortwave signals bounce off aircraft, suggested detecting aircraft by reflected radio waves instead. Their memo, "Detection and Location of Aircraft by Radio Methods" (February 1935), also proposed timing pulses to measure range.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daventry_Experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Robert_Watson-Watt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Frederic_Wilkins

F18. The Daventry Experiment, 26 February 1935: a receiver and an oscilloscope (a cathode-ray screen) in a van in a field near Upper Stowe, Northamptonshire, used the beam of the BBC's 10 kW, 49.8 m shortwave station at Daventry; a Handley Page Heyford bomber made four passes and produced clear effects on the screen on three of them; the aircraft was detected about eight miles away "by reflection of radio waves for the first time in history" (Wikipedia, Wilkins). Watt later claimed he exclaimed "Britain has become an island again!" The Chain Home stations that followed could detect aircraft up to 100 miles away.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daventry_Experiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Frederic_Wilkins ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Robert_Watson-Watt

F19. Sea water conducts electricity, so it soaks up radio waves: at 1 MHz they reach only about 0.25 m (the "skin depth"), and higher frequencies, such as radar's, even less; only very low frequencies get deeper, with huge aerials and little capacity. Sound (underwater acoustics) is the predominant way to reach a submerged submarine. Physlink: microwaves are strongly absorbed by sea water within feet. MISCONCEPTION: radio would beat sound under water because it is faster.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Communication_with_submarines ; https://www.physlink.com/Education/askExperts/ae456.cfm

## Refraction at work: fibre optics

F20. An optical fibre keeps light in by total internal reflection: the core has a higher refractive index than the cladding around it, and light striking the boundary at more than the critical angle is reflected back into the core. Jean-Daniel Colladon guided lamplight along a curving jet of water in 1841; John Tyndall repeated it in 1854. Fibres carry information as pulses of light, with far more capacity than copper and no electrical interference.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Optical_fiber

F21. Charles Kao, at Standard Telecommunication Laboratories in Harlow, presented his results in January 1966 and published them with George Hockham in July 1966. The fibres of the day lost light at "as high as 1,000 dB/km and even more" (that is, a decibel each metre: 90 percent of the light gone in 10 metres). Kao said "the impurity of glass material is the main cause" of the loss, "rather than fundamental physical effects such as scattering," and that the impurities (such as iron ions and water) could be removed; the limit for glass was below 20 dB/km, and he pointed to pure fused silica. Corning made a fibre below that target in 1970 (17-20 dB/km). Kao shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics "for groundbreaking achievements concerning the transmission of light in fibers for optical communication."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Charles_K._Kao ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_K._Kao ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Optical_fiber

## The Doppler effect

F22. Christian Doppler presented "On the Coloured Light of the Double Stars and Some Other Stars of the Heavens" in Prague on 25 May 1842, proposing that motion of a source changes the frequency received. His use of it to explain star colours was mistaken, though his derivation of the frequency shift was correct (Physics Today).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Doppler_effect ; https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/the-fall-and-rise-of-the-doppler-effect ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect

F23. Christoph Buys Ballot tested it for sound in 1845: he loaded an open railway car with musicians (trumpeters) and sent it along the new line between Utrecht and Maarssen; a cold February attempt was spoiled by hail and snow, so it was rerun in June (KNMI: 3 June 1845), with Buys Ballot on the locomotive's footplate while the trumpeters held a steady note. Listeners beside the track heard the approaching note about a half-tone higher and the receding note a half-tone lower (Physics Today; Grokipedia). KNMI: observers were placed in the train and on the platforms.
    https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/the-fall-and-rise-of-the-doppler-effect ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Doppler_effect ; https://www.knmi.nl/over-het-knmi/nieuws/doppler-experiment-buys-ballot-160-jaar-geleden ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect

F24. Why: in front of an approaching source the wavefronts are compressed, behind it they stretch out; the effect "arises solely from the relative motion between source and observer" and the emitted frequency stays fixed (Grokipedia); "the speed of the wave is determined by the medium, not by the speed of the source" (Wikipedia). MISCONCEPTIONS: the source itself changes its note; the sound travels faster because it rides with the train; louder means higher.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Doppler_effect ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect

F25. Police radar guns (near 24 GHz) find a vehicle's speed from the Doppler shift of the reflected microwaves; Doppler weather radar measures the speed of rain and other precipitation moving toward or away from it, and airport radars detect wind shear.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Doppler_effect ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect

## Digital and analog signals

F26. Analog signals degrade continuously under noise; the noise accumulates along the path and cannot be told apart from the signal afterwards ("Generation loss is irreversible as there is no reliable method to distinguish the noise from the signal", Wikipedia). Repeaters "amplify both the signal and any interfering noise" (Grokipedia). Tape hiss "worsens with each playback or duplication."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Analog_signal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_signal

F27. Digital signals are sent as discrete levels (binary 0s and 1s). Noise below the decision threshold does not change what is read, so "simple thresholding circuits can reconstruct the clean signal," and repeaters can "eliminate accumulated noise at regular intervals during transmission"; extra check bits (parity bits, checksums) let systems find and often correct errors. Digital signals do still pick up noise; they shrug it off only up to the threshold. MISCONCEPTIONS: digital signals pick up no noise at all; digital pulses travel faster than an analog signal.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Digital_signal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Analog_signal

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- Multiplying frequency by wavelength gives a wave's energy or power (F1, F2: it gives its speed).
- Light added to light can only make more light (F4-F6: crest on trough cancels).
- Any copy of the noise, at any timing, cancels it (F9: half a wave out and it adds).
- The echo time gives the distance directly (F13: halve the round trip).
- A fluid-filled cyst echoes like solid flesh (F14).
- Radio works under water because it is fast (F19: sea water soaks it up).
- Light leaks out of a fibre through its sides (F20: total internal reflection).
- The moving source changes its own note, or its sound travels faster (F24).
- Digital signals pick up no noise, or travel faster (F27).
