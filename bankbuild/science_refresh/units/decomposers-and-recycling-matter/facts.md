# Decomposers and Recycling Matter: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-25. Grokipedia first; NGSS, National Geographic Education, EPA, Cornell, the National Park Service and the BSCS grade-5 food-web module to corroborate.

## Facts

F1 Decomposers are living things that break down dead plants, dead animals and their wastes. Fungi and bacteria are the main decomposers. https://grokipedia.com/page/Decomposer (organisms that break down dead or decaying organic matter, returning nutrients to the ecosystem; bacteria and fungi the main types); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ls2-1-ecosystems-interactions-energy-and-dynamics (LS2.A: "Some organisms, such as fungi and bacteria, break down dead organisms"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/decomposers/ (decomposers feed on dead plants, animal remains and waste)

F2 Most decomposers are too small to see: bacteria are single cells found in every ecosystem. Bacteria and moulds do most of the chemical breaking down; larger animals that can be seen (worms, woodlice, slugs, vultures) play a supporting part. https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/decomposers/ (most decomposers are microscopic; bacteria found in every ecosystem); https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (BSCS/CPP grade 5, idea 12: microorganisms such as bacteria and mould play the major role)

F3 Dead things do not rot by themselves. Rotting needs decomposers: once-living matter sealed away from every living thing would not rot. https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 11: "If you could put once-living matter in a sealed container that was free of any living organisms, rotting wouldn't occur"); https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/rotting-apple-0 (Rotting Apple probe, grade 5: a living agent must break down once-living material)

F4 Fungi such as mushrooms and moulds are decomposers. They cannot make food from sunlight; they release digestive juices (enzymes) outside their bodies onto dead matter, then soak up the broken-down food. https://grokipedia.com/page/Fungus (cannot photosynthesize; release digestive enzymes outside their bodies and absorb the nutrients; decomposers that recycle nutrients); https://grokipedia.com/page/Decomposer (fungi secrete enzymes outside their bodies and absorb the nutrients); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/decomposers/ (fungi have no chlorophyll and get their nutrients from the dead material they break down)

F5 A mushroom is the above-ground fruiting body of a fungus; the fungus's main body (the mycelium) lives in soil or wood. Many mushrooms are decomposers of dead wood and leaves. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mushroom

F6 Moulds are fungi that act as decomposers of dead matter, including food. They spread by spores carried in the air, and moisture is what they need most. Black bread mould grows on bread and fruit and grows fastest at about 25 degrees Celsius in damp conditions. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mold (molds "serve as essential decomposers"; spores; "moisture being the most critical requirement"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Rhizopus_stolonifer (black bread mould; decomposes fruits, grains and bread; fastest near 25 C, humid)

F7 Detritivores, such as earthworms, millipedes, woodlice, termites and dung beetles, eat dead matter and break it into smaller pieces, which speeds up the work of bacteria and fungi. https://grokipedia.com/page/Detritivore (feed on dead organic matter and faeces; earthworms, millipedes, woodlice, termites, dung beetles); https://grokipedia.com/page/Decomposer (animals mainly fragment organic matter, which speeds up microbial action); https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 12)

F8 Scavengers, such as vultures, burying beetles, hyenas and crows, eat dead animals they did not kill; they speed up the breakdown of a dead body, and without them carcasses last longer. https://grokipedia.com/page/Scavenger

F9 Earthworms eat soil and dead plant matter. Their droppings (castings) hold about 5 times more nitrogen and 7 times more phosphorus than the soil around them, and their burrows let air and water into the soil. Their droppings carry plenty of microbes. https://grokipedia.com/page/Earthworm (castings "contain 5 times more nitrogen, 7 times more phosphorus"; burrows aerate the soil and let water in); https://grokipedia.com/page/Detritivore (earthworms aerate the ground; their pellets concentrate fragmented detritus and microbial biomass and are richer in nutrients than the original litter)

F10 Decomposers break dead matter down into simple substances: carbon dioxide and water go into the air, and minerals such as nitrogen and phosphorus are left in the soil. https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 11: by-products released into the air, such as carbon dioxide and water, or left in the soil, such as nitrogen and phosphorus; idea 22); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/decomposers/ (water, carbon dioxide and simple compounds containing nitrogen, phosphorus and calcium); https://grokipedia.com/page/Nutrient_cycle (decomposers release carbon as CO2 and turn organic compounds into inorganic ions in the soil)

F11 Plants take up the nutrients (such as nitrogen and phosphorus) from the soil through their roots, and take carbon dioxide from the air to make their food. The nutrients from the soil are not food: they give a plant no energy. Plants make their own food from sunlight, air and water. https://grokipedia.com/page/Nutrient_cycle (plants assimilate nutrients from the soil, water and atmosphere; roots absorb nitrogen and phosphorus as ions); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ls2-1-ecosystems-interactions-energy-and-dynamics (clarification: air, water and decomposed materials in soil are changed by plants into food); https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (ideas 5 and 7)

F12 Matter is recycled again and again: it moves between the air and the soil and among plants, animals and microbes. Decomposers neither make nor destroy matter. A rotting thing shrinks, but much of its matter becomes carbon dioxide, and a closed container holding rotting matter weighs the same before and after. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ls2-1-ecosystems-interactions-energy-and-dynamics (LS2.B: "Matter cycles between the air and soil and among plants, animals, and microbes"); https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (ideas 15 and 22); https://grokipedia.com/page/Nutrient_cycle ("the repeated recirculation of essential chemical elements")

F13 Energy is not recycled. It flows through an ecosystem one way, much of it given off as heat that living things cannot use again, so an ecosystem needs a steady supply of new energy, mainly sunlight. Decomposers do not give energy back to plants; they return only matter. https://grokipedia.com/page/Nutrient_cycle (energy flow is one-way; "cannot be recycled within the system"; needs steady input, mainly sunlight); https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (ideas 25 and 26: "Decomposers don't recycle energy")

F14 Decomposers break dead things down to feed themselves: they get the energy and matter they need to live, grow and reproduce from the dead things and wastes they eat. https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 13: "They need food just as all other living organisms do"; idea 11)

F15 Without decomposers, dead leaves, insects and animals would pile up everywhere, and the nutrients locked inside them would never return to the soil for plants. https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/decomposers/ (without decomposers dead leaves, insects and animals would pile up); https://grokipedia.com/page/Decomposer (without them nutrients would stay locked in dead material and ecosystems would decline)

F16 Compost is made by letting decomposers break down kitchen scraps and garden waste: "greens" such as vegetable peels and grass clippings and "browns" such as dry leaves, kept damp (like a wrung-out sponge) and turned to let air in. Bacteria do most of the work, with fungi and earthworms helping. https://grokipedia.com/page/Compost ("Bacteria constitute the primary decomposing organisms in composting"; fungi including moulds; earthworms aid fragmentation; greens and browns; oxygen, moisture, turning); https://www.epa.gov/recycle/composting-home (browns, greens, water and air); https://www.nj.gov/agriculture/farmtoschool/educators/lessons/Grade-5-Break-it-Down.pdf (FoodCorps grade 5: greens, browns, water; "as wet as a wrung-out sponge"; turn the pile)

F17 A compost heap heats up because the microbes give off heat as they break the scraps down; the middle of a heap can reach about 55 to 70 degrees Celsius (130 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit). https://compost.css.cornell.edu/physics.html (heat is "a by-product of the microbial breakdown of organic material"; 40-70 C); https://grokipedia.com/page/Compost (thermophilic phase "often reaching 130-160 F (55-70 C)"); https://www.nj.gov/agriculture/farmtoschool/educators/lessons/Grade-5-Break-it-Down.pdf (grade 5 students graph the temperature as the pile heats up)

F18 Finished compost enriches garden soil with nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, so less fertiliser is needed. Meat, dairy and pet waste are kept out of a home heap, and plastic, metal and glass do not break down in it. https://grokipedia.com/page/Compost; https://www.epa.gov/recycle/composting-home; https://www.nj.gov/agriculture/farmtoschool/educators/lessons/Grade-5-Break-it-Down.pdf (biodegradable and nonbiodegradable sort; compost added to soil to help grow more plants)

F19 A fallen log in a temperate forest rots over roughly 20 to 100 years. Fungi do most of the work, with bacteria and insects. Seedlings often grow on such a log (a nurse log), which gives them nutrients, moisture and shade, and the log's nitrogen, phosphorus and carbon go back into the soil. https://grokipedia.com/page/Nurse_log; https://home.nps.gov/teachers/classrooms/life-in-a-log.htm (NPS, grades 3-5, NGSS 5-LS2-1: fungi, bacteria, earthworms and insects break down the log; it gives seedlings shade, nutrients and water)

F20 Warmth and moisture speed decomposition; dry conditions and cold slow it. Moulds need moisture above all, and bacteria in a compost heap slow down when it is too dry. On a warm, wet rainforest floor, fallen leaves are gone in about half a year to two years, far quicker than in cooler forests. https://grokipedia.com/page/Decomposition ("warmer and wetter conditions generally accelerating the breakdown"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Mold (moisture most critical); https://compost.css.cornell.edu/physics.html (too dry slows bacterial activity); https://grokipedia.com/page/Tropical_rainforest (litter turnover 0.5-2 years, "far quicker than in temperate forests"); https://peerj.com/articles/685 (leaf litter decay faster at warmer sites)

F21 Humus is the dark brown or black, crumbly part of the soil that is left when dead plants and animals have decayed. It is rich in nutrients such as nitrogen and helps soil hold water. Compost is made by people; humus forms naturally. https://grokipedia.com/page/Humus (dark organic fraction of soil formed as microbes break down plant and animal residues; holds water and nutrients); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/humus/ (grades 5-8: "Humus is dark, organic material that forms in soil"; "thick brown or black substance"; nutrients such as nitrogen; compost usually made by people)

F22 Sand, silt and clay are the mineral part of soil: tiny bits of rock broken down over thousands of years, sand the coarsest and clay the finest. They do not come from dead living things. https://kidsgardening.org/lesson-plans-soil-texture-and-composition/ (grades 3-5: sand, silt and clay from rock; organic matter is "the decayed remains of once-living plants and animals")

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1 Dead things rot by themselves, as a result of time or old age; young children think dead things simply disappear. https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/rotting-apple-0; https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 11)

M2 Wind and rain break dead things down, or they dry out or melt away. https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 14); https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/rotting-apple-0 (5th and 6th graders: spreads into the dirt when rain and wind come)

M3 Decomposers are only the things that can be seen (worms, mushrooms); bacteria are overlooked. https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 12); https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/rotting-apple-0

M4 A rotting thing shrinks and its matter disappears (is destroyed). https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 15)

M5 Matter can turn into energy. https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 24)

M6 Energy is recycled; decomposers give energy back to plants. https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (ideas 25 and 26)

M7 Plants get their food from the soil ("plant food"). https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 7)

M8 Decomposers exist only to recycle matter, not to feed themselves. https://www.cpp.edu/respect/resources/documents_5th/gr5.fw_common_student_ideas.pdf (idea 13)

## Revision facts (2026-11-26, the one revision round)

F23 A scavenger feeds on dead or decaying matter such as carrion, eating remains it did not kill, rather than hunting live prey. Vultures are obligate scavengers, and burying beetles and carrion beetles colonise a carcass soon after death; scavenging speeds up the breakdown of a carcass. National Geographic's 5th-grade reading on scavengers says "Vultures are scavengers." https://grokipedia.com/page/Scavenger ("feeds primarily on dead or decaying organic matter, such as carrion or refuse, rather than hunting live prey"; "without having killed the original prey or host"; "Obligate examples include vultures"; "blowflies and burying beetles that colonize dead tissue almost immediately after death"; "Carrion beetles (family Silphidae)"; scavenging "accelerates the decomposition of carcasses"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/role-scavengers-carcass-crunching/5th-grade/ ("Vultures are scavengers"; a scavenger is an "organism that eats dead or rotting biomass")

F24 A predator kills and eats another living thing, its prey; a parasite takes what it needs from a living host, usually without killing it straight away. Neither is a scavenger, which eats what is already dead. https://grokipedia.com/page/Predation ("Predation is a biological interaction in which one organism, the predator, kills and consumes another organism"; the parasite "derives benefits from a living host without typically causing immediate death"; "Unlike incidental scavenging, predation requires the predator to exert effort in locating and overpowering a living victim")

F25 CORRECTION to F9's "5 times more nitrogen and 7 times more phosphorus": a 2019 meta-analysis of 81 studies (405 observations) found earthworm casts on average only about 40 to 48 percent richer than the surrounding soil in total nitrogen, total phosphorus and organic carbon, but far richer in the forms plants can take up at once (mineral nitrogen about 241 percent higher, available phosphorus about 84 percent higher). So castings are "richer" in nitrogen and phosphorus, not "several times" richer. https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/how-fertile-are-earthworm-casts-a-meta-analysis/ (Van Groenigen, Van Groenigen, Koopmans, Stokkermans, Vos and Lubbers, Geoderma 338: 525-535, 2019)

F26 The nutrients a plant takes from the soil are a small part of it: most of a plant's matter (its carbon, hydrogen and oxygen) comes from carbon dioxide in the air and from water, not from the soil. https://grokipedia.com/page/Plant_nutrition (carbon, hydrogen and oxygen come "primarily from atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O), rather than soil minerals"; macronutrients "typically constituting more than 0.1% of the dry weight"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ls1-1-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes ("plant matter comes mostly from air and water, not from the soil")

G1 Humus at this grade: KidsGardening's grades 3-5 soil lesson teaches organic matter as "the decayed remains of once-living plants and animals", beside "Sand, silt, and clay ... the mineral particles derived from rock broken down over thousands of years"; National Geographic's humus entry is written for grades 5 to 8. https://kidsgardening.org/lesson-plans-soil-texture-and-composition/; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/humus/
