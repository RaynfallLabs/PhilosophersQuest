## The shared system of units

F1. The International System of Units (SI), the modern metric system, is the global standard for physical quantities in science, engineering and commerce. It was established at the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1960 and has seven base units.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/International_System_of_Units ("serves as the global standard for expressing physical quantities in science, engineering, commerce, and daily life"; "officially established at the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM)"; "It consists of seven base units")

F2. The base units include the metre (length), the kilogram (mass) and the second (time); temperature's base unit is the kelvin. The other three are the ampere, the mole and the candela.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/International_System_of_Units ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metre ("the base unit of length in the International System of Units (SI)") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kilogram ("the SI unit of mass ... one of seven base units") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Second ("the base unit of time in the International System of Units (SI)")

F3. The litre is a unit of volume "accepted for use with" the SI, though not itself an SI unit: one litre is one cubic decimetre, exactly 1,000 cubic centimetres, and a millilitre (a thousandth of a litre) is one cubic centimetre.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Litre ("a non-SI unit accepted for use with the International System of Units (SI)"; "equal to one cubic decimetre (dm3), or exactly 1,000 cubic centimetres (cm3)")

F4. The degree Celsius is an SI derived unit equal in size to the kelvin. At standard atmospheric pressure, water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius and boils at 100.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Celsius ("the degree Celsius (C) is a derived unit equal in magnitude to the kelvin (K)"; "the freezing point of water under standard atmospheric pressure is defined as 0 C and the boiling point as 100 C")

F5. Shared SI symbols let written technical information be understood across languages, whatever the spelling or sound of the words.
    https://www.nist.gov/pml/owm/metric-si-prefixes (the SI helps "written technical information" be "effectively communicated")

F6. The Mars Climate Orbiter, a NASA spacecraft, was launched on 11 December 1998 and approached Mars on 23 September 1999 for its orbit-insertion burn. Its signal was lost about five minutes into the burn, and NASA presumed it lost. It passed about 57 kilometres above the surface, far lower than planned.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mars_Climate_Orbiter ("was launched on December 11, 1998"; "approached Mars on September 23, 1999, for orbit insertion"; signal dropped "approximately five minutes after the planned 16-minute Mars orbit-insertion burn began"; "presumed lost"; "the periapsis occurred at approximately 57 km")

F7. The cause: Lockheed Martin, which built the spacecraft, wrote ground software that gave the thrusters' results in pound-force seconds, an English (US customary) unit, while the navigators' software at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory expected newton-seconds, the metric unit. JPL's own words: "one team used English units (e.g., inches, feet and pounds) while the other used metric units". The spacecraft team was in Colorado, the navigation team in California. NASA's Edward Weiler also blamed the failure of the checks that should have caught the error.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mars_Climate_Orbiter ("pound-force seconds (lbf s)"; JPL's software "expected newton-seconds (N s)"; "Lockheed Martin Astronautics was selected as the prime contractor") ; https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/mars-climate-orbiter-team-finds-likely-cause-of-loss/ ("one team used English units (e.g., inches, feet and pounds) while the other used metric units"; "the Mars Climate Orbiter spacecraft team in Colorado and the mission navigation team in California")

## Prefixes

F8. SI prefixes make decimal multiples and fractions of a unit by powers of ten, and the same prefixes are put on base and derived units alike (on the metre, the gram, the litre, the second). The French Academy of Sciences introduced the first metric prefixes on 7 April 1795.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Metric_prefix ("decimal multiples or submultiples of SI units by factors of 10n"; "Prefixes are applied to SI base units and derived units"; "On 7 April 1795, the French Academy of Sciences formally introduced the initial set of metric prefixes") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/International_System_of_Units ("decimal multiples and submultiples of SI units by powers of 10")

F9. Kilo- means a thousand (from the Greek khilioi, "thousand"): a kilogram is 1,000 grams and a kilometre 1,000 metres.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Metric_prefix ("kilo- derives from the Greek khilioi meaning 'thousand'") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kilogram ("the kilogram equaled exactly 1,000 grams") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metre ("kilometre (1,000 m)") ; https://www.nist.gov/pml/owm/metric-si-prefixes (kilo, 10^3, "thousand")

F10. Centi- means a hundredth (from the Latin centum, "hundred"): a centimetre is a hundredth of a metre, so 100 centimetres make a metre.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Metric_prefix (centi, 10^-2, Latin "centum", "hundred") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metre ("the centimetre (1/100 m)") ; https://www.nist.gov/pml/owm/metric-si-prefixes (centi, 10^-2, "one-hundredth")

F11. Milli- means a thousandth (from the Latin mille, "thousand"): a millimetre is a thousandth of a metre, a millilitre a thousandth of a litre, a millisecond a thousandth of a second.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Milli- ("denotes a factor of one thousandth"; "originates from the Latin word mille, meaning 'thousand'"; "the millimeter (mm) for length or the milligram (mg) for mass"; "milliliter for volume"; "The millisecond (ms), representing one-thousandth of a second") ; https://www.nist.gov/pml/owm/metric-si-prefixes (milli, 10^-3, "one-thousandth")

F12. The other prefixes used for wrong choices: micro- is a millionth (10^-6), mega- a million (10^6), deci- a tenth (10^-1), hecto- a hundred (10^2), deca- ten (10^1). So a decimetre is a tenth of a metre and a hectometre is 100 metres.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Metric_prefix (table: hecto 10^2, Greek "hundred"; deca 10^1; deci 10^-1, Latin "tenth"; micro 10^-6; mega 10^6) ; https://www.nist.gov/pml/owm/metric-si-prefixes ("deka, hecto, and kilo meaning, respectively, 10, 100, and 1000"; micro "millionth")

## Two kinds of data

F13. Qualitative data are descriptions: "descriptive information that does not use numbers", such as colour or smell. Quantitative data are "measured information using numbers". Both are evidence in science.
    https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/qualitative-data/ (grades 5-8: qualitative data are "descriptions of the qualities of whatever it is you're studying"; glossary: "descriptive information that does not use numbers"; quantitative: "measured information using numbers") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Observation (qualitative "describes attributes in descriptive terms", "the sky appears cloudy"; quantitative "employs numerical metrics"; "Both forms are essential")

F14. An observation records what is directly perceived ("the liquid turns red"); an inference is the conclusion drawn from it ("the substance is acidic based on the color change").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Observation

F15. The grade's standards (Texas, grade 6, 19 TAC 112.26): 6.1E "collect quantitative data using the International System of Units (SI) and qualitative data as evidence"; 6.1F "construct appropriate tables, graphs, maps, and charts using repeated trials and means to organize data"; 6.2B "analyze data by identifying any significant descriptive statistical features, patterns, sources of error, or limitations".
    https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-26 ; https://teksguide.org/teks/s61e/alignments

## Repeated trials, odd results and spread

F16. Repeated observations let a result be checked and confirmed; a single result needs cross-checking before conclusions rest on it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Observation (observations "can be repeated by others under similar conditions to confirm data integrity"; "conclusions rest on reproducible data rather than conjecture")

F17. An outlier is a result that lies far from the others. It can come from a measurement error or a slip in the equipment, or from real but rare variation, so it is investigated: it is corrected or removed only when shown to be an error, every such decision is written down, and removing a point merely because it is an outlier is frowned upon by many scientists and science teachers.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Outlier ("an observation that lies an abnormal distance from other values"; "Measurement errors constitute a primary source of outliers"; "deleting outlying points only if they are confirmed erroneous"; "Researchers must document all decisions transparently"; "selective removal without justification can mislead interpretations") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outlier ("A physical apparatus for taking measurements may have suffered a transient malfunction"; "it is more desirable to correct the erroneous value, if possible"; removing a point "solely because it is an outlier" is "a controversial practice, often frowned upon by many scientists and science instructors"; exclusions "should be clearly stated")

F18. Spread (dispersion) is how far results differ from one another; the range (largest minus smallest) is its simplest measure. A small spread means the results cluster closely and are reliable; a large spread shows more variability and more uncertainty, and tells how far the average can be trusted.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Statistical_dispersion ("The range is the simplest measure of statistical dispersion"; "Low dispersion indicates that data points cluster closely around the central value", linked to "high reliability"; "high dispersion reveals greater variability"; dispersion lets analysts "interpret the reliability and context of the average")

F19. Repeatability is how closely repeated measurements of the same thing, made the same way, agree; the smaller their spread, the more repeatable the measurement, and a wide scatter means the measurement needs improving.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Repeatability ("the closeness of agreement between the results of successive measurements of the same measurand"; "lower values indicate higher repeatability"; wide spread signals "the measurement system needs improvement")

F20. The scatter of repeated readings is used to estimate a measurement's uncertainty, and taking more readings shrinks the uncertainty of their average.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Measurement_uncertainty (Type A: "the statistical analysis of a series of repeated observations"; the uncertainty of the mean is found by "dividing the standard deviation by the square root of the number of observations")

F21. Random errors scatter results both ways and can be reduced by averaging many trials; systematic errors (such as a scale with a zero error) shift every reading the same way and do not shrink with repeated trials. So results that agree closely are not proof that they are right.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Observational_error ("Random errors result from unpredictable fluctuations"; "can be minimized through averaging multiple trials"; systematic errors shift "all measurements in the same direction" and "do not diminish with increased sample size or repeated trials"; "zero error in a scale")

## Sources of error

F22. Human sources of error include misreading an instrument's scale, parallax from looking at a slant, and inconsistent reaction times in timing experiments.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Observational_error ("misreading an instrument scale"; "parallax errors from angled observations"; "inconsistent reaction times in timing experiments")

F23. In hand timing with a stopwatch, "the uncertainty arises primarily from the observer's reaction at start and stop, typically around 0.2 seconds" (up to about 0.3 seconds in all), while a digital stopwatch displays hundredths of a second. Hand timing carries reaction-time errors of up to about 0.2 seconds, which is why races moved to electronic timing; a simple reaction to something seen takes about 150 to 250 milliseconds.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Stopwatch ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fully_automatic_time ("manual timing with stopwatches, which introduces human reaction time errors of up to 0.2 seconds") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mental_chronometry ("150 to 250 milliseconds for visual stimuli in young adults")

F24. Ordinary quartz clocks and watches keep time to within about 15 to 20 seconds a month, so a stopwatch's own clock drifts far too little to matter in a fall lasting about a second.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Quartz_clock ("typically ranges from 15 to 20 seconds per month")

F25. Near Earth's surface gravity gives every falling object the same acceleration, about 9.8 metres per second each second, the same for all bodies at a given place; it differs only slightly from place to place (about 9.780 at the equator to 9.832 at the poles), with only tiny short-term wobbles. So a ball dropped the same way from the same spot falls in the same time.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gravity_of_Earth ("approximately 9.8 m/s2"; g "remains constant for all bodies at a given location"; "around 9.780 m/s2" at the equator, "approximately 9.832 m/s2" at the poles; "short-term fluctuations" from tides and the like)

F26. Evaporation is measured as the water lost from an open pan over time, and any water lost some other way that is not accounted for (splashed out, for instance) makes the measured evaporation too high. A leak is the same kind of unaccounted loss.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pan_evaporation ("Water level is observed at consistent times, usually daily, to capture net loss due to evaporation"; "splash-out from wind, heavy rain, or animal interference, which causes unaccounted water loss", overstating evaporation). The leak is author's reasoning from this rule; the page itself does not mention leaks.

F27. When a result is off, students should say why it is higher or lower than expected: "human error" alone is not enough, and the procedure and the apparatus shape the measurements.
    https://edu.rsc.org/ideas/help-students-explain-anomalous-results/3008915.article ("It is important to be specific and explain why the anomalous result is higher or lower than the expected result"; "the nature of the experimental procedure and the apparatus influence the measurements taken")

## Misconceptions a 6th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Milli- is taken for "million" because the words sound alike; the prefixes for a millionth (micro-) and a million (mega-) are different ones (F11, F12). Metric symbols are also confused by case (M is mega, m is milli): https://www.nayuki.io/page/common-mistakes-when-using-the-metric-system (seen in search). The sound-alike mix-up itself is author's judgment; no study found.

M2. A prefix that means "a hundred" or "a thousand" is mixed up with the one that means "a hundredth" or "a thousandth" (hecto- with centi-, kilo- with milli-). Author's judgment, from F9 to F12.

M3. An odd result is either quietly erased to tidy the data or trusted over the results that agree. Both are wrong by F17.

M4. A wider spread is thought to mean more or better data, or the number of trials alone is thought to make a result trustworthy, or any variation at all is thought to spoil it. Author's judgment; wrong by F18 to F20 (a small spread is normal, a large one lowers trust).

M5. "Less water left" is read as "less evaporation", mixing up the water left with the water lost; a leak in fact makes evaporation look larger (F26).

M6. Differences between hand-timed trials are blamed on the falling ball or on gravity changing, rather than on the timer's reactions (F23, F25).

M7. NOT used: Grokipedia's Mars Climate Orbiter page states the error's direction inconsistently (a factor of 4.45 over- or under-estimated), so no question or context says which way the figures were off; JPL's plain statement (F7) is used instead.

## Added at revision (2026-11-26)

F28. For repeated measurements of the same quantity, a suspect value "may be thrown out provided that fact is well documented and that the measurement is repeated a number of times more" (University of Toronto physics error-analysis notes); school practice sets an anomalous repeat aside before working out the mean, and repeats the measurement (Save My Exams revision notes). So an odd run is checked and rerun, not averaged in as it is, and the runs that agree need no rerun.
    https://faraday.physics.utoronto.ca/PVB/Harrison/ErrorAnalysis/Rejection.html ; https://www.savemyexams.com/international-a-level/physics/edexcel/19/revision-notes/6-practical-skills-in-physics-ii/practical-skills-ii-implementation-and-measurements/6-13-anomalous-readings/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outlier

F29. The joule is "the derived unit of energy, work, and heat in the International System of Units (SI)", named for James Prescott Joule; the kilojoule is "equivalent to 10^3 joules" (1,000 J), and kilojoules are used for "food nutrition" values on food labels. The European Union "mandates declaration of energy values primarily in kilojoules (kJ)" on food labels.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Joule ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_energy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metric_prefix

F30. "The period of oscillation for a simple pendulum is independent of the mass and amplitude (for small angles)"; Galileo noted the period is "roughly independent of swing amplitude", and pendulums regulated clocks. So a pendulum keeps very nearly the same time from swing to swing, and a wide spread in timing ten swings comes from the measuring, not from the pendulum.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pendulum

F31. Random errors make "observed values to scatter around the true value in an unbiased manner" (some too high, some too low), and "these can be minimized through averaging multiple trials"; systematic errors "do not diminish with increased sample size or repeated trials". Gross errors (blunders) "arise from human mistakes or procedural lapses", such as misreading a scale. So in an average, too-long and too-short times partly cancel, but no number of runs removes every error. (Re-read for the added averaging question; see also F20, F21.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Observational_error ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Measurement_uncertainty
