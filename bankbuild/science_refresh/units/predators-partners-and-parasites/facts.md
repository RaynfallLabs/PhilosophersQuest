# Predators, Partners and Parasites (T2, 6th grade, life strand)

Curriculum line G6.L5: relationships between species; predator and prey;
competition; symbiosis in three forms (mutualism, commensalism, parasitism).
Sources were read on 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; a second source is given

## Symbiosis and its three kinds

F1. Symbiosis is a close, long-term interaction between two different species living together; biologists count three main kinds: mutualism, commensalism and parasitism. Predation is not counted as symbiosis.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Symbiosis ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology

F2. The kinds are told apart by what each partner gets: in mutualism both benefit; in commensalism one benefits and the other neither benefits nor is harmed; in parasitism one (the parasite) benefits at the cost of the other (the host).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mutualism_(biology) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Commensalism ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Parasitism ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology

F3. Symbiosis is not always mutualism: Wikipedia says "symbiotic relationships are not always mutualistic" and "mutualistic interactions are not always symbiotic". Some scientists once used "symbiosis" for mutualism alone (OpenStax: "some restrict the term to only those species that are mutualistic"; Grokipedia: "sometimes interpreted more narrowly as mutualism").
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutualism_(biology) ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Symbiosis

## Mutualism

F4. Bees and flowers: bees use flowering plants as their food source (nectar and pollen), and as they feed they carry pollen to other flowers ("bees transferring pollen between flowers while collecting nectar"), which pollinates the plant. Wikipedia: "Bees use these plants as their food source with pollen and nectar" and they "transfer pollen to other nearby flowers".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mutualism_(biology) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutualism_(biology)

F5. Pollination is the transfer of pollen to the stigma of the same or another flower; it starts fertilization and lets the plant make seeds and fruit. Pollen sticks to the hairs on a bee's body.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pollination

F6. Gut bacteria: the human gut holds trillions of bacteria (about 38 trillion bacterial cells, 2016 estimate), most of them in the large intestine (the colon). They ferment dietary fibre that the body cannot digest itself, and they make vitamins such as vitamin K and some B vitamins.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gut_microbiota ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gut_microbiota ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mutualism_(biology)

F7. Wikipedia calls the relationship between some gut microbiota and humans "not merely commensal (a non-harmful coexistence), but rather a mutualistic relationship"; the gut's mucus layer gives the microbes a place to "anchor and feed", and they feed on the undigested fibre that reaches them.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gut_microbiota ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Human_microbiome

F8. A lichen is a fungus living in partnership with a green alga or a cyanobacterium. The alga makes carbohydrates (sugars) by photosynthesis that nourish both partners; the fungus gives structure, protection and access to water and minerals. Lichens grow on bare rock ("pioneers on barren rocks"). Grokipedia and OpenStax both call it mutualism.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lichen ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology

F9. Legumes such as peas, beans, clover and soybeans grow root nodules that hold rhizobia bacteria. The bacteria turn nitrogen gas from the air into ammonia, a form the plant can use (nitrogen fixation); the plant gives the bacteria carbohydrates (sugar-derived compounds). Grokipedia calls it a mutualistic symbiosis.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rhizobia ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_nodule ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mutualism_(biology)

F26. Nitrogen gas makes up about 78% of Earth's atmosphere, but its stable triple bond makes it "inaccessible to most organisms", plants included, until bacteria fix it. In the root nodules of legumes, rhizobia turn it into ammonium the plant can use, in exchange for carbohydrates; legume rotations were an old way to keep up soil nitrogen (Boussingault's 1830s farm trials found legumes "maintained or increased it").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nitrogen_fixation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_nodule

## Commensalism

F10. Epiphytes such as orchids and bromeliads grow on tree trunks and branches; they gain height and light without drawing nutrients from the tree, and Grokipedia names them as a form of commensalism. They take water and nutrients from the air, rain, mist and debris; orchids have water-absorbing root tissue (velamen). Epiphytes "do not draw nourishment" from the host (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Commensalism ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Epiphyte ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epiphyte ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Symbiosis

F11. True commensalism is hard to prove: small costs or benefits to the host "may exist but remain undetected", and "purely neutral effects are debated in nature". OpenStax's plain example is birds nesting in trees.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Commensalism ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology

## Parasitism

F12. A parasite lives in or on another living thing, its host, and gets food or shelter from it at the host's expense. It usually harms the host "without usually causing immediate death"; a predator kills and eats its prey.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Parasitism ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Predation ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology

F13. OpenStax: a parasite "is unlikely to kill the host, especially not quickly", since that would leave no time to complete its life cycle by spreading to another host.
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology

F14. Ticks are parasitic arachnids (not insects) that feed on the blood of mammals, birds and reptiles. A hard tick stays attached for days or weeks until it is full, then drops off. Ticks cause blood loss and spread diseases such as Lyme disease.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tick ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tick

F15. Tapeworms are parasites that live attached to the wall of a vertebrate's intestine and absorb nutrients from the host's digested food.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Parasitism ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology

F16. Mistletoe is a hemiparasite: its green leaves photosynthesize and make some of its own sugars, but a root-like organ (the haustorium) grows into the host branch and draws water and minerals from the host's xylem. Birds eat its berries and spread its sticky seeds. It can reduce the host's growth, kill outer branches, and a heavy infestation may kill the tree.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mistletoe ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mistletoe ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Parasitism

F17. Spanish moss, by contrast, is "a non-parasitic epiphyte that does not harm healthy trees": it takes water and nutrients from the air, rain and debris through scales on its leaves. It is neither a moss nor a lichen but a bromeliad.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spanish_moss ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Epiphyte

## Predation and the predator-prey cycle

F18. Predation: one living thing, the predator, kills and eats another, the prey. Predation drives population cycles, such as the roughly 10-year rise and fall of Canada lynx and snowshoe hares, in which predator numbers lag behind prey booms and crashes.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Predation ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology

F19. Snowshoe hares are most of the lynx's diet (40-96% of diet biomass; 60-90% in peak hare years). The Hudson's Bay Company's ledgers of lynx pelts from trappers, 1845 to 1935, show boom-and-bust peaks that track hare numbers.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Canada_lynx ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Snowshoe_hare

F20. Lynx numbers peak one to two years after the hares peak (Grokipedia Canada lynx: "Lynx densities typically peak 1 to 2 years after hare maxima"; OpenStax: the predator lags "1-2 years behind that of the prey population"). The cycle lasts about 10 years (Grokipedia: 8 to 11 years for hares; 9 to 11 for lynx).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Canada_lynx ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Snowshoe_hare ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology

F21. Why the numbers rise and fall together (OpenStax): as hares increase there is more food for lynx, so lynx increase; lynx then kill so many hares that the hares decline, followed by a lynx decline "because of scarcity of food". Grokipedia: starvation during hare lows is a leading cause of lynx death, kitten survival falls to near zero 1-2 years after a crash, and lynx fertility drops. Food for the hares matters too: food added in Yukon experiments tripled hare densities, and food scarcity and predation "interact synergistically".
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Canada_lynx ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Snowshoe_hare

F22. Snowshoe hares do not dig burrows to shelter in; they rest in "shallow depressions called forms" scraped in snow, leaf litter or soft ground (they tunnel into snow only to reach buried food).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Snowshoe_hare

## Misconceptions a 6th grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Symbiosis means both partners gain (the everyday sense, and an older narrow definition). In biology it covers parasitism and commensalism too (F1, F3).
M2. Any plant growing on a tree is a parasite (orchids, Spanish moss). Most are epiphytes that take nothing from the tree (F10, F17); mistletoe, which grows into the branch, is the parasite (F16).
M3. A plant with green leaves that makes its own food cannot be a parasite. Mistletoe makes sugar and still takes the tree's water (F16).
M4. Any animal that feeds on another is a predator, so a tick drinking blood is a predator. A predator kills its prey; a parasite feeds on a host that usually lives on (F12, F14).
M5. Predator and prey peak in the same year, or the predator peaks first. The predator lags the prey by one to two years (F20).
M6. Predators hold back or stop hunting to leave prey for later, or prey escape into burrows. In fact the predators starve and grow fewer when prey run short (F21), and snowshoe hares have no burrows (F22).
M7. The bacteria in the gut are all germs. Many are partners that digest fibre and make vitamins (F6, F7).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26; read on the web this day)

F27. Tapeworms (cestodes) have no gut. An adult fastens itself to the wall of its host's intestine with its scolex ("a holdfast organ equipped with suckers, hooks, or spines") and absorbs nutrients "directly through their tegument", its outer covering; the beef tapeworm absorbs "sugars and amino acids". Egg-filled segments leave the host in the faeces, and new hosts are infected by swallowing the eggs or larvae. OpenStax: "The tapeworm can live inside the intestine of the host for several years, benefiting from the food the host is eating"; the adult beef tapeworm "can persist in the human host for up to 25 years" and is "often asymptomatic". Adults compete with the host for nutrients (Diphyllobothrium latum can cause vitamin B12 deficiency).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cestoda ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Taenia_saginata ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology

F28. Why a parasite seldom kills its host: OpenStax, a parasite "is unlikely to kill the host, especially not quickly, because this would allow no time for the organism to complete its reproductive cycle by spreading to another host". Grokipedia Parasitism: "Obligate parasites require a host to complete their life cycle and cannot survive independently"; parasite adaptations "avoid premature host death, which would interrupt transmission"; "parasitism weakens the host gradually over time". (No source has a tapeworm leaving a weakening host, taking only food the host does not need, or helping its host digest: the adult stays fastened for years and takes nutrients the host would otherwise absorb, F27.)
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-6-community-ecology ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Parasitism

F29. Ticks harm their hosts by "energy depletion, anemia, and pathogen transmission", and "heavy tick burdens have been documented to reduce reproductive success and survival rates" (Grokipedia Tick). Deer do not get Lyme disease: "Deer are a dead-end host for the Lyme disease bacteria"; deer do not "contract the disease when an infected tick feeds on them" (Pennsylvania Game Commission); reservoir competence is "negligible in deer" (Grokipedia Tick). So a heavy load of ticks can wear an animal down, but Lyme disease is no cost to the deer.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tick ; https://www.pa.gov/agencies/pgc/wildlife/wildlife-health/wildlife-diseases/lyme-disease.html

F30. The lynx and hare records: the lynx series are fur returns (the Hudson's Bay Company's; F19) running 1821 to 1939; the classic hare series (MacLulich, 1845-1935) "are really two different sets of data": "1844-1904 represents fur records, whereas 1905-1935 derives from questionnaires" (Stenseth et al. 1997, PNAS). So a stem may say the Company counted lynx furs for close to a century and that hare records cover the same years, but not that hare furs were sold to it for the whole century.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC24646 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Canada_lynx

F31. Food is moved along the gut by peristalsis, "coordinated contractions of circular and longitudinal smooth muscles" in the gut wall: the gut's own muscles do the squeezing, not its bacteria.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Peristalsis

F32. Bile is made by liver cells ("synthesized and secreted continuously by hepatocytes") and breaks fat up: "Bile facilitates the digestion of dietary fats primarily through the emulsifying action of its bile salts", which break large fat globules into small droplets for lipase.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Bile

F33. Decomposers, chiefly bacteria and fungi, break dead leaves and other dead matter down "into simpler inorganic substances" (carbon dioxide, water and mineral nutrients) and release "inorganic compounds back into the soil or water" for plants. They hand plants no sugar; a legume makes its own sugar and pays its root bacteria with it (F9, F26).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Decomposer
