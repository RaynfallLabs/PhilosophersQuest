## The three kinds of rock

F1. Rocks are grouped into three main kinds, igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic, and each kind is defined by how the rock formed. A rock's physical properties (colour, texture, grain size) are the result of the processes that formed it.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_(geology) ("Rocks are usually grouped into three main groups: igneous rocks, sedimentary rocks, and metamorphic rocks"; "These physical properties are the result of the processes that formed the rocks") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rock_(geology) ("broadly classified into three primary types... distinguished by their formation processes, mineral content, and physical characteristics")

F2. By volume, Earth's crust is about 65% igneous rock, 27% metamorphic and 8% sedimentary; sedimentary rock nonetheless covers about 75% of the land surface, as a thin skin.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_(geology) (65%, 27.4%, 7.9% of the crust by volume) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sedimentary_rock ("only about 5% of the Earth's total crustal volume"; "approximately 75% of the planet's land surface")

## Igneous rock

F3. Igneous rock forms when melted rock cools and hardens: magma that cools underground, or lava that cools at the surface. The name is from the Latin word for fire.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Igneous_rock ("formed from the cooling and solidification of molten rock material, originating from magma") ; https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-are-igneous-rocks ("Igneous rocks (from the Latin word for fire) form when hot, molten rock crystallizes and solidifies")

F4. Cooling speed sets crystal size. Magma trapped deep underground cools slowly, so its mineral crystals have a long time to grow and the rock is coarse-grained (intrusive or plutonic rock). Lava at or near the surface cools quickly, so the crystals have little time to grow and the rock is fine-grained (extrusive or volcanic rock).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Igneous_rock ("The slow cooling rate enables the growth of large mineral crystals"; "quick cooling that inhibits large crystal growth") ; https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-are-igneous-rocks ("Slow cooling means the individual mineral grains have a very long time to grow"; "Quick cooling means that mineral crystals don't have much time to grow") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_rock ("the magma cools slowly, and intrusive rocks are coarse-grained")

F5. Granite is a coarse-grained igneous rock whose crystals are visible to the naked eye. It forms by the slow crystallization of magma deep in the crust, at depths of about 5 to 10 km, cooling over some 100,000 to 1,000,000 years. Its minerals are mainly quartz and feldspar (light) with some mica such as biotite (dark), and it is nearly always massive, with no internal layers or structure.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Granite ("coarse-grained, intrusive igneous rock"; "individual mineral crystals are visible to the naked eye"; "depths of 5-10 km"; "slow cooling rates, spanning 10^5 to 10^6 years"; "quartz, alkali feldspar, plagioclase feldspar, and minor amounts of biotite or muscovite mica") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite ("slowly cools and solidifies underground"; "Granite is nearly always massive (lacking any internal structures)")

F6. Basalt is a dark (black or dark grey), fine-grained igneous rock formed when lava cools quickly at the surface; the fast cooling keeps its crystals too small to see with the naked eye. It is the most abundant igneous rock at Earth's surface and the main rock of the ocean crust.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Basalt ("fine-grained, aphanitic texture arises from this rapid cooling"; "preventing the growth of large crystals visible to the naked eye"; "typically black or dark gray"; "the dominant rock type in Earth's oceanic crust") ; https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-are-igneous-rocks (basalt among the common extrusive rocks)

F7. Obsidian is a natural volcanic glass, usually black and glossy, with no crystal structure. It forms when silica-rich lava cools so rapidly that mineral crystals have almost no time to grow. It breaks with very sharp edges, and ancient peoples made cutting tools, blades and arrowheads from it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Obsidian ("naturally occurring volcanic glass formed as an extrusive igneous rock"; "rapidly, preventing the growth of mineral crystals"; "extremely sharp edges"; "crafting cutting tools, arrowheads, and blades"; "black or dark color with a glossy, vitreous luster") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsidian ("formed when lava extruded from a volcano cools rapidly with minimal crystal growth"; "Produced from felsic lava"; "it fractures with very sharp edges")

F8. Granite that formed kilometres down reaches the surface by uplift and erosion: the land is raised and the rock above it is worn away. Exposed granite forms domes, cliffs and tors.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Granite ("Granite, exposed at the Earth's surface after uplift and erosion"; granite domes and cliffs; "tors") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite (exposed "when overlying material is removed by erosion or other processes")

## Metamorphic rock

F9. Metamorphic rock forms when an existing rock is changed by heat, pressure or hot, mineral-rich fluids while it stays solid: metamorphism does not melt the rock (typically at about 150 to 800 degrees Celsius). Rock that did melt would become magma, and then igneous rock.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Metamorphic_rock ("solid-state recrystallization of a preexisting rock"; "typically ranging from 150 C to 800 C") ; https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-are-metamorphic-rocks ("The process of metamorphism does not melt the rocks, but instead transforms them into denser, more compact rocks") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Marble ("Unlike melting, which would produce igneous rocks, metamorphism remains solid-state")

F10. Marble is the metamorphic rock made from limestone (or dolostone): its calcite recrystallizes into a mosaic of interlocking crystals. Marble has been a favourite stone for sculpture since ancient Greece and is used in buildings.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Marble ("formed by the recrystallization of limestone or dolostone"; "interlocking calcite crystals"; "a preferred medium for sculpture since the Archaic period in ancient Greece"; "employed in construction") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble ("metamorphism of sedimentary carbonate rocks, most commonly limestone or dolomite")

F11. Contact metamorphism: rock beside an intrusion of hot magma is heated by it, in a zone around the intrusion (the aureole). Limestone there becomes marble as its small calcite crystals grow larger, and the rock stays mostly solid.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphism (the Contact metamorphism section: "Contact metamorphism occurs typically around intrusive igneous rocks as a result of the temperature increase"; "the small calcite crystals in the sedimentary rocks limestone and chalk change into larger crystals"; "the rock remains mostly solid") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metamorphic_rock (contact metamorphism "confined to aureoles surrounding igneous intrusions")

F12. Slate is a fine-grained metamorphic rock made from shale or mudstone (hardened mud and clay) under mild heat and pressure. Pressure turns and regrows its flat clay flakes so they lie in parallel planes, so slate splits easily into thin, flat sheets (slaty cleavage). It is frequently grey, and it has long been used for roof tiles, blackboards and writing slates.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Slate ("fine-grained, foliated, homogeneous metamorphic rock"; "slaty cleavage"; roofing; "thin slate sheets served as erasable writing surfaces in classrooms") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slate ("derived from an original shale-type sedimentary rock composed of clay or volcanic ash"; "low-grade, regional metamorphism"; "Slate is frequently grey in color"; "extensively used for blackboards and individual writing slates")

F13. Quartzite is the metamorphic rock made from quartz-rich sandstone by heat and pressure: the sand grains recrystallize into a strong, interlocking mosaic. It is very hard (7 on the Mohs scale) and breaks across its grains, not around them. (Caution: a sedimentary "orthoquartzite", heavily cemented with silica, also breaks across its grains, so no question uses the fracture as a test of metamorphism.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Quartzite ("formed through the metamorphism of quartz-rich sandstone under high temperatures and pressures"; "recrystallizes the original sand grains into a strong, interlocking mosaic structure"; "ranking 7 on the Mohs scale") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartzite ("Sandstone is converted into quartzite through heating and pressure"; "the individual quartz grains recrystallize"; orthoquartzite "will fracture across grains, not around them")

F14. Gneiss is a high-grade metamorphic rock with distinct light and dark bands, made when its minerals separate and recrystallize under intense heat and pressure. It can form from igneous rock such as granite (orthogneiss) or from sedimentary rock (paragneiss). Foliation, the layering of metamorphic rock, forms when pressure lines up its flat or long minerals.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gneiss ("high-grade metamorphic rock characterized by its distinctive banded or foliated texture"; "distinct light and dark bands"; "the segregation and recrystallization of components under intense heat and pressure"; "Orthogneiss originates from igneous precursors") ; https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-are-metamorphic-rocks ("granite gneiss"; "Foliation forms when pressure squeezes the flat or elongate minerals within a rock so they become aligned")

## Round the cycle

F15. Metamorphic rock that grows hot enough (about 650 to 900 degrees Celsius) partly melts (anatexis). The melt can collect and move upward to form granitic magma, and granite itself forms by the partial melting of crustal rocks, metamorphic rocks among them. So metamorphic rock can become igneous rock.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rock_cycle ("The transition from metamorphic to igneous rocks occurs primarily through anatexis, or partial melting") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Migmatite (anatexis "typically 650-850 C"; "portions of the melt can be extracted and migrate upward, potentially aggregating to form granitic magmas") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite ("granitic melts can be produced in place through the partial melting of metamorphic rocks") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Granite ("The formation of granite involves partial melting of crustal rocks")

F16. Igneous and metamorphic rocks that are uplifted and exposed are weathered and eroded; the pieces become sediment, which can become sedimentary rock. So igneous rock can become sedimentary rock, but only after it is worn down.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rock_cycle (igneous rocks: "Once uplifted and exposed, these rocks undergo physical and chemical weathering"; metamorphic rock "exposed to weathering and erosion") ; https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-are-sedimentary-rocks ("Pieces of rock are loosened by weathering, then transported to some basin or depression where sediment is trapped")

F17. Any of the three kinds of rock can become either of the other two, and the rock cycle runs in no fixed order: igneous rock can be changed straight into metamorphic rock, metamorphic rock can be worn into sediment, and so on. These changes typically take millions of years.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rock_cycle ("any can serve as the parent material for the formation of the others"; "typically spanning millions of years") ; https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/how-to-teach-the-chemistry-of-the-rock-cycle-at-11-14/4012977.article ("The rock cycle proceeds in no particular order"; igneous rock may become metamorphic or sedimentary) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_cycle ("Any pre-existing type of rock can be modified by the processes of metamorphism")

F18. The cycle is driven from inside by Earth's internal heat (mantle convection, plate tectonics) and from outside by the Sun, through weathering and the water cycle. (Background; not asked.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rock_cycle ("propelled by internal Earth dynamics, particularly through mantle convection"; "external (e.g., solar-driven weathering) forces"; "The hydrologic cycle plays a pivotal role") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_cycle ("the driving forces of the rock cycle, plate tectonics and the water cycle")

F19. Dark rock can be any of the three kinds: basalt is igneous, coal is sedimentary (from buried plant matter), and slate is metamorphic.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Basalt (black or dark grey) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sedimentary_rock (coal from "compressed plant matter") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slate (frequently grey; metamorphic)

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. "Metamorphic rocks melt." Recorded by Kortz and Murray (2009) among college students' alternative conceptions of rock formation; metamorphism happens without melting (F9).
    Kortz, K.M. and Murray, D.P., Barriers to college students learning how rocks form, Journal of Geoscience Education 57, 300-315 (2009), Table 4, as reprinted in https://cdn.serc.carleton.edu/files/NAGTWorkshops/earlycareer2015/monday_am_example_misconceptio.pdf

M2. "Rocks come to the surface through volcanoes or earthquakes." Same table (Deep Time barrier). The real route for deep rock such as granite is slow uplift and erosion (F8).
    Same source as M1.

M3. "Granite is made from sediments"; "Magma turns into a black rock and black rocks were magma (i.e. black = igneous)"; "Volcanism is needed to provide the heat for rock formation"; "Pressure to form rocks is caused by things like heat, water, faults, and air". Same table.
    Same source as M1.

M4. Students describe rocks mostly by their physical characteristics and "do not understand that rocks are the consequences of earth processes and not simply a matter of classification" (Kortz and Murray, GSA 2008 abstract). Sorting rocks by colour or hardness is the classroom form of this.
    https://studio.m-anage.com/gsa/2008AM/webprogram/Paper149559.html

M5. "Many students believe there is only one set path through the rock cycle", and students think of the world as unchanging because of the timescales involved (Royal Society of Chemistry teaching guide).
    https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/how-to-teach-the-chemistry-of-the-rock-cycle-at-11-14/4012977.article

M6. Students who have seen erupting volcanoes on film may take eruption to be THE rock-forming process (Victoria Department of Education, "All rocks are recycled"; seen in a search excerpt, the page refused automated fetches).
    https://www.education.vic.gov.au/school/teachers/teachingresources/discipline/science/continuum/Pages/rockrecycle.aspx

M7. CAUTION from the same Kortz and Murray table: "Rocks can change into any other rock" is listed there as an alternative conception (a rock changing straight into any other rock, with no process). The questions therefore say only that a rock can become another KIND of rock through melting, weathering, or heat and pressure (F15-F17).

M8. Author's judgment, used for wrong choices and labelled as such: glass is made from melted sand, so a child may think a natural glass is pressed sand; banding in a metamorphic rock is easily put down to the surface processes a child already knows, frost splitting rock, mud filling cracks or water staining it (the bands are made inside the rock by heat and pressure, F14; frost wedging is the T1 weathering unit's); crystals are thought to grow bigger as a rock grows older, or under harder squeezing, when cooling speed is what sets an igneous rock's crystal size (F4).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F20. Basalt is "the dominant rock type in Earth's oceanic crust", which forms mainly at mid-ocean ridges and leaves "a layer of basaltic rocks averaging 7 km thick across the ocean basins". A middle-school text: "Oceanic crust is made of basalt lavas that flow onto the seafloor", and "The average composition of continental crust is granite."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Basalt ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Earth_Science/03%3A_Planet_Earth/3.17%3A_Earth's_Crust (CK-12 Earth Science) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Crust_(geology) (oceanic crust "primarily basaltic", continental "predominantly granitic")

F21. Limestone forms from "calcium carbonate sediments, such as shells, coral, and other marine organisms", "in warm, shallow marine environments over millions of years". (Lure use: limestone is not the rock of the open ocean floor.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Limestone

F22. Obsidian has ALMOST no crystals, not none: "Impurities such as microcrystals of magnetite (Fe3O4) or pyroxene can occur in trace amounts" (under 1 percent), and snowflake obsidian holds "radiating clusters of white cristobalite inclusions". It forms from felsic (silica-rich) lava ("Produced from felsic lava"), cooled "with minimal crystal growth", while basalt forms from mafic lava; so obsidian is not the rock of the ocean floor.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Obsidian ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsidian ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Basalt

Note on M1 (from the fact-check): Kortz and Murray's table records 'Metamorphic rocks melt' but gives no frequencies, so the grade notes call it "a recorded mix-up", not the commonest one.
