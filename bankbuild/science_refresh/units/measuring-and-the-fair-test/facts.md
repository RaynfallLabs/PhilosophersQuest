## Facts

F1 Length is measured in metres: the metre is the SI base unit of length, a centimetre is one hundredth of a metre, and a kilometre is 1,000 metres. A metric ruler or a metre stick (or a measuring tape) reads length. https://grokipedia.com/page/Metre ("the base unit of length in the International System of Units (SI)"; "the centimetre (1/100 m) and kilometre (1,000 m)"); TEKS 5.1D names "metric rulers" among the grade's tools: https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-7

F2 Mass is measured with a balance, which compares the object with known masses, and is given in kilograms and grams. https://grokipedia.com/page/Weighing_scale (balances compare an unknown with known masses until "equilibrium is achieved"; "Mass is quantified in kilograms (kg)"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass_versus_weight ("Mass is typically measured using a balance scale that compares it to known standards")

F3 The mass of an empty container is its tare. To find the mass of what is inside, take the container's mass away from the total (net = gross minus tare), or set the scale to zero with the empty container on it (the "tare" button). https://grokipedia.com/page/Tare_weight ("Tare weight refers to the mass of an empty container"; "net weight = gross weight - tare weight"; "pressing a dedicated 'tare' button after placing the empty container on the scale")

F4 A graduated cylinder measures the volume of a liquid, in millilitres; one millilitre is one cubic centimetre. A graduated cylinder measures more exactly than a beaker. A litre is 1,000 cubic centimetres, so 1,000 millilitres. https://grokipedia.com/page/Graduated_cylinder ("designed to accurately measure the volume of liquids"; "The measured volume in milliliters (mL) is equivalent to cubic centimeters"; "greater precision than beakers or flasks"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Litre ("a unit of volume in the metric system"; "exactly 1,000 cubic centimetres"); https://www.msnucleus.org/membership/html/k-6/as/scimath/3/assm3_5a.html ("A graduated cylinder measures in milliliters, which is a measure of volume")

F5 Water in a glass cylinder curves down in the middle (a concave meniscus), because water clings to the glass and climbs its sides. The volume is read at the bottom of the curve, with the eye level with it; looking from above or below gives a wrong reading (parallax error). https://grokipedia.com/page/Meniscus_(liquid) (liquid "higher at the container's sides than at the center"; read the bottom; "viewing the meniscus at eye level"; "parallax errors from improper viewing angles"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Graduated_cylinder ("align your eye level with the meniscus to eliminate parallax error"; "the volume is read at the lowest point of this curve"); https://www.msnucleus.org/membership/html/k-6/as/scimath/3/assm3_5a.html (elementary lesson: "Students need to read the meniscus at eye level in order to get an accurate reading"); https://www.wayground.com/admin/quiz/6894cf41e3ce374839c03d4e/how-to-read-graduated-cylinders (a grade-5 quiz keys "Look at the bottom of the meniscus at eye level", seen in search)

F6 A thermometer measures temperature. In a liquid-in-glass thermometer the liquid expands as it warms and climbs the narrow tube. The Celsius scale (degrees Celsius, C) sets 0 at the freezing point of water and divides the range to boiling into 100 degrees. https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermometer ("an instrument designed to measure temperature"; "rely on the thermal expansion of liquids within a capillary tube"; "assigning 0 C to the freezing point of water"; "100 equal degrees"); TEKS 5.1D names "Celsius thermometers" (URL in F1)

F7 A spring scale measures force, such as a pull, by how far its spring stretches: the harder the pull, the farther the spring stretches. Force is measured in newtons, named after Isaac Newton. Schools use spring scales to show the pull needed to lift or move objects. https://grokipedia.com/page/Spring_scale ("used to measure the weight or force acting on an object"; spring scales "measure force in general, distinguishing them from devices that directly assess mass"; the restoring force "directly proportional to the displacement"; dials "marked in ... newtons"; school demonstrations show the "pull needed to lift objects"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton_(unit) ("the derived unit of force"; "named in honour of Sir Isaac Newton")

F8 Weight is a force (measured in newtons), not the amount of matter; a spring scale responds to gravity's pull, a balance compares masses. Mass and weight are often confused in everyday speech. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass_versus_weight ("The confusion between mass and weight often arises in everyday language"; "Weight, being a force, is measured in newtons"; weight is found "with a spring scale that responds to gravitational pull")

F9 A stopwatch (timer) measures how long an event lasts. Timing by hand carries the timer's reaction time, an uncertainty of roughly 0.3 seconds. https://grokipedia.com/page/Stopwatch ("designed to measure the duration of specific events"; "Human reaction time introduces a primary variability"; about plus or minus 0.3 seconds)

F10 A hand lens (magnifying glass) is a single convex lens in a frame with a handle; handheld ones magnify about 2 to 10 times. https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnifying_glass ("a convex lens typically mounted in a frame with a handle"; "magnification powers ranging from 2x to 10x"); TEKS 5.1D names "hand lenses" and "microscopes" (URL in F1)

F11 A magnifying (makeup) mirror is a concave mirror; held close, it gives an enlarged image of up to about 3 times. https://grokipedia.com/page/Curved_mirror ("Makeup mirrors with concave surfaces, held close to the face, produce enlarged upright images (up to 3x magnification)")

F12 A telescope is for observing distant objects. https://grokipedia.com/page/Telescope ("an optical instrument that employs lenses, mirrors, or electronic detectors to observe remote objects")

F13 A compound light microscope magnifies about 40 to 1,500 times and is used to examine cells and microorganisms. https://grokipedia.com/page/Optical_microscope ("total magnifications typically ranging from 40x to 1500x"; used "for examining cells, tissues, and microorganisms")

F14 Bacteria are microorganisms, too small to be seen with the naked eye; most are 0.5 to 5 micrometres long. The eye at reading distance can make out about 0.07 millimetres (70 micrometres) at best, so even 10 times magnified (5 to 50 micrometres) a bacterium is still too small to see; a microscope at 400 times makes a 1-micrometre bacterium 0.4 millimetres. Microorganisms live in water (Leeuwenhoek saw them in lake water). https://grokipedia.com/page/Bacteria ("range in size from 0.5 to 5 um"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Microorganism ("too small to be seen with the naked eye, requiring magnification for observation"; bacteria are microorganisms; "examining lake water from Delft"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Naked_eye (features "as small as approximately 0.07 mm" at 25 cm)

F15 A fair test (a controlled experiment) changes only one thing, the variable being tested, and keeps every other condition the same, so that any difference in the result can be put down to that one change. The changed thing is the independent variable, the result measured is the dependent variable, and the things kept the same are the controlled variables. If more than one thing changes, nobody can tell which change caused the result. https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_control ("ensuring all other conditions remain identical"; lets researchers "attribute any observed differences in outcomes directly to the manipulation"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Dependent_and_independent_variables (independent: "manipulated or controlled by the researcher"; dependent: "measured or observed"; controlled: "factors that researchers intentionally hold constant"; fertiliser and plant-growth example); https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/videos/2066-fair-testing ("A single variable is changed while keeping other variables the same"); TEKS 5.1B ("plan and conduct descriptive and simple experimental investigations", URL in F1)

F16 Children do not use the one-change-at-a-time rule (the control of variables strategy) on their own until about fourth grade, though they can be taught it directly (Chen and Klahr, 1999). https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.800226 ("they do not use this strategy spontaneously until approximately the fourth grade"; "can learn this Control of Variables strategy via direct instruction (Chen and Klahr, 1999)")

F17 Measurements of the same thing vary a little from trial to trial by chance (random error), so scientists repeat their trials; averaging several trials makes the result more reliable, and one trial alone may be off. Repeating does not fix an unfair test or a faulty tool. https://grokipedia.com/page/Observational_error (random error "varies unpredictably in replicate measurements"; it "can be minimized through averaging multiple trials"; systematic errors "do not diminish with increased sample size or repeated trials"; averaging ten timed trials "minimizes variations due to reaction time"); https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/videos/2066-fair-testing (scientists "repeat and reflect on their tests"); NGSS 3-5 practice: plan "fair tests in which variables are controlled and the number of trials considered" (California NGSS, seen in search: https://www2.cde.ca.gov/cacs/science?c0=15&c2=3)

F18 A prediction (hypothesis) is tested by experiment, then kept, changed or thrown out according to the evidence; it is not made true by being believed. A test whose result goes against the prediction still worked: it gave a result to interpret. https://grokipedia.com/page/Hypothesis (researchers "test it through controlled experiments" and "refine or discard it based on evidence"; it must be "testable"); https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/5287/chapter/5 (National Research Council, Science Teaching Reconsidered: students "often express disappointment that an experiment did not work", but experiments are "a means of testing ideas and hypotheses, not of arriving at an expected result"; each experiment "works")

F19 A bigger magnet is not always the stronger one: the kind of magnet matters as much as its size. A neodymium magnet holds about 18 times the magnetic energy of an ordinary ferrite magnet of the same volume, so a ferrite magnet must be much larger to match a small neodymium one. https://grokipedia.com/page/Neodymium_magnet (ferrite magnets "require larger sizes to achieve comparable field strengths to NdFeB"; force "scales with magnet size and grade"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neodymium_magnet ("about 18 times greater than 'ordinary' ferrite magnets by volume"; such magnets "can lift thousands of times their own weight")

F20 Science answers only questions that evidence can settle, such as measurements and observations of the natural world. It does not settle questions of beauty or taste, or what people should do (moral judgments). https://www.commonplanner.com/standards/next-generation-science-standards-35ce4c68/appendix-h-understanding-the-scientific-enterprise-the-nature-of-science-in-ngss/grades-3-4-5-8ac46840 (NGSS Appendix H, grades 3-5: "Science findings are limited to what can be answered with empirical evidence"; "Scientists use tools and technologies to make accurate measurements and observations"); https://undsci.berkeley.edu/understanding-science-101/what-is-science/science-has-limits-a-few-things-that-science-does-not-do/ (aesthetic judgments: "Individuals make those decisions for themselves"; "individual people must make moral judgments"; "Science deals only with natural phenomena")

F21 Data are recorded in tables and graphs. A line graph joins data points with lines and shows how something changes over time; a bar graph compares separate groups; a pie chart shows the parts of a whole; a Venn diagram shows what groups share in overlapping circles; a flow chart shows the steps of a process in order. https://grokipedia.com/page/Line_chart ("a series of data points connected by straight lines"; used to "illustrate changes, trends, and relationships within sequential or time-series data"; bar charts are for "comparing discrete categories"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Pie_chart ("how individual components contribute to an overall total"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Venn_diagram ("overlapping closed curves such as circles"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Flowchart ("a sequence of operations"); TEKS 5.1F names tables, graphs, Venn diagrams and flow charts (URL in F1)

F22 The grade's standards: TEKS 5.1B (plan and conduct descriptive and simple experimental investigations), 5.1D (tools: microscopes, hand lenses, metric rulers, Celsius thermometers, mirrors, scales, glassware, timers), 5.1E (collect observations and measurements as evidence), 5.1F (tables, graphs, Venn diagrams, flow charts); NGSS Nature of Science 3-5 (findings limited to what evidence can answer; tools for accurate measurements). URLs in F1 and F20.

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Mass and weight are the same thing, so a spring scale and a balance measure the same quantity. Grokipedia Mass versus weight (F8).

M2 A test whose result goes against the prediction "did not work" and should be thrown out, or the prediction should be kept because the student is sure of it. National Research Council (F18).

M3 Young children change several things at once and still think the test tells them something; the one-change rule is not used unprompted before about fourth grade (F16). Two related lures: making the tested thing the same as well (removing the variable), and thinking that keeping conditions the same makes the results come out the same.

M4 Reading a graduated cylinder at the top of the curve, where the water climbs the glass, or looking down on it from above (parallax). Grokipedia Meniscus and Graduated cylinder (F5).

M5 Bigger magnets are always stronger. Grokipedia Neodymium magnet says force scales with size AND grade (F19); the lure is the size-only version.

M6 Mass and volume (and their units) are mixed up: bigger is thought to mean more mass. ACER: "27% of 8,433 students believed bigger objects always have more mass than smaller objects": https://www.acer.org/au/news/article/three-science-concepts-primary-students-struggle-to-grasp (cited in telling-materials-apart's sheet; not re-read).

M7 Any magnifier will do for the very small: a hand lens or a magnifying mirror is thought to show what only a microscope can. Lure from F10, F11, F13, F14 (author's choice; no study found).

## Revision (2026-11-26): facts added in the one revision round

F23 (corrects F10 and F11, which the fact-check found too low) Handheld magnifying glasses magnify about 2 to 10 times, and Grokipedia's introduction gives magnifying glasses as "typically achieving magnifications between 2x and 20x"; loupes reach 10 to 30 times. Magnifying (make-up) mirrors are commonly sold at 5, 10, 15 and 20 times, not only the "up to 3x" of Grokipedia's Curved mirror page. So a hand lens or a magnifying mirror makes things look at most about 10 to 20 times bigger: a 1-micrometre bacterium at 20 times is 20 micrometres, still well below the eye's limit of about 70 micrometres (F14), far too little for bacteria. https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnifying_glass ("magnification powers ranging from 2x to 10x"; "between 2x and 20x"); https://zadroinc.com/blogs/news/best-magnification-mirror-guide (guide to 1X, 5X, 10X, 15X and 20X mirrors: "magnification mirrors come in multiple strengths"); https://www.hollywoodmirrors.co.uk/blogs/guide/5x-versus-10x-magnification-mirror (5x and 10x mirrors, seen in search)

F24 Investigations come in kinds. A descriptive investigation makes observations (describing, counting) with no variable changed and no hypothesis to test; an experimental investigation is "a test with identified variables", comparing a changed condition with the rest kept the same. Counting the seeds in a sunflower's head is descriptive, not a fair test. TEKS 5.1B asks 5th graders to "plan and conduct descriptive and simple experimental investigations". https://teksguide.org/teks/s21c ("Some investigations involve simple observations as scientists try to understand a process or behavior"; "An experimental investigation involves a test with identified variables"); https://teksguide.org/taxonomy/term/209741 ("descriptive investigations, which have no hypothesis that tentatively answers the research question"); https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/19-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-112-7 (TEKS 5.1B)

F25 Tally marks count things one mark at a time and keep a running total; a tally chart (a frequency table) records how often each category or value occurs. So a tally chart shows how often something happens, not how a reading changes over time. https://grokipedia.com/page/Tally_marks ("one-to-one correspondence, where each mark denotes a single object, event, or quantity"; "frequency tables to record occurrences of categories or values"; "the total number of tally marks for each category providing its frequency")

F26 Cherry picking is "the selective presentation of evidence that favors a desired conclusion": keeping the results that agree and leaving out those that do not. Leaving out the results that go against a prediction is this error (with F18: a prediction is kept or changed by the evidence). https://grokipedia.com/page/Cherry_picking

M8 Blaming the equipment (deciding the big magnet must be broken) instead of changing the idea, when results go against a prediction. Author's judgment on the judge's suggestion; wrong by F18 and F19 (a small neodymium magnet really can out-pull a bigger magnet of a weaker kind, so the result needs no faulty magnet to explain it).
