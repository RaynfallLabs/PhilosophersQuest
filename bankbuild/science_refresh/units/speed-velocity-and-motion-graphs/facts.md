# Speed, Velocity and Motion Graphs (T3, 7th grade): fact sheet

Acceleration as any change of speed or direction is this unit's by the brief. Newton's first law is left to `inertia-newtons-first-law`; F = ma to T4; orbits to `gravity-and-orbits`.

## Speed and average speed

F1. Speed measures how fast an object moves. It is a scalar: it has a size but no direction.
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed ("Speed is a scalar quantity in physics that measures the rate of motion of an object"; speed "measures only the magnitude of motion without regard to direction"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed ("Speed denotes only how fast an object is moving")

F2. Average speed is the total distance covered divided by the total time taken. A car that travels 100 km in 2 hours has an average speed of 50 km/h.
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed ("computed as the total distance covered divided by the total time"; "a car that travels 100 km in 2 hours has an average speed of 50 km/h"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed

F3. Speed is measured in metres per second (m/s), the scientific unit, or in kilometres per hour (km/h), the usual unit for road travel.
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed ("The metre per second ... is the coherent derived unit of speed"; "The kilometre per hour (km/h) is a common metric-derived unit for expressing speeds in transportation")

F4. An average speed does not describe the changes of speed within a trip. Time spent stopped counts in the total time, so a stop adds time but no distance and pulls the average speed down. Common errors: leaving the stopped time out, or taking the mean of the different speeds instead of total distance over total time.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed ("Average speed does not describe the speed variations that may have taken place during shorter time intervals"); https://thirdspacelearning.com/gcse-maths/ratio-and-proportion/distance-time-graph/ (misconceptions: average speed "incorrectly calculated by finding the mean of the different speeds during a journey"; warns against using only the moving periods and ignoring stationary periods)

F5. Instantaneous speed is the speed at one moment. A car's speedometer shows instantaneous speed, not the average for the trip and not the direction.
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Speedometer ("A speedometer is an instrument that measures and displays the instantaneous speed of a land vehicle"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed ("Instantaneous speed refers to the magnitude of an object's velocity at a precise moment in time"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed ("By looking at a speedometer, one can read the instantaneous speed of a car at any instant")

## Velocity, distance and displacement

F6. Velocity is speed together with a direction (a vector, with size and direction). Two things moving at the same speed in different directions have different velocities.
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Velocity ("Velocity is a fundamental vector quantity in physics that describes both the rate and direction of an object's motion"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed (velocity "is a vector quantity incorporating both magnitude and direction")

F7. Distance travelled is the whole length of the path. Displacement is the straight-line change of position from start to finish, with its direction. A trip that ends where it began has zero displacement.
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Displacement_(geometry) (distance "measures only the total path length traveled and lacks directional information"; displacement "is inherently directional and focuses on the net change in location"; closed paths yield "a net displacement of zero")

F8. Average velocity uses displacement, so a round trip back to the start has an average velocity of zero even though its average speed is not zero. Grokipedia's example: 5 km east, then 5 km west, in one hour is an average speed of 10 km/h but an average velocity of zero.
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Velocity (the 5 km east, 5 km west example; a round trip's average velocity "is zero despite continuous motion and non-zero average speed"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed ("When something moves in a circular path and returns to its starting point, its average velocity is zero")

F9. A standard outdoor running track is a 400-metre oval, so one lap ends where it began.
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Track_and_field ("held on a standardized 400-meter oval track with marked lanes"; "the standard configuration being a 400-meter oval track")

F10. To know whether a moving thing will reach a place, its speed is not enough: its direction is needed too, and speed with direction is its velocity. (Applies F6.)
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Velocity; https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed

## Acceleration

F11. Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes. It happens whenever velocity changes in speed, in direction, or in both: speeding up, slowing down, or changing course. Its unit is metres per second squared (metres per second, per second).
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Acceleration ("Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity of an object with respect to time"; "it occurs whenever velocity changes in speed, direction, or both—such as speeding up, slowing down, or altering course"; SI unit "meters per second squared"); https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-1-acceleration ("Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by a period of time during which the change occurs")

F12. Slowing down is an acceleration too, pointing opposite to the motion. Everyday speech calls it deceleration; physics counts it as acceleration. (Misconception M5: in everyday speech "accelerate" means only "speed up".)
  https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-1-acceleration ("but in physics, it is acceleration—whose direction happens to be opposite that of the velocity"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleration ("an acceleration in the opposite direction of the velocity vector, sometimes called deceleration")

F13. An object at a constant speed accelerates if it changes direction. A car on a curved road at a steady speed has a constantly changing velocity, so it is accelerating; turning the steering wheel of a moving car makes it accelerate. In circular motion at a steady speed the acceleration points toward the centre.
  https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-1-acceleration ("An object traveling at a constant speed does accelerate if it changes direction"; "turning the steering wheel of a moving car makes the car accelerate because the velocity changes direction"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Velocity (a car on a curved road "experiences continuously changing velocity due to shifting directions, even at constant speed"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Acceleration ("centripetal acceleration in circular motion points toward the center, even if speed remains constant"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleration (an object in circular motion "is accelerating due to the change of direction of motion, although its speed may be constant")

F14. Motion at a steady speed in a straight line is motion at a constant velocity, and an unchanging velocity means zero acceleration (no change in velocity). Moving fast is not the same as accelerating.
  https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-1-acceleration (acceleration is the change in velocity over the time taken, so no change gives none); https://grokipedia.com/page/Acceleration (acceleration occurs only "whenever velocity changes")

## Distance-time graphs

F15. On a distance-time graph (distance up the side, time along the bottom), the slope of the line is the speed: the steeper the line, the faster the motion.
  https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-3-position-vs-time-graphs ("the slope in a d versus t graph, is the average velocity"; teacher note: "the steepness of the line (slope) is a measure of the speed"); https://myedspace.co.uk/myresources/gcse/physics/aqa/revision-notes/distance-time-graphs ("the gradient of the line = the speed"; "The steeper the line, the faster the speed")

F16. A straight sloping line on a distance-time graph means a steady (constant) speed.
  https://myedspace.co.uk/myresources/gcse/physics/aqa/revision-notes/distance-time-graphs ("A straight diagonal line means the object is travelling at a constant speed"); https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-3-position-vs-time-graphs (straight line, constant velocity: a parent driving 5 km to school in 10 minutes)

F17. A flat (horizontal) line on a distance-time graph means the object is standing still: time passes but no distance is added.
  https://myedspace.co.uk/myresources/gcse/physics/aqa/revision-notes/distance-time-graphs ("A horizontal line (flat, gradient = 0) means the object is stationary - not moving at all"); https://thirdspacelearning.com/gcse-maths/ratio-and-proportion/distance-time-graph/ ("A horizontal line on a distance-time graph shows that ..." keyed "The object is stationary"; "moving at a constant speed" marked wrong)

F18. A curved line on a distance-time graph means the speed is changing: a line growing steeper means speeding up (OpenStax's jet car), and a line growing less steep means slowing down.
  https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-3-position-vs-time-graphs ("The slope of the curve becomes steeper as time progresses, showing that the velocity is increasing over time"); https://myedspace.co.uk/myresources/gcse/physics/aqa/revision-notes/distance-time-graphs ("A curved line means the speed is changing - the object is accelerating or decelerating")

F19. On a graph of distance from a start line against time, a point where two lines cross is a moment when the two objects are at the same distance from the start, that is, side by side on a straight track. Their speeds there are the slopes of the two lines, which differ where one line crosses the other.
  Follows from F15 and the graph's axes: https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-3-position-vs-time-graphs. Misconception M1 below.

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. The position criterion: students judge which of two objects is faster by where it is, so they take "at the same place" to mean "at the same speed". Trowbridge and McDermott (1980) found that "virtually every failure to make a proper comparison can be attributed to use of a position criterion to determine relative velocity."
  https://www.compadre.org/portal/items/detail.cfm?ID=2964 (Trowbridge and McDermott, "Investigation of student understanding of the concept of velocity in one dimension", American Journal of Physics 48, 1020-1028, 1980)

M2. Slope/height confusion: students read the height of a graph where they should read its slope. McDermott, Rosenquist and van Zee (1987) found students "confused distance, represented by the height of the graph, with velocity, represented by the slope of the graph."
  https://mste.illinois.edu/murphy/Papers/GraphInterpPaper.html

M3. Graph as picture: students expect a graph to be "a picture of the phenomenon described", reading a distance-time graph as if it were a road map, and draw graphs that copy the shape of hills or tracks (McDermott et al. 1987; Mokros and Tinker 1987).
  https://mste.illinois.edu/murphy/Papers/GraphInterpPaper.html

M4. Mixing up distance-time and speed-time graphs: on a speed-time graph a flat line means a steady speed, but on a distance-time graph it means standing still. "It is common for students to confuse distance-time graphs with speed-time graphs."
  https://thirdspacelearning.com/gcse-maths/ratio-and-proportion/distance-time-graph/

M5. "Accelerate" in everyday speech means only "speed up"; physics counts slowing down and turning as acceleration too (F12, F13).
  https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-1-acceleration

M6. Speed and velocity used as if they were the same word; velocity carries a direction and speed does not (F1, F6).
  https://grokipedia.com/page/Speed
