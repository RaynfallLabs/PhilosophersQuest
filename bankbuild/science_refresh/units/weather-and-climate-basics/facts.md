# Weather and Climate: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; NOAA, the National Weather Service (NWS), NASA, Penn State's meteorology course, Florida and Texas grade 5 pages to corroborate and to place the grade.

Neighbours: `the-water-cycle` keys evaporation, condensation into clouds, cloud droplets joining to fall as rain, and the Sun's energy driving the cycle; none of those is keyed here. `winds-air-masses-and-fronts` (T4) owns uneven heating from equator to poles, high-to-low pressure winds, fronts and "falling pressure warns of a storm"; `how-hurricanes-form` (T4) owns hurricanes over warm ocean water. So the barometer is asked here only as the tool for air pressure, and the Sun-and-ocean piece is the sea breeze, a 5th-grade TEKS 5.10A example. Named places' climates are the geography bank's: every place here is "a desert", "a town", "a beach".

## Facts

F1 Weather is the state of the atmosphere (the air) at a particular place and time, described by temperature, humidity, precipitation, wind speed and direction, air pressure and the clouds. https://grokipedia.com/page/Weather ("Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a specific time and place, characterized by variables such as" "temperature, humidity, precipitation, wind speed and direction, atmospheric pressure, and visibility"); https://floridascience.squarespace.com/sc5e73-weather-obervations-and-data (grade 5, SC.5.E.7.3: "Weather is a description of what is happening in the atmosphere at a certain place and time"; described by air temperature, barometric pressure, humidity, wind speed and direction, and precipitation)

F2 Weather is short-term: a rainstorm or a hot day, tracked over hours or days; it can differ even a few blocks apart. https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/about/k-12-education/understanding-our-planet/whats-the-difference-between-weather-and-climate ("Weather is a specific event... that happens over a short period of time"; "tracked within hours or days"; "It could be raining at your house, but dry at school a few blocks away"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Climate ("weather, which describes short-term atmospheric conditions over hours, days, or weeks")

F3 Climate is a place's usual weather: the long-term average of its temperature, precipitation and other weather, usually taken over 30 years or more (the World Meteorological Organization's climate normals). https://grokipedia.com/page/Climate ("Climate is the long-term average of temperature, precipitation, and other weather variables at a given location"; "typically at least 30 years as defined by the World Meteorological Organization"); https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/about/k-12-education/understanding-our-planet/whats-the-difference-between-weather-and-climate ("Climate is the average weather conditions in a place over a long period of time (30 years or more)")

F4 One event, such as a single heatwave or one rainy day, is weather and does not change a place's climate; "climate is what we expect, while weather is what we get" (New Zealand's National Institute of Water and Atmospheric Research, NIWA). https://grokipedia.com/page/Climate ("a single heatwave constitutes weather"); https://niwa.co.nz/climate-and-weather/whats-difference-between-weather-and-climate

F5 A desert is a place with very little precipitation, generally less than 250 millimetres (10 inches) a year; even so, rare storms do bring rain to deserts and can send flash floods down their dry channels. Deserts can be hot or cold (Antarctica's interior is a polar desert), so a desert's climate is dry, not necessarily hot. https://grokipedia.com/page/Desert ("extremely low annual precipitation, generally less than 250 millimeters (10 inches)"; "Rare precipitation events, concentrated in convective storms, generate flash floods in ephemeral channels"; "cold deserts with freezing winters"; "Polar deserts")

F6 Humidity is the water vapour in the air, a measure of how much water vapour the air holds; relative humidity is given as a percentage and is 100% when the air is saturated. It is measured with a hygrometer. https://grokipedia.com/page/Humidity ("Humidity is the presence of water vapor in the Earth's atmosphere"; relative humidity "expressed as a percentage"; "reaches 100% when the air is saturated"; hygrometers); https://forecast.weather.gov/glossary.php?word=humidity (NWS: "Generally, a measure of the water vapor content of the air"); https://floridascience.squarespace.com/sc5e73-weather-obervations-and-data (grade 5: humidity is one of the factors of weather, "measured with a tool called a hygrometer")

F7 Water vapour is an invisible gas: its molecules are spread invisibly among the other gases of the air. What can be seen (clouds, fog, the white puff over a kettle) is water that has condensed into tiny liquid drops or ice. https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_vapor ("individual molecules exist separately and invisibly dispersed among other atmospheric gases, distinct from liquid water or steam visible above boiling surfaces"; "water vapor condenses into tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals")

F8 A thermometer measures temperature; in a liquid-in-glass thermometer the liquid expands as it warms and rises up a thin tube. Weather stations shade their thermometers from direct sun to read the air's temperature. https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermometer ("an instrument designed to measure temperature"; liquid-in-glass thermometers "rely on the thermal expansion of liquids within a capillary tube"); https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/dyw-weather-station.html (NOAA: "Build a Screened Thermometer to Measure Air Temperature"); Florida grade 5 page above ("Air temperature is how warm or cool the air is", measured with a thermometer)

F9 A barometer measures air pressure (atmospheric pressure): the push of the weight of all the air above a place. The air above a place stretches many kilometres up; its lowest layer, where weather happens, reaches about 12 kilometres on average. Evangelista Torricelli made the first mercury barometer in 1643 (1644 in another line of the same page). https://grokipedia.com/page/Barometer ("an instrument designed to measure atmospheric pressure"; the force that the weight of the air above a location exerts; "In 1643, Torricelli devised the first mercury barometer"); https://floridascience.squarespace.com/sc5e73-weather-obervations-and-data (grade 5: "Barometric pressure is the weight of the Earth's atmosphere pushing down"; "measured with a tool called a barometer"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Weather ("The troposphere forms the lowest layer of the atmosphere, extending from the surface to an average height of 12 km")

F10 Rising air pressure often goes with clear, settled weather and falling pressure may come before storms (the T4 unit's key; used here only in a context line). https://grokipedia.com/page/Barometer ("Rising pressure often indicates clear, stable weather, while falling pressure may precede storms or precipitation"); Florida grade 5 page above (high pressure generally sunny, low pressure generally stormy)

F11 An anemometer measures wind speed. The common cup anemometer has three or four cups on arms round an upright spindle; the wind spins them, and the faster the wind, the faster they turn. Leon Battista Alberti made the first known mechanical anemometer in 1450; John Thomas Romney Robinson introduced the cup design in 1846. https://grokipedia.com/page/Anemometer ("an instrument designed to measure the speed of the wind"; "the rotational frequency is linearly proportional to wind speed"; Alberti 1450; Robinson 1846); https://pvpmc.sandia.gov/modeling-guide/1-weather-design-inputs/weather-observations/wind-speed-and-direction/ ("a cup anemometer consisting of three or four cups... mounted symmetrically about a vertical spindle"); https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/dyw-weather-station.html (NOAA: "Build an Anemometer to Measure Wind Speed")

F12 A wind vane (weather vane) shows wind direction. Its arrow points into the wind, toward the direction FROM which the wind blows, and winds are named for where they come from: a north wind blows from the north toward the south. The fixed letters N, E, S, W under the arrow show the compass directions. https://grokipedia.com/page/Weather_vane ("an instrument that determines the direction from which the wind is blowing"; "the narrower end points into the wind"); https://courses.ems.psu.edu/meteo101/node/2239 (Penn State: "a wind vane points into the wind and thus toward the direction FROM which the wind blows"; "wind direction is always the direction from which the wind is blowing"); https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/dyw-weather-station.html (NOAA: "The head of the Weather Vane will point to the direction from which the wind is blowing"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Wind (direction "conventionally reported as the direction from which it originates")

F13 A rain gauge measures rainfall: an open container, in its standard form a funnel feeding a marked tube, collects the rain, and the water is read as a depth (millimetres or inches). https://grokipedia.com/page/Rain_gauge ("designed to measure the amount of liquid precipitation, such as rain or melted snow"; "an open-topped container"; the equivalent depth of rainfall; "The standard rain gauge"); https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/dyw-weather-station.html (NOAA: "Build a Rain Gauge to Measure Rainfall")

F14 Clouds are made of countless tiny drops of liquid water, or tiny ice crystals, or both, floating in the air: water condensed out of the vapour, not the vapour itself. Cloud droplets are about 1 to 100 micrometres across. https://grokipedia.com/page/Cloud ("A cloud is a visible aggregate of minute particles of water droplets or ice crystals suspended in the atmosphere"; "The key ingredient is water, but in its condensed form rather than vapor"; "diameters ranging from approximately 1 to 100 micrometers"); https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/what-are-clouds-made (NSTA probe: "The best response is Glenda's: I think clouds are made of tiny drops of water or tiny ice crystals."); https://www.weather.gov/lmk/cloud_classification (NWS: low clouds "normally consist of liquid water droplets")

F15 Cumulus clouds are low clouds that stand apart, with flat bottoms and puffy, rounded (dome-shaped) tops, like heaps of cotton. Scattered small cumulus on a sunny day are "fair weather" clouds. They form when the Sun warms the ground and warm, moist air rises; in unstable air they can grow into cumulonimbus. The name is Latin for "heap". https://grokipedia.com/page/Cumulus_cloud ("a distinctive puffy or cotton-like appearance, featuring flat bases and rounded tops"; "often called "fair weather" clouds"; "Cumulus clouds form primarily through thermal convection, a process driven by surface heating"; "precursors to cumulonimbus"); https://www.weather.gov/lmk/cloud_classification (NWS: "flat bottoms and rounded tops"; "scattered cumulus clouds showing little vertical growth on an otherwise sunny day"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Cloud (cumulus, "heap")

F16 Stratus clouds are low, flat, even grey (or white) layers that can cover the whole sky like a sheet, on dull, overcast days; they may bring light drizzle, and a stratus cloud resting on the ground is fog. The name comes from Latin for "spread out". https://grokipedia.com/page/Stratus_cloud ("form a continuous, uniform gray or white layer with a relatively even base"; "featureless, sheet-like appearance"; "overcast conditions"; "may produce light precipitation in the form of drizzle"; "at ground level, it manifests as fog"); https://www.weather.gov/lmk/cloud_classification (NWS: "Stratus clouds are uniform and flat, producing a gray layer of cloud cover"; "may cause periods of light precipitation or drizzle")

F17 Cirrus clouds are thin, white, wispy or feathery strands high in the sky, above about 6 kilometres (20,000 feet), made entirely of ice crystals; they bring no rain or snow to the ground. The name is Latin for "curl of hair". https://grokipedia.com/page/Cirrus_cloud ("a wispy, fibrous, or feathery appearance"; "altitudes generally exceeding 6 kilometers (20,000 feet)"; "non-precipitating clouds composed exclusively of ice crystals"); https://www.weather.gov/lmk/cloud_classification (NWS: "Cirrus clouds are wispy, feathery, and composed entirely of ice crystals"; high clouds "above about 20,000 feet"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Cloud (cirrus "from Latin for "curl of hair"")

F18 Cumulonimbus clouds are towering, anvil-topped storm clouds that bring heavy rain, hail, thunder and lightning. https://grokipedia.com/page/Cloud ("towering anvil-topped storm clouds"; "heavy precipitation, hail, or thunderstorms"); https://www.weather.gov/lmk/cloud_classification (NWS: "a thunderstorm producing heavy rain"; collisions of charged particles result "in lightning and thunder")

F19 Luke Howard named the cloud kinds with Latin words in his 1803 essay "On the Modifications of Clouds": cirrus, cumulus, stratus and nimbus (rain cloud). https://grokipedia.com/page/Cloud

F20 Clouds look white because their drops and crystals scatter sunlight; thicker clouds look grey. https://grokipedia.com/page/Cloud ("Clouds generally appear white because their water droplets or ice crystals" scatter light; "Thicker clouds exhibit darker or gray tones")

F21 Wind is air moving across Earth's surface. It comes from differences in air pressure, which come from the Sun heating the planet unevenly. https://grokipedia.com/page/Wind ("Wind is the movement of air relative to Earth's surface"; "differences in atmospheric pressure resulting from the uneven heating of the planet by solar radiation")

F22 A sea breeze: on a sunny day the Sun heats the land faster than the sea beside it; the warm air over the land rises, and cooler air from the sea flows in to take its place. It usually builds in the afternoon, strongest on hot summer days. At night the land cools faster than the sea and the flow reverses as a land breeze. A Texas grade 5 lesson for TEKS 5.10A teaches it: "Sea breezes and land breezes happen because land and water heat up and cool down at different rates." https://grokipedia.com/page/Sea_breeze ("solar radiation heats the land surface more rapidly than the adjacent ocean"; "The warmer air over land, resulting from diurnal heating, expands and rises"; "draws in denser, cooler maritime air"; "typically developing in the afternoon and peaking during hot summer days"; "A land breeze... blows from land toward the sea during nighttime hours"); https://keslerscience.com/sun-ocean-interactions-lesson-plan-teks-5.10a ("Grade level: 5th grade science")

F23 The Sun and the ocean together drive the weather: the Sun's energy evaporates ocean water, and the ocean supplies most of the water vapour that weather is made from (TEKS 5.10A, grade 5: "explain how the Sun and the ocean interact in the water cycle and affect weather"). https://teksguide.org/teks/s510a/alignments ("The ocean's impact on weather comes from the large quantity of water it contributes to the water cycle."; "The ocean affects local and global weather, such as rain (matter) and wind (energy) from tropical storms.")

F24 Tides are raised mainly by the Moon's gravity (with the Sun's, and Earth's spin), not by winds; the water-cycle unit's sheet sources it. https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide (tides are "caused primarily by the gravitational interactions between the Earth, Moon, and Sun, along with the planet's rotation")

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Clouds are made of water vapour. Listed by Ohio State's Beyond Weather and the Cold ("Clouds are made of water vapor." Correct: "Clouds are mainly tiny water droplets or ice crystals."), https://beyondweather.ehe.osu.edu/?p=1996 . Alkis (2007), "An Investigation of Grade 5 Students' Understanding of Humidity Concept", Elementary Education Online 6(3), 333-343: of 300 fifth graders, about 42% called the water vapour in the air "a cloud" and 31% thought the water vapour in the air is visible, https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/90991

M2 Clouds are smoke, cotton wool or bags of water; clouds form by boiling. Bar (1986), ages 5-15, as cited in Driver et al. (1994), and Phillips (1991) for high-school students, both summarised in NSTA's probe: https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/what-are-clouds-made . The "puffs of cold, dry air" lure is the author's (no survey found); it is plainly false (F14).

M3 Humid air is heavier than dry air (67% of the grade 5 students in Alkis 2007), so humidity gets mixed up with air pressure. https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/90991 . Mixing humidity with precipitation or temperature: author's judgment.

M4 A wind vane points the way the wind is going. Penn State: wind direction "often gives students some trouble"; teacher guides ask students whether the vane points to where the wind comes from or where it goes. https://courses.ems.psu.edu/meteo101/node/2239 . Confusing the vane's arrow with a compass needle (it does not show north; the fixed letters do): author's judgment.

M5 Weather and climate confused. NCSE lists the misconception "Climate is simply long-term weather and can't be predicted over long periods of time", https://ncse.ngo/countering-climate-confusion . A study in Research in Science Education (doi 10.1007/s11165-010-9196-z) is reported to find that students cite short-term weather as evidence about long-term climate; seen only in a search summary (the paper is behind a login), so it is not relied on. The lures "the town's climate has turned wet" and "the guide was wrong" (one rainy day read as a verdict on the climate) are the author's judgment built on that confusion; both are plainly false by F3, F4, F5.

M6 The anemometer's cups catch rain (mixing it up with a rain gauge), and it measures direction rather than speed (mixing it up with the wind vane, which often sits beside it, F11). Author's judgment.

M7 The sea warms faster than the land; waves or tides push the sea breeze ashore. Author's judgment; corrected by F22 and F24.

## Notes and cautions

- Do not say a desert is hot as part of its climate: deserts can be cold (F5). Say "dry".
- Do not say cirrus are "the highest clouds": the tops of cumulonimbus can rise higher. Say "high in the sky" (F17).
- Do not call humidity "the amount of water vapour the air CAN hold" (the Florida page's slip): that is the saturation amount. Humidity is the water vapour the air DOES hold (F6, NWS).
- "Steam" is ambiguous (in physics a gas, in everyday speech the visible white puff of droplets): keep it out of the choices.
- Keep "falling pressure warns of a storm" (T4) and "hurricanes form over warm ocean water" (T4) out of the keys.
- No named places, no climate change (a 9th-grade stance unit).

## Revision (2026-11-26): facts added in the one revision round

F25 The World Meteorological Organization's climate normals, the standard against which a place's usual weather is judged, are averages of weather records over a 30-year period (now 1991-2020, updated every decade); a long-term average is used "because of the natural variability in our climate". National Geographic's 5th-grade text: climate "describes the conditions in the atmosphere over a long time, usually 30 years or more". So the standard stretch is 30 years: one year or five is too short, and 100 years is not the standard. https://wmo.int/media/news/its-warmer-average-what-average ("averages of climatological data over a 30-year period"; "we use a 30-year baseline, known as 'Climatalogical Standard Normals'"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Climate ("The 30-year standard, established by organizations like the World Meteorological Organization"; "typically at least 30 years as defined by the World Meteorological Organization for climatological normals"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/weather-or-climate-whats-difference/5th-grade/ (5th-grade text level)

F26 Cirrus heights vary with latitude: the WMO International Cloud Atlas says cirrus "usually occurs at altitudes between 3 km and 8 km" in polar regions, between 5 km and 13 km in temperate regions and between 6 km and 18 km in the tropics. So "usually more than 6 kilometres up" (Grokipedia: "generally exceeding 6 kilometers"; NWS: high clouds "above about 20,000 feet"), not a flat rule. https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/observation-of-clouds-from-aircraft-descriptions-cirrus.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cirrus_cloud ; https://www.weather.gov/lmk/cloud_classification

M8 Evaporation from the sea pushes the sea breeze ashore. Author's judgment (the judge's suggestion: the-water-cycle teaches that sunlight evaporates sea water, so a 5th grader may reach for it). Wrong by F22: the breeze is a heating circulation (warm air over the land rises, cooler sea air flows in); evaporation adds vapour to the air (F23) and drives no wind ashore.
