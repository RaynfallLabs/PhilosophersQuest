## Atoms in a reaction

F1. In a chemical reaction, bonds between atoms break and new ones form: a chemical change "involves the rearrangement of atoms through the breaking and forming of chemical bonds"; "atoms are neither created nor destroyed, only rearranged among the molecules"; "the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products". The reactants "are consumed and transformed into new substances called products."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Conservation_of_mass

F2. NGSS core idea PS1.B (middle school): "In a chemical process, the atoms that make up the original substances are regrouped into different molecules, and these new substances have different properties from those of the reactants. The total number of each type of atom is conserved, and thus the mass does not change."
    https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ps1-5-matter-and-its-interactions

F3. The number of molecules can change in a reaction while the number of each kind of atom does not: hydrogen peroxide breaks down as 2 H2O2 -> 2 H2O + O2, two molecules becoming three (four hydrogen and four oxygen atoms on each side).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_peroxide ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction (coefficients "represent the relative number of molecules")

F4. A chemical reaction changes the bonds between atoms, not the atoms themselves: atoms "interact via their valence electrons to form or sever bonds" (Grokipedia); classically a reaction changes "only ... the positions of electrons", "with no change to the nuclei (no change to the elements present)" (Wikipedia). So an atom of carbon stays carbon through a reaction. (Already taught in inside-the-atom, F8; used here only to make a wrong choice wrong.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction

## Reading an equation

F5. In a chemical equation the reactants are "placed on the left side of an arrow and products on the right" (Grokipedia). OpenStax: "The substances undergoing reaction are called reactants, and their formulas are placed on the left side of the equation"; methane and oxygen react "to yield" carbon dioxide and water.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_equation ; https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations

F6. A coefficient is a whole number "placed before chemical formulas" and sets how many units (molecules) take part; a subscript is part of the formula and denotes "the number of atoms of each element" in one molecule, and "altering subscripts would represent a different substance" (Grokipedia). OpenStax on CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O: "One methane molecule and two oxygen molecules react to yield one carbon dioxide molecule and two water molecules"; coefficients "represent the relative numbers of reactants and products."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_equation ; https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations

F7. To count the atoms of an element on one side, multiply the coefficient of each formula holding it by the element's subscript there (OpenStax: "The number of atoms for a given element is calculated by multiplying the coefficient ... by the element's subscript"). A balanced equation has the same number of each kind of atom on both sides, because atoms "cannot be created or destroyed in ordinary chemical reactions" (Grokipedia), which makes it "consistent with the law of conservation of matter" (OpenStax).
    https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_equation

F8. Methane burns as CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O (complete combustion). Natural gas "typically includes 70 to 90 percent methane by volume"; a Bunsen burner burns natural gas or propane, and its blue flame is complete combustion, giving carbon dioxide and water. Counting: carbon 1 and 1; hydrogen 4 and 2 x 2 = 4; oxygen 2 x 2 = 4 and 2 + 2 x 1 = 4.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Combustion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_gas ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Bunsen_burner ; https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations

F9. Hydrogen peroxide breaks down into water and oxygen gas, 2 H2O2 -> 2 H2O + O2. It "produces effervescence (bubbling) upon contact with wounds", the fast reaction coming from "catalase enzymes present in blood, pus, damaged tissue"; catalase is especially plentiful in the liver and in red blood cells, and the bubbles are the oxygen. (Grokipedia notes that doctors now advise against putting it on wounds; no question recommends it.) Counting: oxygen 2 x 2 = 4 on the left, 2 x 1 + 2 = 4 on the right; hydrogen 4 and 4.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_peroxide ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Catalase

F10. Iron joins oxygen to make iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3, the compound of rust (rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3.nH2O, and forms only with oxygen and moisture) and of the red-brown mineral hematite. The rusting reaction is written 4Fe + 3O2 + 2xH2O -> 2Fe2O3.xH2O; leaving out the water, 4Fe + 3O2 -> 2Fe2O3, with iron 4 and 2 x 2 = 4, oxygen 3 x 2 = 6 and 2 x 3 = 6. So "3O2" is three oxygen molecules, six oxygen atoms.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Iron(III)_oxide ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rust

## Mass that seems to vanish or appear

F11. Limestone and marble are calcium carbonate; it reacts with hydrochloric acid as CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2(g), and the reaction "produces effervescence due to the rapid evolution of CO2 bubbles", a common test for the mineral in rock samples.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Calcium_carbonate

F12. In an open container a reaction that gives off a gas loses mass: "the carbon dioxide gas escapes into the atmosphere, causing the mass of the system to decrease" (Grokipedia, of baking soda and vinegar in an open container). RSC: in open systems "gases produced or consumed can give the false impression that mass has been lost or gained"; "Many chemical reactions appear to involve a change in mass because not all matter has been accounted for"; the same demonstration in a closed flask shows the mass constant. Heated copper carbonate loses mass: "students need to look beyond the test tube ... to 'find' the carbon dioxide gas."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Conservation_of_mass ; https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/conservation-of-mass/4011856.article ; https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/teaching-conservation-of-mass-at-14-16/4020546.article

F13. Magnesium burned in a crucible: "Magnesium burns with a bright white light"; the lid is lifted a little now and then "to allow some oxygen to get in"; the product is white when the reaction is complete; "the increase in mass (mass 3 – mass 2) ... corresponds to the mass of oxygen"; Magnesium + oxygen -> magnesium oxide, 2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO. An RSC sample result: 0.24 g of magnesium gives 0.40 g of magnesium oxide (so 0.16 g of oxygen joined). If the lid is lifted too long "the magnesium oxide product will begin to escape."
    https://edu.rsc.org/experiment/the-change-in-mass-when-magnesium-burns/718.article ; https://edu.rsc.org/ideas/4-ways-to-teach-the-law-of-conservation-of-mass/4019851.article (burning magnesium in air seems to gain mass; "the law holds overall")

F14. Iron wool "increases in mass when it burns in air", and a candle "decreases in mass when it burns in air" (RSC).
    https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/conservation-of-mass/4011856.article

F15. Wood is roughly 50 percent carbon, 40 percent oxygen and 5 percent hydrogen by weight; burned completely, its carbon and hydrogen are "completely converted to carbon dioxide (CO2) and water vapour (H2O)" (Forest Research). Wood's ash comes from its minerals (calcium, potassium) and is 0.5-3 percent of its dry mass (Grokipedia, Wood fuel; dry wood "typically yields 1–3% ash by dry weight", Grokipedia, Wood ash). Incomplete burning also gives carbon monoxide and soot.
    https://www.forestresearch.gov.uk/tools-and-resources/fthr/biomass-energy-resources/reference-biomass/facts-figures/emissions-2 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Wood_fuel ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Wood_ash

F16. When two clear solutions, lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide, are mixed, they react as Pb(NO3)2 + 2KI -> PbI2 + 2KNO3 and give "a bright yellow precipitate of lead(II) iodide". RSC recommends this precipitation reaction for showing that mass is conserved in a reaction with no gas.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lead(II)_iodide ; https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/teaching-conservation-of-mass-at-14-16/4020546.article

## Boyle, Lavoisier and phlogiston

F17. Robert Boyle, in his Essays of Effluviums (London, 1673), sealed tin and lead in glass retorts and heated them for hours. On breaking the seal "I heard the outward Fire rush in" (tin) and "the external Air rushed in with a noise" (lead), and the metal weighed more afterward (the lead "very near, if not full, six grains of increase"). He concluded that "Igneous particles were trajected through the Glass": particles of fire, with weight, had passed through the glass into the metal (Science History Institute: he explained the gain as "fiery particles that were able to pass through the walls of glass vessels").
    https://distantreader.org/stacks/earlyprint/txt/A28965.txt (Boyle 1673, Essays, Exp. V and Appendix Exp. III, Corollary III) ; https://www.sciencehistory.org/historical-profile/robert-boyle ; https://web.sbu.edu/chemistry/wier/matter/lavoisier.html

F18. Antoine Lavoisier, in spring 1774, heated tin and lead in sealed vessels (memoir "on the calcination of tin in closed vessels and on the cause of the gain in weight which this metal acquires", Memoires de l'Academie Royale des Sciences for 1774, published 1777). With 8 onces of tin and 15 1/3 grains of air sealed in, the solids came out 3.12 grains heavier than the tin and the gas 3.12 grains lighter than the air. "The chemical reaction did not change the weight of the sealed container and its contents" (Wikipedia); "total weight of the sealed retort is unchanged", and "when opened, air rushes in and total weight increases" (St. Bonaventure teaching page). He "carefully weighed the reactants and products of a chemical reaction in a sealed glass vessel so that no gases could escape" (Wikipedia). His balances were accurate to about 0.1 grain (Grokipedia).
    https://web.lemoyne.edu/~giunta/classicalcs/lavoisier.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Lavoisier ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass ; https://web.sbu.edu/chemistry/wier/matter/lavoisier.html ; https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/science-and-technology/chemistry-biographies/antoine-laurent-lavoisier ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antoine_Lavoisier

F19. Lavoisier concluded that combustion "consists of rapid combination with oxygen" and that the calx "weighed more than the original metal by an amount corresponding to absorbed air"; in 1783 he called phlogiston "imaginary". In his Traite (1789): "in every operation an equal quantity of matter exists both before and after the operation." The sentence "Nothing is lost, nothing is created, everything is transformed" is a later paraphrase.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antoine_Lavoisier ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Lavoisier ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Combustion

F20. Phlogiston theory (Johann Joachim Becher's "terra pinguis" in the 1660s, renamed phlogiston and popularised by Georg Ernst Stahl around 1700-1730; "influencing chemists across Germany, France, and Britain for over a century") held that a fire-like substance, phlogiston, "was released from combustible materials during processes such as burning", leaving "a non-combustible residue or calx". It explained: a flame going out in a closed vessel ("the air's capacity to receive phlogiston was exhausted"); the light ash left by wood ("the apparent weight loss of burned materials like wood turning to ash" was counted as support); and smelting a calx with charcoal back into metal (charcoal was "rich in phlogiston", which combined with the calx "and regenerate[d] pure metal"). Its trouble: "metallic calces were found to be heavier than the original metals, contradicting the notion" that phlogiston was released. Defenders patched it (Chardenon in 1764 gave phlogiston negative weight; Stahl suggested calces were denser), but Lavoisier's sealed-vessel weighings showed the gain came from the air.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Phlogiston_theory ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Combustion

F21. Jean Rey, a French doctor, wrote in 1630 that tin and lead gain weight on calcination because air attaches to them. (Background only.)
    https://web.lemoyne.edu/Giunta/rey.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. "Some students think that gases have no mass", and "substances in their solid state weigh more than their liquid form", confusing density with mass (Vanessa Kind's research, via RSC). RSC also names "the idea that gases are lighter than liquids and solids."
    https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/conservation-of-mass/4011856.article ; https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/teaching-conservation-of-mass-at-14-16/4020546.article
M2. Matter is "used up" in a reaction, so the mass falls (RSC); in burning, students take the fuel to be destroyed or the mass lost.
    https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/teaching-conservation-of-mass-at-14-16/4020546.article
M3. Özmen and Ayas (2003, 150 students aged 15-16) found two main misconceptions: the total mass rises in a precipitation reaction because the solid precipitate is heavier than a liquid, and mass falls when burning happens in a closed system.
    https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/2003/rp/b3rp90017g/unauth (Chemistry Education Research and Practice 4(3), DOI 10.1039/B3RP90017G; abstract read through search, full text not reached)
M4. Students mix up subscripts and coefficients (Yarroch 1985: even students who balanced equations had "very poor understanding of the concept of chemical subscript").
    https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/michigantech-p/3925
M5. A candle "is just melting", so its mass stays the same (RSC).
    https://edu.rsc.org/ideas/4-ways-to-teach-the-law-of-conservation-of-mass/4019851.article

## Left out, or not sure

- Lavoisier's twelve-day mercury run (the air shrinking by about a sixth, 45 grains of red calx, the leftover air killing a taper): the primary text (Gutenberg) could not be reached, so no question rests on it.
- The photosynthesis equation is the TEKS model, but the T4 life unit photosynthesis-atoms-in-and-out owns it; this unit counts atoms in other equations.
- 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O is T5's example and is not used.
- A balloon over a flask is not used as a "closed" example: the swelling balloon adds buoyancy and the balance reads a little less, which would make the key arguable.
- That Lavoisier read Boyle's account before his own trials is not claimed.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F22. Boyle's own reason for the in-rush of air (tin, Exp. V of the experiments annexed to Essays of Effluviums, 1673): "whereupon I heard the outward Fire rush in, because when the Retort was sealed the Air within it was highly rarified", that is, he had sealed the retort while it was hot. Of lead: "the external Air rushed in with a noise". His conclusion (Corollary III): "Igneous particles were trajected through the Glass". His gain was too large for a small, hot-sealed retort, and a history of technology article judges that "presumably the seal was not tight", so the context says only that his gain came from air, not from fire, and does not say from the air inside his flask.
    https://distantreader.org/stacks/earlyprint/txt/A28965.txt (read again at the revision) ; https://library.hungaricana.hu/hu/view/ORSZ_KOZL_TechnTortSzle_10/?pg=133 (via the fact-check)

F23. In phlogiston theory a metal was its calx plus phlogiston, and phlogiston gave it its metal nature: Stahl held that "metals were compounds containing phlogiston in combination with metallic oxides (calces)" (Wikipedia); in smelting "the calx regained its metallic properties upon absorbing phlogiston from the charcoal", and alloying "involved balancing phlogiston content to achieve desired properties, such as ductility" (Grokipedia). So the theory explained why a metal bends while its calx is a crumbly powder; it was the calx's gain in weight that it could not explain ("some metals gained mass after they burned, even though they were supposed to have lost phlogiston", Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Phlogiston_theory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phlogiston_theory

F24. Soot carries off only a small share of burning wood: fine particles from wood burning come to about 5 to 9.5 g per kg of wood burned (Danish EPA, table after Larson and Koenig 1994), under 1 percent, and smoky open fires about 8 to 73 g per kg in one field study (search summary only); nearly all (99 percent) of the fuel carbon in burned wood becomes carbon dioxide (US EPA AP-42, 1.6, via the search summary). Wood smoke is "besides the major combustion products carbon dioxide and water, a complex mixture" (Danish EPA).
    https://www2.mst.dk/Udgiv/publications/2008/978-87-7052-769-9/html/kap02_eng.htm ; https://gaftp.epa.gov/ap42/ch01/s06/final/c01s06_oct1996.pdf

M6. Özmen and Ayas (2003, 150 Turkish 10th-graders) report, in their own words, that "One of the most common misconception encountered was that the total mass increases in a precipitation reaction because the precipitate produced is solid and it is heavier than a liquid." (Read again through the search summary of the paper's HTML page, 2026-11-26.) This is the lab partner's reasoning in the lead nitrate question.
    https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2003/rp/b3rp90017g
