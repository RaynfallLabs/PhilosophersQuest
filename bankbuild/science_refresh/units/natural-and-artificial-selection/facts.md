## Darwin and the idea

F1. Charles Darwin sailed as the naturalist on HMS Beagle from 1831 to 1836 ("departing Plymouth on December 27, 1831, and returning on October 2, 1836"). His book On the Origin of Species was "first published on 24 November 1859 by John Murray in London."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/On_the_Origin_of_Species ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_selection

F2. Darwin named the process after the breeders' work: "I have called this principle, by which each slight variation, if useful, is preserved, by the term of Natural Selection, in order to mark its relation to man's power of selection" (Origin, chapter 3). Grokipedia: Darwin "termed this process 'natural selection,' analogous to artificial selection by breeders."
    https://talkorigins.org/faqs/origin/chapter3.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_selection

F3. Natural selection stated simply: individuals with inherited traits that help them survive and reproduce in their surroundings leave more offspring, so those traits become more common over generations. Grokipedia: it leads to "changes in the genetic composition of populations over successive generations."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/On_the_Origin_of_Species ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Natural_selection

F4. Populations evolve, individuals do not. MISCONCEPTIONS (UC Berkeley, Understanding Evolution): "Individual organisms can evolve during a single lifespan" (correction: "Populations, not individual organisms, evolve"); "Natural selection involves organisms trying to adapt" and "Natural selection gives organisms what they need" (correction: selection "cannot sense what a species or an individual 'needs'" and acts on variation that already exists). Bacteria "do not evolve resistance to our antibiotics because they 'try' so hard."
    https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/

F5. The respectable wrong rival: Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, in Philosophie Zoologique (1809), held that organs strengthened by use or weakened by disuse in a lifetime pass to offspring (inheritance of acquired traits). Darwin's mechanism instead depends on differential survival and reproduction. August Weismann cut the tails off mice for generations and "no offspring exhibited shortened tails." (Grokipedia notes the stretching-giraffe story is often used to illustrate Lamarck's idea; it is not used here.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lamarckism ; https://grokipedia.com/page/On_the_Origin_of_Species

F6. "Survival of the fittest" is Herbert Spencer's phrase (Principles of Biology, 1864); Darwin took it up in the fifth edition of the Origin (1869): "Natural Selection, or the Survival of the Fittest." In biology, fitness is measured by reproductive success: "survival alone, without reproduction, yields zero fitness contribution." MISCONCEPTION: "a prevalent misconception equates evolutionary fitness with physical strength or aggressive behavior"; Berkeley: the misconception that the fittest are "strongest, healthiest, fastest, and/or largest"; fitness is how many fertile offspring an organism leaves.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Survival_of_the_fittest ; https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/

## Artificial selection

F7. Darwin's pigeons (Origin, chapter 1): "I have kept every breed which I could purchase or obtain" and "I have been permitted to join two of the London Pigeon Clubs." "The fantail has thirty or even forty tail-feathers, instead of twelve or fourteen, the normal number in all members of the great pigeon family." His conclusion: "I can feel no doubt that all our domestic breeds have descended from the Columba livia [the rock pigeon] with its geographical sub-species." Against this, "I have never met a pigeon, or poultry, or duck, or rabbit fancier, who was not fully convinced that each main breed was descended from a distinct species." Grokipedia: his pigeon work informed "his analogy between human-directed change and natural selection."
    https://talkorigins.org/faqs/origin/chapter1.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Domestic_pigeon

F8. Artificial selection: people choose which plants and animals breed, for the traits they want. Darwin: "The key is man's power of accumulative selection: nature gives successive variations; man adds them up in certain directions useful to him." The Origin opens with variation under domestication (breeds and crops) before natural selection.
    https://talkorigins.org/faqs/origin/chapter1.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/On_the_Origin_of_Species

F9. Wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea), in the mustard family Brassicaceae, grows wild on limestone sea cliffs of the Atlantic coasts of western Europe (France, Great Britain, Spain). Breeding it for different parts gave: kale and collards (leaves), cabbage ("enlarged terminal buds forming heads"), broccoli ("edible flower heads") and cauliflower (flower clusters, "condensed inflorescences"), Brussels sprouts ("axillary buds", the side buds along the stem) and kohlrabi ("swollen stem"). Wikipedia: these forms came from "artificial selection for various phenotypic traits." Many school texts call the ancestor "wild mustard"; one of Grokipedia's references titles it "Wild Cabbage, Wild Mustard."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Brassica_oleracea ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brassica_oleracea

F10. Dogs from wolves. Dog breeds range "from Chihuahuas under 3 kg to Great Danes over 70 kg"; "All modern dogs trace ancestry to an extinct wolf population closely related to the gray wolf"; domestication began roughly 15,000 to 40,000 years ago (Grokipedia; Wikipedia gives 15,000-30,000). Robert Wayne's team (Vila et al., Science, 1997) sequenced mitochondrial DNA of 162 wolves and 140 dogs of 67 breeds; the sequences supported wolves as the dogs' ancestors. In Wayne's comparisons every coyote and jackal sequence differed from every dog sequence "by at least 20 sites", far more than wolves' did: "Dogs are domesticated wolves." Earlier views (respectable wrong theories): Darwin thought "wolves, coyotes and jackals ... may all have played a role"; Konrad Lorenz, in the 1950s, proposed that "some dog breeds derive from jackals, others from wolves."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dog ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Domestication_of_the_dog ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_of_the_domestic_dog ; https://www.stlpr.org/health-science-environment/2017-11-19/on-science-evolution-of-the-family-dog ; https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de4ca363b8185d9ca819ca (Vila 1997 abstract: 162 wolves, 140 dogs, 67 breeds)

F11. Corn (maize) from teosinte. Maize "originated from the domestication of teosinte (Zea mays ssp. parviglumis)" about 9,000 years ago in the Balsas River Valley of southwestern Mexico. Teosinte has small ears of about five to twelve kernels (Wikipedia: five to 10 seed segments; UW-Madison: "just 10 to 12 kernels"), each in "a very hard fruitcase" ("a stony casing"). A single change in one gene, tga1, "stripped away the hard, inedible casing", leaving the kernel exposed on the cob; people selected it by "growing more of the plants they preferred."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Maize ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teosinte ; https://news.wisc.edu/uw-study-shows-how-a-kernel-got-naked-and-corn-became-king/

## Applying the idea to new cases

F12. Antibiotic resistance evolves by natural selection: "exposure to naturally occurring antimicrobials then selects for advantageous traits", letting "resistant cells outcompete sensitive ones"; the variation comes from mutations or genes passed between bacteria. "Resistance to penicillin followed rapidly upon its therapeutic deployment in 1941." Berkeley: some bacteria are born with traits that help them survive the drug, and those survivors reproduce more.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antimicrobial_resistance ; https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/

F13. MISCONCEPTION: in a World Health Organization survey of about 10,000 people in 12 countries (November 2015), "Three quarters (76%) of respondents think that antibiotic resistance happens when the body becomes resistant to antibiotics. In fact bacteria, not humans or animals, become resistant to antibiotics."
    https://www.who.int/news/item/16-11-2015-who-multi-country-survey-reveals-widespread-public-misunderstanding-about-antibiotic-resistance ; https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/news-perspective/2015/11/who-survey-shows-antibiotic-resistance-myths-common

F14. Pesticide resistance: "repeated exposure ... selects for rare individuals possessing pre-existing genetic variations"; "the most resistant specimens survive and pass on" their traits. Paul Muller found DDT's insect-killing power in 1939, and it was "widely applied from the 1940s onward"; "by 1947 housefly resistance to DDT had evolved" (first reports from Sweden and Italy, 1946-1947). Resistance is documented "in over 500 insect species"; the Colorado potato beetle resists more than 50 insecticides.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pesticide_resistance ; https://grokipedia.com/page/DDT ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_resistance

F15. A housefly's life cycle, egg to adult, takes "as few as 7-10 days under optimal warm conditions", "enabling 10-12 or more generations annually in favorable climates." Selection works generation by generation, so a kind that breeds fast can change fast.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Housefly ; https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/

F16. Tuskless elephants of Gorongosa National Park, Mozambique. Mozambique's civil war lasted "from 1977 to 1992"; armies hunted elephants "for food and ivory", and large herbivores fell "more than 90 percent" (Science News); the park had 2,542 elephants in 1972 and 242 by 2000 (Berkeley). Tuskless females: 18.5% before the war, 50.9% after (Campbell-Staton et al., Science, 2021); "over half the female elephants in Gorongosa were tuskless in the year 2000." "Tusked elephants were targets for ivory poachers, while tuskless elephants were not"; tusklessness is inherited (a gene version on the X chromosome, lethal in males), and tuskless females were "more than five times as likely to survive to reproduce."
    https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evo-news/the-evolution-of-tuskless-elephants-foils-poachers-but-at-a-cost ; https://www.sciencenews.org/article/tuskless-elephants-evolution-poaching

## The peppered moth re-tested

F17. The peppered moth's pale form (typica) and dark form (carbonaria). The dark form was rare before industry; the first was caught near Manchester in 1848, and by 1895 it was about 98% of the moths there. Coal smoke brought "heavy soot deposition that blackened tree trunks and branches" and killed pale lichens "through sulfur dioxide exposure." The colour is inherited at "a single genetic locus." The cause: "differential predation by visually foraging birds."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Peppered_moth_evolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppered_moth_evolution

F18. Bernard Kettlewell, of Oxford University, marked moths "on the underside of the wings with cellulose paint", released them and counted recaptures. In a polluted wood near Birmingham (1953) he recaptured 27.5% of the dark moths and about 13% of the pale ones. In Deanend Wood, Dorset (1955), clean and "well covered with lichens", he recaptured 13.7% of the pale moths but only 4.7% of the dark (Grokipedia gives 12.5% and 6.3%: in both counts, about twice or more of the pale). Niko Tinbergen filmed birds eating the moths. "The more conspicuous form was always scarcer after recapture."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kettlewell%27s_experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Peppered_moth_evolution ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12052-008-0107-y

F19. The criticisms: "moths were often placed on tree trunks, which were argued to be abnormal resting sites", and "released at greatly inflated densities"; Theodore Sargent could not reproduce the results. Judith Hooper's book Of Moths and Men (2002) suggested Kettlewell's experiment was fraudulent; scientists who examined the charge "found them to be without merit." Critics also raised bat predation.
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3391436/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kettlewell%27s_experiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppered_moth_evolution ; https://darwin200.christs.cam.ac.uk/melanism-moths

F20. Michael Majerus re-tested it in his garden near Cambridge for six years (2001-2007): he released 4,864 moths, "the largest ever attempted for any similar study." Each night one moth went into each of 12 netting sleeves around branches, on resting sites normal for the species, at under 10 moths per hectare a night; the sleeves came off before dawn, and he watched with binoculars. Nine bird species (robins, blue tits, great tits, blackbirds, wrens and others) took the dark moths at significantly higher rates. Over those years the dark form near Cambridge fell from about 12% (2001) to about 1% (2007). Majerus died in 2009; L. M. Cook, B. S. Grant, I. J. Saccheri and J. Mallet published the work in Biology Letters in 2012: "the most direct evidence yet to implicate camouflage and bird predation."
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3391436/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppered_moth_evolution ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Peppered_moth_evolution

F21. After clean-air laws (Britain's Clean Air Act of 1956), the dark form steadily fell from around 1962, to under 5% at surveyed UK sites by the 2000s; the pale form returned to predominance. (The timing of the lichens' return is disputed, so it is not taught here.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Peppered_moth_evolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppered_moth_evolution ; https://www.talkorigins.org/indexcc/CB/CB601_2_4.html

## Misconceptions used as wrong choices
- M1. Individuals change in a lifetime to suit their surroundings, or because they need to (F4, F5).
- M2. The fittest are the strongest, fastest or longest-lived (F6).
- M3. A person's body becomes resistant to an antibiotic (F13); a germ or insect "learns" or "toughens up" (F4).
- M4. The environment or a poison directs the genes to change in a useful way (F4, F14).
- M5. Each breed of a domestic animal came from its own wild species (F7, F10).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F23. Where cabbage's crops began: domestication "began approximately 2,500 to 3,000 years ago in the Mediterranean region"; wild B. oleracea diverged in the Eastern Mediterranean, the "Aegean endemic Brassica cretica" is its closest living relative (Mabry et al., Molecular Biology and Evolution, 2021, as the fact-check read it), and the low diversity of European coastal populations suggests they are "primarily feral derivatives from early cultivated forms rather than true progenitors". So the western European cliff plants are not taught as the ancestor's home.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Brassica_oleracea

F24. "Wild mustard" is a loose school name for wild cabbage: the name is more often given to another plant of the family, Sinapis arvensis, "commonly known as charlock mustard, field mustard, wild mustard, or charlock" (Wikipedia); the RHS lists "wild mustard" among its names.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlock ; https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/119101/wd/details

F25. The blue rose: roses lack the gene for a blue pigment, so breeding never gave a true blue rose. Grokipedia: the rose family "lacks the critical flavonoid 3'5'-hydroxylase (F3'5'H) gene", needed to make the blue pigment delphinidin; hybridizers tried from the 19th century into the early 20th without a true blue. Wikipedia: "roses lack the specific gene that has the ability to produce a 'true blue' color", and bred results such as 'Blue Moon' "are more accurately described as lilac". Florigene and Suntory made a bluish rose by genetic engineering, inserting "a gene for the blue plant pigment delphinidin cloned from the pansy" (Wikipedia gives 2002, Grokipedia 2004 for 'Applause'; no year is taught); it is "lavender or pale mauve in color". Selection can only choose among differences that already exist (F4, F8); a gene does not blend away in a cross (the T3 Mendel unit).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Blue_rose ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_rose

F26. Gorongosa caution (why the elephants question was dropped): the Science paper's 18.5% (n = 52) and 50.9% (n = 108) are tuskless shares of females sighted before and after the war, and the share among daughters born after the war is reported as about a third (PBS NOVA, National Geographic) or about half (NPR), so no question rests on it.
    https://www.npr.org/2021/10/22/1048336907/elephants-tuskless-ivory-poaching-africa ; https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/elephant-poaching-tuskless-evolution-mozambique-africa
