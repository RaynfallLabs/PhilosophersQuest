# The Tides (T2, 6th grade, earth strand)

Sources were read on 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; NOAA (the National Ocean
Service tides tutorial and JetStream) and NASA corroborate anything a child
could be taught wrong. Lane: the process only; no named coast, bay or harbour.

## What raises the tides

F1. Tides are the regular rise and fall of the sea, caused mainly by the gravity of the Moon and the Sun acting on Earth's oceans, together with Earth's spin; "the moon is the dominant force affecting the Earth's tides" (NOAA).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html

F2. The Moon's gravity pulls the ocean on the side of Earth nearest the Moon toward it, "causing a 'bulge' of water on the near side toward the moon" (NOAA).
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides03_gravity.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tides/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide

F3. At the same time a second bulge forms on the side of Earth facing away from the Moon. The Moon's pull is strongest on the near side and weakest on the far side, so the far-side water "is effectively left behind as the planet is accelerated toward the Moon" (Grokipedia). NASA: the oceans bulge "on both the side closest to the Moon and the side farthest from the Moon". (NOAA's classroom version says inertia beats the weaker pull on the far side.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide ; https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tides/ ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides03_gravity.html

F4. Low tides happen at the low places between the two bulges (NASA: "The low points are where low tides occur").
    https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tides/

F5. As Earth spins, a coast passes through both bulges, so "most shorelines experience two high and low tides per day" (NASA); a fixed point gets two highs and two lows per lunar day (Grokipedia). A few coasts get only one high and one low tide a day (a diurnal tide).
    https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tides/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides07_cycles.html

## The timing

F6. High tides come 12 hours and 25 minutes apart; coasts get two high and two low tides every 24 hours and 50 minutes, the lunar day (NOAA). Grokipedia gives the main lunar tide a period of 12.42 hours.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides05_lunarday.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide

F7. On a coast with two tides a day, high and low tide come "about six hours apart" (NOAA JetStream).
    https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/ocean/tides

F8. The Moon travels around Earth in the same direction that Earth spins, so "it takes the Earth an extra 50 minutes to 'catch up' to the moon" (NOAA). So successive high tides come "roughly 50 minutes later each day" (Grokipedia); NASA gives the extra time as about 50 minutes for a point on Earth to turn back under the Moon.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides05_lunarday.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide ; https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tides/

## The Sun's smaller part, and why

F9. The Sun raises tides too, but smaller ones: "the sun's tide-generating force is about half that of the moon" (NOAA); solar tides are "about half as large as lunar tides" (NOAA); NASA says "a little less than half"; Grokipedia says the Sun contributes about half the Moon's tidal effect.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides06_variations.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tides/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide

F10. The Sun has about 27 million times the Moon's mass (NOAA, whose page says "27 million times larger" of mass; Grokipedia gives the Sun's mass as 1.989 x 10^30 kg), and its pull on Earth as a whole is about 177 to 178 times the Moon's (NOAA), yet "the Moon's tidal pull on the Earth is over twice that of the Sun's" (NOAA JetStream).
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html ; https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/ocean/tides ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun

F11. The reason is distance. The Sun is about 390 times farther from Earth than the Moon (NOAA; NASA). The tide-raising force falls off with the cube of distance, while the ordinary pull falls off with the square, so for tides "the distance between two objects usually is more critical than their masses" (NOAA). Grokipedia: tidal effect is "proportional to the celestial body's mass divided by the cube of its distance". A tide comes from the difference in pull between Earth's near and far sides (F3).
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tides/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide

F12. Gravity between two bodies depends on their masses and the distance between them, not on what they are made of: the force is "directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance" (Grokipedia). The Sun is made mostly of hydrogen and helium, a hot ionised gas (plasma).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun

F13. Earth and the other planets travel around the Sun, which does not travel around Earth (Grokipedia: the Sun is the centre "around which the Earth and other planets revolve in orbits").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Heliocentrism

## Spring and neap tides

F14. Spring tides come around new moon and full moon, when the Sun, the Moon and Earth line up (syzygy). Then "the solar tide has an additive effect on the lunar tide, creating extra-high high tides, and very low, low tides" (NOAA): the biggest tidal range of the cycle (Grokipedia: "the highest high tides and the lowest low tides of the lunar cycle"). NASA: the two pulls combine.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides06_variations.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spring_tide ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/springtide.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tides/

F15. Spring tides come "twice each lunar month all year long" (NOAA). The name "has nothing to do with the season of spring" (NOAA); it comes from the tide "springing forth" (NOAA), "from the water springing or rising up" (NOAA JetStream). Grokipedia notes "common misconceptions linking it to the season of spring".
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/springtide.html ; https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/ocean/tides ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spring_tide

F16. Neap tides come at the first and third quarter moons, when the Moon looks half full and the Sun and the Moon are at a right angle to each other as seen from Earth. Then "the solar tide partially cancels out the lunar tide" (NOAA), giving the smallest difference between high and low tide (NOAA JetStream; Grokipedia). Neap tides also come twice a month, about a week from the spring tides.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/springtide.html ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides06_variations.html ; https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/ocean/tides ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spring_tide ; https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tides/

F17. The tidal range is "the difference in height between the high tide and the low tide" (NOAA); spring tides have the greatest range and neap tides the smallest.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides01_intro.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide

F18. Other tide words: "The change from high to low tide is called the 'ebb tide'", and from low to high the "flood tide" (NOAA JetStream). Slack tide or slack water is the short period when "there is no movement either way in the tidal stream" (Wikipedia), as the tide turns.
    https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/ocean/tides ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slack_water

F19. The Moon's distance also matters a little: near perigee (the Moon closest, once a month) tidal ranges are above average, and near apogee below average (NOAA). Not asked; it keeps "the Moon is closest" out of the wrong choices for spring tides.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides06_variations.html

F20. With no Moon, the Sun's gravity would still raise its own tide (the solar tide, about half the size of the lunar tide, F9), so Earth's oceans would still rise and fall, by less. (Follows directly from F9 and NOAA's account of the solar and lunar tides as separate tides that add or partly cancel.)
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides06_variations.html ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html

## Wind and weather

F21. Wind and air pressure can push water toward or away from a coast and raise or lower the water beyond the predicted tide (NOAA: "Strong offshore winds can move water away from coastlines"); storm surges are driven by wind and air pressure (Grokipedia). These are weather, not the cause of the regular tides.
    https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides08_othereffects.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Wind causes the tides: a common 6th-grade starting point (Kesler Science: students think "Tides are caused by wind blowing the water around").
    https://keslerscience.com/predicting-tides-activity-teks-6.9b

M2. Only one high tide a day, on the side of Earth facing the Moon (Kesler Science: "There's only one high tide per day, on the side of Earth facing the Moon").
    https://keslerscience.com/predicting-tides-activity-teks-6.9b

M3. Spring tides belong to the season of spring (Grokipedia: "common misconceptions linking it to the season of spring"; NOAA: the name "has nothing to do with the season of spring").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spring_tide ; https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/springtide.html

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26; read on the web that day)

F23. The Sun's pull, like the Moon's, works on all of Earth, by night as well as by day: NASA says of the Moon that its gravity "affects the entire Earth, pulling at every point on our planet", and the Sun's tide runs on "a cycle time of 12 hours" (Wikipedia), two highs each solar day, one of them on the night side. The pull between two bodies depends only on their masses and the distance between them (F12), so it is not shared out among the planets. The Sun is about 390 times farther than the Moon (NASA, F11), written "nearly 400 times" in the context to agree with moon-phases-and-eclipses ("about 400").
    https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tides/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide (the Sun "similarly causes tides ... (46% of that of the Moon) with a cycle time of 12 hours") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation
