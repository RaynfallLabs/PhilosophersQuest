## The habitable zone

F1. The habitable zone (also called the Goldilocks zone, after the fairy tale's porridge that was "just right") is the range of distances from a star at which a rocky planet with suitable air could keep liquid water on its surface. Too close, and the water is lost to a runaway greenhouse; too far, and it freezes. NASA's phrase: "not too hot, not too cold."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Habitable_zone ; https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanets/habitable-zone

F2. Earth (about 1 AU from the Sun) sits inside the Sun's habitable zone. Venus (about 0.72 AU) lies inside the zone's inner edge. Mars (about 1.52 AU) lies near the outer edge: outside the older conservative estimate (Kasting 1993, 0.95-1.37 AU) but inside the newer one (Kopparapu 2013, about 0.99-1.70 AU). Being in the zone does not guarantee liquid water or life: composition, magnetic field and internal heat matter too. CAVEAT: do not say flatly that Mars is "outside the habitable zone"; the estimates differ.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Habitable_zone

F3. The habitable zone's distance depends on the star's brightness: cooler, dimmer stars have zones closer in (and narrower); hotter stars have wider zones farther out. Under the classical definition a red dwarf's zone lies 10 to 20 times closer in than Earth is to the Sun. TRAPPIST-1, an ultracool red dwarf about 40 light-years away with about 1/2000 of the Sun's brightness, has seven rocky planets; three (e, f and g) lie in its habitable zone at about 0.029, 0.038 and 0.047 AU, far closer than Mercury is to the Sun (about 0.39 AU). MISCONCEPTION: that a dim star can have no habitable zone at all.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Habitable_zone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/TRAPPIST-1 ; https://astrobiology.nasa.gov/news/red-dwarf-stars-and-the-planets-around-them-2/ ; https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanets/habitable-zone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mercury_(planet)

F4. Life on Earth depends on water in its liquid state, which is why "follow the water" became NASA's guiding theme in searching for life on Mars and elsewhere.
    https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090305014028/http://astrobiology.nasa.gov/tags/water ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Habitable_zone

## Gravity holds the air

F5. The Moon circles Earth, so it travels round the Sun at Earth's distance, inside the habitable zone, yet it has almost no air: its thin exosphere is about 10^15 times less dense than Earth's air at sea level, "virtually a vacuum". The main reason is weak gravity: gas molecules always include a few moving fast enough to escape, and the Moon's escape speed is only 2.38 km/s against Earth's 11.2 km/s (its surface gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's), so any atmosphere would be lost over geological time. Lacking a magnetic field, it is also exposed to the solar wind. Water on its surface would evaporate and escape; only some ice may survive in permanently shaded craters near the poles. MISCONCEPTIONS: that air is made by plants (Earth had nitrogen-rich air long before plants), or that air is what causes gravity.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_the_Moon ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Book%3A_Physics_-_From_Stargazers_to_Starships_(CK-12)/09%3A_The_Moon_-_a_Closer_Look/9.03%3A_the_Airless_Moon ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Great_Oxidation_Event

## The greenhouse effect keeps Earth mild

F6. Earth's average surface temperature is about 15 degrees Celsius (59 F). Without the greenhouse effect it would be about -18 degrees Celsius (0 F), some 33 degrees colder. This physics is settled.
    https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/global-warming ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Greenhouse_effect ; https://www.energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Earth_Temperature_without_GHGs

F7. How it works: most incoming visible sunlight passes through the air and warms the ground and sea. The warm surface gives off infrared ("heat") radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb part of that infrared (NASA: "much of it is absorbed by water vapor and long-lived greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane") and re-emit it in all directions, some back down toward the surface, which slows the loss of energy to space (energy still leaves; the effect reduces the net loss, it does not stop it). MISCONCEPTIONS: that greenhouse gases reflect heat like a mirror, trap it for good, or work by blocking incoming sunlight.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Greenhouse_effect ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/global-warming

F8. The main greenhouse gases are water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane (with nitrous oxide and ozone). Nitrogen and oxygen, nearly all of the air (about 78 and 21 percent), are not greenhouse gases: they are transparent to infrared. MISCONCEPTION: that the air's main gases do the warming.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Greenhouse_effect ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Earth ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/global-warming

F9. John Tyndall, an Irish-born physicist at the Royal Institution in London, measured in 1859 how much radiant heat passed through a long tube filled with one gas at a time (heat from a copper cube of boiling water, measured with a thermopile). Dry air, oxygen, nitrogen and hydrogen let it pass almost freely; water vapour, carbon dioxide and ozone absorbed much more. Eunice Newton Foote reported in 1856 that a cylinder of carbon dioxide in sunlight warmed more than one of ordinary air (priority is debated). Joseph Fourier had proposed in 1824 that the atmosphere keeps Earth warmer.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/John_Tyndall ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Greenhouse_effect

## Oxygen to breathe

F10. Earth's early air held almost no free oxygen (below about one hundred-thousandth of today's level); it was mostly nitrogen with carbon dioxide and methane. Oxygen built up in the Great Oxidation Event, beginning about 2.4 billion years ago, made by cyanobacteria, microbes whose photosynthesis splits water and releases oxygen. At first the oxygen reacted with iron dissolved in seawater (laid down as banded iron formations) and only later gathered in the air. Today oxygen is about 21 percent of dry air. Photosynthesis is the primary source of free oxygen. Volcanic gas is mostly water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide (together over 99 percent of the molecules), with no free oxygen among its listed parts. MISCONCEPTION: that volcanoes or the rocks themselves supplied the air's oxygen.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Great_Oxidation_Event ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Earth ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Volcanic_gas

## The ozone layer

F11. Ozone (O3) is a form of oxygen with three atoms. About 90 percent of it lies in the stratosphere, roughly 15 to 35 km up (densest at about 20 to 30 km); there it forms when ultraviolet light splits oxygen molecules (O2) and the loose atoms join other oxygen molecules. The ozone layer absorbs about 97 to 99 percent of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet light (mainly UV-B and UV-C). A stable ozone layer could form only after oxygen built up in the air.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ozone_layer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Great_Oxidation_Event

F12. Ozone depletion lets more UV-B reach the ground. UV-B causes sunburn, skin cancers and cataracts, and harms plants and plankton. The Antarctic ozone hole was reported in 1985 by British Antarctic Survey scientists (Farman, Gardiner and Shanklin); it was caused mainly by chlorine from CFCs, and the 1987 Montreal Protocol phased them out.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ozone_layer ; https://19january2021snapshot.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/health-and-environmental-effects-ozone-layer-depletion_.html ; https://19january2025snapshot.epa.gov/sunsafety/health-effects-uv-radiation

F13. MISCONCEPTIONS: that the ozone layer keeps Earth warm or that a thin ozone layer makes the air at the ground hotter (NASA: the extra UV from ozone loss "cannot explain the warming of the planet's surface"; ozone loss chiefly cools the stratosphere), and that the ozone layer supplies the oxygen people breathe (breathed oxygen is O2 in the lower air; ozone is harmful to breathe).
    https://science.nasa.gov/climate-change/faq/is-the-ozone-hole-causing-climate-change/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ozone_layer

## The magnetic shield

F14. Earth's magnetic field comes from a geodynamo: molten, electrically conducting iron alloy churning in the liquid outer core, together with Earth's spin.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnetosphere

F15. The solar wind is a stream of charged particles (plasma) flowing out from the Sun's corona. Because the particles are electrically charged they feel magnetic forces, and Earth's magnetosphere deflects most of them around the planet, shielding the air from being stripped away; some are guided down toward the poles, where they make auroras. Light is made of photons, which carry no electric charge; the ozone layer, not the magnetic field, is what absorbs ultraviolet light. MISCONCEPTION: that the magnetic field blocks the Sun's ultraviolet light or heat.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnetosphere ; https://science.nasa.gov/resource/the-solar-wind-across-our-solar-system ; https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/spaceweather/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Photon

## Mars: thin air, lost water, no global magnetic field

F16. Mars's air is about 95 percent carbon dioxide (about 2.7 percent nitrogen, 1.6-1.9 percent argon, 0.13-0.17 percent oxygen) at a surface pressure of about 6.1 millibars, about 0.6 percent of Earth's. Its average surface temperature is about -60 C (-63 C in another section).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Mars

F17. Liquid water is not stable on the surface of Mars today: below a pressure of about 0.006 bar water's boiling point is as low as or lower than its freezing point, so water there is ice or vapour (salty water can sometimes stay liquid briefly). Long ago water flowed: Mars has dry runoff channels, huge outflow channels carved by floods, and basins that were once underwater; the Curiosity rover found mudstones from an ancient lakebed in Gale crater, and hematite spheres that form only in water were found elsewhere. MISCONCEPTION: that Mars has never had water.
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/10-5-water-and-life-on-mars ; https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-rover-team-confirms-ancient-lakes-on-mars/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Mars

F18. Mars has no global magnetic field today, only patches of magnetism left in its crust; its global dynamo stopped about 4.1 billion years ago, leaving its air exposed to the solar wind. NASA's MAVEN spacecraft (orbiting Mars from 2014) measured the solar wind stripping gas from the top of Mars's air at about 100 grams a second, faster during solar storms; because the young Sun gave off more intense ultraviolet light and solar wind, the loss was "likely much greater in Mars' history". In 2017 the MAVEN team reported that about 65 percent of Mars's argon, and the majority of its carbon dioxide, had been lost to space, and that solar wind and radiation stripping transformed Mars "from a planet that could have supported life billions of years ago into a frigid desert world".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Mars ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnetosphere ; https://lasp.colorado.edu/maven/2017/03/30/maven-reveals-most-of-mars-atmosphere-was-lost-to-space/ ; https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-mission-reveals-speed-of-solar-wind-stripping-martian-atmosphere

F19. Although Mars's air is mostly carbon dioxide, there is so little of it that its greenhouse effect is "essentially negligible" (Mars's average temperature is close to the temperature it would have with no greenhouse effect at all, about 210 K). Venus's atmosphere, by contrast, is more than 10,000 times as massive as Mars's, and its greenhouse effect raises its surface temperature by hundreds of degrees (Grokipedia: over 500 K). The difference is the amount of gas, not a different gas.
    https://www.energyeducation.ca/encyclopedia/Greenhouse_effect_on_other_planets ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Mars ; https://openstax.org/books/astronomy/pages/10-3-the-massive-atmosphere-of-venus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Venus

## Venus: a runaway greenhouse

F20. Venus's air is about 96.5 percent carbon dioxide and 3.5 percent nitrogen, with a surface pressure of about 92-93 bars (over 90 times Earth's) and a surface temperature of about 460-467 C. Its clouds of sulfuric acid reflect about three-quarters of the sunlight that reaches it, so less sunlight reaches its ground than reaches Earth's, even though Venus, at 0.72 AU, receives about 1.9 times Earth's sunlight at the top of its air. It spins very slowly (one turn in about 243 Earth days), yet its surface is nearly the same temperature by day and by night (usually within 1 K), because the thick air spreads the heat.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Venus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Venus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Venus

F21. The runaway greenhouse: Venus may once have had a climate like Earth's, with liquid water. As it warmed (a slowly brightening Sun), its seas put more water vapour, a greenhouse gas, into the air, which warmed it further and evaporated more water, a self-feeding loop; the oceans boiled away. Ultraviolet light split the water vapour and the light hydrogen escaped to space, so the loss could not be undone. Evidence: Venus's ratio of deuterium (heavy hydrogen) to hydrogen is about 100-150 times Earth's. CAVEAT: whether Venus ever had true oceans is still debated; say "may have had".
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy/pages/10-3-the-massive-atmosphere-of-venus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Venus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Venus

F22. Where Earth's carbon dioxide is: on Earth most of the carbon dioxide is chemically bound in the rocks of the crust (carbonate rocks such as limestone) or dissolved in the oceans; Earth has some 35 to 50 whole atmospheres' worth of carbon dioxide in carbonates, and its carbonate rocks hold about as much carbon dioxide as Venus's atmosphere (Rasool and de Bergh 1970; Kasting 1988). Earth's liquid water drives the carbonate-silicate cycle: carbon dioxide dissolves in rain as carbonic acid, weathers rock, rivers carry the products to the sea, and they settle as carbonate rock. Venus has no liquid water, so the cycle cannot run and volcanic carbon dioxide stays in its air.
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy/pages/10-3-the-massive-atmosphere-of-venus ; https://astronomynotes.com/solarsys/s9.htm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Carbonate%E2%80%93silicate_cycle ; https://ebsco.com/research-starters/earth-and-atmospheric-sciences/comparative-planetology-venus-earth-and-mars ; https://burro.case.edu/Academics/Astr221/SolarSys/CompPlanet/venusmars.html

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F23. Water on the Moon's sunlit surface does not last: water desorbs at sunlit daytime temperatures, and "solar ultraviolet radiation photolyzes H2O" (Grokipedia). But permanently shadowed craters near the poles are "cold traps where water ice is retained"; NASA: some water ends up in permanently shadowed polar craters, where ice is relatively stable. So "most water left on its sunlit surface" escapes, not all of the Moon's water.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lunar_water ; https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/ice/ice_moon.html

F24. The ozone layer "absorbs about 97-99% of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation": over 99 percent of UV-C and about 95 percent of UV-B, with "negligible absorption in the UV-A range (315-400 nm)" (Grokipedia); EPA: "UVA is not absorbed by ozone". So it absorbs most of the Sun's HARMFUL ultraviolet light, not most of all its ultraviolet light.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ozone_layer ; https://19january2025snapshot.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/basic-ozone-layer-science

F25. The order of the first oxygen: oxygenic photosynthesis "first emerged in cyanobacteria around 2.7 to 3.0 billion years ago"; "Dissolved Fe2+ in anoxic surface waters served as the primary sink" for that oxygen until the supply was largely used up, and oxygen then built up in the air in the Great Oxidation Event, "generally dated to have initiated around 2.43 billion years ago". Land plants came far later: "the fossil record of embryophytes begins in the Ordovician period", with cryptospores about 470 million years old. So the air's FIRST oxygen came from microbes in the sea, some two billion years before plants grew on land. (Land plants did help raise oxygen later, which is why the revised question asks for the first oxygen, not most of today's.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Great_Oxidation_Event ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Embryophyte

F26. A meteoroid is "a small rocky or metallic body in outer space"; as it enters the atmosphere "friction with air molecules causes it to heat up and vaporize", making a meteor. A magnetic field pushes only on electrically charged particles (F15), so it turns aside neither light nor space rock; with no field, bits of rock from space would reach the air just as before.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Meteoroid ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnetosphere

F27. Why liquid water does not last on Mars's surface today. OpenStax: "At a pressure of less than 0.006 bar, the boiling point is as low or lower than the freezing point", so "even if the temperature on a sunny summer day rises above the freezing point", the low pressure keeps liquid water from lasting on the surface (only the lowest ground comes close). Grokipedia: "Mars' current atmospheric pressure of about 6 mbar prevents sustained liquid water on the surface"; salt lowers water's freezing point, so brines can last "minutes to hours". Surface temperatures: NASA, "as high as 70 degrees Fahrenheit (20 degrees Celsius)"; Grokipedia Mars, about 21 C at the equator in summer. Surface pressure is "less than 1% of Earth's"; a Martian solar day is "about 24.660 hours", "slightly longer than an Earth day" (JPL: 24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds); Mars has "gravity only 38 percent as strong as Earth's" (JPL). Boiling point: "as pressure decreases, the boiling point lowers", about 1 degree C for every 300 m of height (Grokipedia). MISCONCEPTIONS used as wrong choices: weak gravity letting the water float off, long days letting the Sun dry it, salt making it freeze.
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/10-5-water-and-life-on-mars ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_on_Mars ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mars ; https://science.nasa.gov/mars/facts/ ; https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/press_kits/insight/launch/facts/mars-at-a-glance/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Boiling_point

Martian wind is weak in force: NASA, "The winds in the strongest Martian storms top out at about 60 miles per hour", "less than half the speed of some hurricane-force winds on Earth", and because "Mars' atmospheric pressure is a lot less", "things get blown, but it's not with the same intensity".
