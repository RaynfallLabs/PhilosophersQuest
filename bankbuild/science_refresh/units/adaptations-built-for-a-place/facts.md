# Adaptations: Built for a Place: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; Wikipedia, National Geographic Education, UC Berkeley's Understanding Evolution, Scientific American and the American Bird Conservancy to corroborate. Curriculum line G5.L4 (TEKS 5.13A; NGSS 4-LS1-1).

## Facts

F1 An adaptation is an inherited (heritable) feature that helps a living thing survive and reproduce in its environment. https://grokipedia.com/page/Adaptation ("a heritable trait that has been molded by natural selection to enhance the fitness of organisms in their environment"); https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/best-adapted-beaks-bring-science-home/ ("Adaptations are traits that developed to perform a certain function and allow organisms to thrive in their environment")

F2 Adaptations are passed down from parents to young, generation after generation, and become common in a group of living things over many generations, because those that have them survive and have young more often. https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/adaptation/ ("An adaptation is passed from generation to generation"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Adaptation ("becoming more prevalent in populations over generations through differential reproductive success")

F3 One living thing cannot grow an adaptation in its own lifetime by trying, wanting or needing it; adjustments made during one life (acclimation) are not inherited. https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/ (misconception "Natural selection involves organisms trying to adapt"; correction: it "does not involve effort, trying, or wanting"; "Populations, not individual organisms, evolve"); https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/adaptation/ ("Individual organisms cannot adapt"; an individual's response "is referred to as acclimation"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Adaptation (acclimation and plasticity are "non-heritable adjustments within an individual's lifetime")

F4 Body parts and plant parts are adaptations when they help a living thing survive where it lives: examples taught include thorns, stems, roots, skin, a cactus's spines and thick stem, a turtle's hard shell, a heron's spear-like beak, webbed feet, and thick fur for arctic cold. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/4-ls1-1-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes (4-LS1-1: "internal and external structures" "that function to support survival, growth, behavior, and reproduction"; examples "thorns, stems, roots, colored petals, heart, stomach, lung, brain, and skin"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.13a-organism-structures-functions (TEKS 5.13A, grade 5: "Analyze the structures and functions of different species to identify how organisms survive in the same environment"; turtle "hard shell for protection from predators"; cactus "spines" and "a thick stem (to store water)"; "thick fur to keep warm in arctic cold")

F5 An instinct is a behaviour an animal is born able to do, with no teaching or practice; it is a behaviour, not a body part. https://grokipedia.com/page/Instinct ("an innate, species-typical pattern of behavior that is triggered by specific environmental stimuli", occurring without earlier training)

F6 A habitat is the place where a living thing lives and finds what it needs; it is a place, not a feature of the body. https://grokipedia.com/page/Habitat ("the specific set of resources and environmental conditions in a given area that enable occupancy")

F7 A life cycle is the series of stages a living thing goes through in its life, not a body part. https://grokipedia.com/page/Biological_life_cycle ("the complete sequence of developmental stages that an organism undergoes")

F8 Blubber is a thick layer of fat under the skin of sea mammals such as whales, seals and walruses. Its main job is to keep body heat in, against icy ocean water; it also stores energy and helps them float. https://grokipedia.com/page/Blubber ("a dense, vascularized layer of adipose tissue located beneath the skin of marine mammals"; "primarily as thermal insulation against frigid ocean waters"; also "energy storage" and "buoyancy")

F9 Fur keeps an animal warm by trapping a layer of still air against the skin; animals of cold places have denser coats, and long oily guard hairs on top shed water. https://grokipedia.com/page/Fur ("functions as a thermal insulator by trapping a layer of still air adjacent to the skin"; "dense underfur"; "many temperate mammals grow thicker winter coats"; guard hairs "repel water")

F10 Camouflage is colouring and patterning that matches an animal's background, so predators (or prey) fail to see it. A sandy-coloured lizard blends into sand and escapes birds that hunt by sight. Camouflage is inherited and built up by natural selection. https://grokipedia.com/page/Camouflage (coloration and patterns "that render them inconspicuous against their backgrounds"; "background matching"; "evolves primarily through natural selection imposed by predation pressure"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Crypsis (the tawny dragon lizard's patterns "disrupt its silhouette against sandy substrates", helping it evade "avian and reptilian predators"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.13a-organism-structures-functions (horned lizard's "flat shape and earth-colored skin")

F11 Warning colours do the opposite of camouflage: bright reds, yellows and blues tell predators that an animal is poisonous or stings (poison frogs, wasps). https://grokipedia.com/page/Aposematism (prey "employ conspicuous traits to signal their unprofitability to potential predators"; poison dart frogs' "vibrant reds, yellows, and blues"; wasps' stripes "signaling their stinging capability")

F12 Hibernation is a seasonal winter rest of some mammals and birds through cold and food shortage; migration is a seasonal journey from place to place. Neither one is colouring. https://grokipedia.com/page/Hibernation ("a seasonal physiological adaptation observed primarily in certain mammals and birds"; torpor through cold and food shortage); https://grokipedia.com/page/Animal_migration ("the directed and predictable movement of animals across space and time, typically seasonal")

F13 A bird's beak shape fits its food: seed-eaters such as finches have short, stout, cone-shaped beaks for crushing seeds; hawks and other raptors have sharp, hooked beaks to tear meat; hummingbirds have long, thin beaks to reach nectar deep in flowers; shorebirds have long, slender bills to probe mud for small animals. https://grokipedia.com/page/Beak ("Granivorous species, like finches, possess stout, conical beaks optimized for husking and crushing seeds"; "raptors employ hooked, robust beaks to grasp and tear flesh"; "Probing foragers like shorebirds employ long, slender bills to extract invertebrates from mud"); https://abcbirds.org/news/diverse-world-bird-beaks/ ("Hummingbirds' long, delicate beaks allow them to reach deep into flowers to drink their sweet nectar"; raptors "use their sharp, hooked beaks to tear into the flesh of their prey"); https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/best-adapted-beaks-bring-science-home/ ("A hummingbird's beak is long and thin, which helps it dip into the flowers to sip nectar"; "A hawk's beak is razor sharp and can tear the flesh")

F14 Teeth fit food too: plant-eaters (herbivores) have broad, flat back teeth (molars) for grinding tough plants; meat-eaters (carnivores) have sharp, pointed teeth, such as canines, for gripping and tearing flesh, and fish-eating hunters have fang-like pointed teeth; insect-eaters have sharp teeth that pierce insects' hard shells. https://grokipedia.com/page/Herbivore ("broad, flat molars for grinding tough vegetation"; horses' "flat, ridged molars"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Carnivore ("sharp, pointed teeth for tearing flesh," including canines and carnassials; pike have "fang-like, conical oral teeth adapted for piercing and impaling victims"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Insectivore ("sharp teeth adapted for piercing exoskeletons"); https://wdfw.wa.gov/get-involved/environmental-education-curriculum/lesson-plans/what-specialized-teeth-you-have (grades 3-5: students tell carnivores, herbivores and omnivores apart from skulls)

F15 A cactus stores its water in its thick, fleshy stem, which works as a water tank; ribs or pleats let the stem swell with water after rain and shrink again in dry times. In some cacti the stem tissue is 80-95% water. https://grokipedia.com/page/Cactus (cacti "conserve water through anatomical adaptations in their stems, which function as reservoirs"; ribs and bumps "allow for reversible expansion and contraction"; "ribbed or accordion-like stems that contract during desiccation"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Xerophyte (in Opuntia and Cereus, stem tissues "hold 80-95% water by fresh weight"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.13a-organism-structures-functions (cactus "a thick stem (to store water)")

F16 A cactus's spines are leaves of a special kind (modified leaves). They guard the plant against animals that would eat it, and they shade the stem, which cuts water loss. The green stem, not leaves, makes the cactus's food, and a waxy coat on the stem keeps water in. https://grokipedia.com/page/Cactus (spines "constitute modified leaves or multicellular hairs"; "evolved primarily as a mechanical defense against herbivores"; they "minimize water loss by providing shade to the stem surface"; stems are "the primary photosynthetic organs"; "A waxy cuticle envelops the stem surface"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Xerophyte (in many cacti, leaves are "entirely reduced or modified into spines")

F17 Most cacti have shallow roots that spread out wide just under the surface of the ground, so they take in rain quickly when it falls. https://grokipedia.com/page/Cactus ("shallow, fibrous root systems that spread laterally near the soil surface"; a taproot in certain species gives "anchorage and access to deeper water sources"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Xerophyte (Joshua tree roots "extend horizontally and vertically to capture infrequent rainfall")

F18 Many desert plants have very deep roots that reach water far underground (groundwater), sometimes tens of feet down; the deepest roots ever recorded belong to a desert tree whose root reached 68 metres. Deep roots also help anchor a plant. https://grokipedia.com/page/Xerophyte (deep root systems "sometimes penetrat[e] tens of feet into the soil"; "extensive or deep root systems to access groundwater"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Root ("The deep penetration allows plants to tap into groundwater during periods of surface drought"; Boscia albitrunca, a Kalahari Desert tree, "found reaching 68 meters in depth"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phreatophyte (deep-rooted plants such as mesquite have "very deep roots that are able to reach the water table"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.13a-organism-structures-functions (tree "deep roots (to reach water and stay anchored)")

F19 Many trees in places with cold winters (deciduous trees) drop their leaves in autumn and grow new ones in spring. Leaves lose water into the air, and in winter liquid water is hard to get from cold or frozen ground, so dropping the leaves saves water; the bare tree rests (is dormant) through the winter, saving energy. https://grokipedia.com/page/Deciduous (shedding prevents "water loss through transpiration when environmental stress would otherwise damage the plant"; energy and nutrients conserved "during dormancy"; "rapid regrowth of new foliage in spring"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciduous (plants "lose their leaves to conserve water" and "can reduce water loss due to the reduction in availability of liquid water during cold winter days")

F20 Plants make their own food in their green parts (mostly the leaves) from sunlight, water and air; they do not take food from the soil. So a tree with no leaves makes no food. https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ("This process enables these organisms to produce their own food"; "Plants, algae, and cyanobacteria carry out oxygenic photosynthesis from carbon dioxide and water"; starch "in green leaves"); https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290 (roots take up water and minerals, not food); https://grokipedia.com/page/Cactus (where leaves are gone, the stem does the job: stems "structurally evolved to supplant leaves"); see also this bank's unit how-plants-make-food

F21 Ducks, geese, frogs and otters have webbed feet: skin joins the toes into a paddle that pushes against water. The webbing forms while the animal is still an embryo (for a bird, inside the egg), when the cells between the toes that would normally die stay alive. https://grokipedia.com/page/Webbed_foot ("a paddle-like structure that enhances propulsion and reduces drag"; "Palmate feet, common in ducks and geese"; digits separate by "a programmed cell death process that normally separates digits during embryogenesis", and webbing persists when it is suppressed); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webbed_foot ("In ducks, BMPs are not expressed at all"; in chickens, a changed BMP receptor stopped the death of the tissue between the toes and gave webbed feet)

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1 Living things grow adaptations because they want, try or need to. Berkeley lists "Natural selection involves organisms trying to adapt" and "Natural selection gives organisms what they need" as misconceptions: https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/. Used as the lure "It grows them because it wants to swim".

M2 One animal changes its body by using it, or because of its surroundings, and that is how it gets an adaptation. Berkeley: "Individual organisms can evolve during a single lifespan" is a misconception; National Geographic: "Individual organisms cannot adapt." Used as the lures "Swimming stretches its toes into webs" and "Cold pond water makes its toes grow webs" (F3, F21: the webs form in the egg).

M3 Plants get their food from the soil through their roots. Ohio State's Beyond Penguins lists "Soil provides a support structure and food for plants" as a misconception: https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290 (the source this bank's how-plants-make-food unit uses for its M1). Used as the lure "Food stored deep in the soil" (F20).

## Notes for the author

- Lane: kinds of living things only (water birds, sea mammals of icy seas, desert plants, seed-eating birds, plant-eaters). No single species' famous trick is asked; the Kalahari tree in F18 is background only and is not named in any question.
- Neighbours: habitats and the living and nonliving parts of a place belong to ecosystems-living-and-nonliving; instincts and learned behaviour belong to born-knowing-or-learned. "Instinct" and "Habitat" appear here only as wrong choices beside the term "adaptation".
- Blubber also helps buoyancy and energy storage (F8), so no question asks blubber's job with "floating" as a wrong choice; the blubber question asks where the coverings suit an animal.
- Deep roots also anchor a plant (F18), and fallen leaves insulate the ground, so neither anchoring nor warm leaf litter is used as a wrong choice.

## Revision facts (2026-11-26, the one revision round)

F22 A learned skill or behaviour comes from experience, such as practice or being taught; it is a behaviour, not a body part. https://grokipedia.com/page/Learning ("Learning is a relatively permanent change in an organism's behavior or knowledge that results from prior experience")

F23 Blubber is the fat of marine mammals (whales, seals, walruses), thickest in polar and subpolar species adapted to cold waters. Animals of snowy mountains keep warm mainly with fur: the mountain goat of alpine and subalpine regions has a dense woolly undercoat and coarse guard hairs that trap air, "supplemented by subcutaneous fat reserves", but it is a land animal and has no blubber. https://grokipedia.com/page/Blubber ("located beneath the skin of marine mammals, such as whales, seals, and walruses"; "Thickness is greatest in polar and subpolar species adapted to cold waters"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Mountain_goat ("a dense undercoat of fine wool and coarser guard hairs that trap air, providing superior insulation"; "supplemented by subcutaneous fat reserves for additional thermal buffering")

F24 Mimicry is one living thing looking like a different kind of living thing (its model), as harmless hoverflies look like stinging wasps; it is not camouflage (crypsis), which is blending into the background. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mimicry ("one organism, known as the mimic, evolves to resemble another organism, the model"; crypsis is where an organism "blends into its background to avoid detection"; harmless hoverflies resemble stinging wasps)

F25 Moulting is shedding and replacing an animal's outer covering: reptiles shed skin, birds feathers, mammals fur. https://grokipedia.com/page/Moulting ("the physiological process by which animals periodically discard and replace their outer integumentary layers"; "Reptiles exhibit periodic ecdysis, the shedding of the outer epidermal layer"; "Birds undergo periodic feather moulting"; mammals' "periodic shedding and regrowth of pelage (hair or fur)")

F26 Omnivores, which eat both plants and animals, have sharp front teeth (incisors and canines) as well as broad back teeth: bears eat berries and fish, and have prominent hooked canines and broad, flat molars. https://grokipedia.com/page/Omnivore ("sharp incisors and canines for cutting and tearing meat, alongside broad molars"; bears "consume berries, fish, and mammals"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Bear ("prominent hooked canines up to several centimeters for seizing prey"; "broad, flat molars for pulverizing vegetation and insects")

F27 Hunters of small animals have sharp, pointed teeth: shrews have "sharp, spike-like teeth" and eat insects such as beetles, and earthworms; red foxes hunt mice, voles and rabbits, and meat-eating mammals have sharp, pointed canines. https://grokipedia.com/page/Shrew ("sharp, spike-like teeth specialized for piercing tough insect exoskeletons"; "insects such as beetles and larvae, earthworms, spiders"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Red_fox ("ambush larger prey like rabbits or dig into burrows with rapid forepaw strikes to extract voles and mice"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Carnivore ("sharp, pointed teeth for tearing flesh—such as canines and carnassials in mammals"). CAUTION (fact-check): not every animal-eater has sharp teeth (anteaters and pangolins have none; the aardvark and armadillos have blunt pegs), so no context states a rule for all animal-eaters and no choice names ants or termites. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myrmecophagy

F28 Dew is small drops of water that condense on cool surfaces, usually at night or early in the morning; it forms on the surface of the ground, not deep in it. https://grokipedia.com/page/Dew ("small water droplets that condense on cool surfaces")

F29 Next year's leaves are made before autumn: trees form their leaf buds at the end of summer, and the buds, holding preformed leaves under protective scales, wait through the winter; the old leaves do not fall to make room for them. https://www.forestryengland.uk/blog/why-trees-wake-spring ("These buds are formed at the end of summer"; twigs in autumn and winter "already contain tiny leaf buds"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Bud (buds "typically form during the active growing season", "containing embryonic tissues such as preformed leaves"; "In temperate climates, buds often overwinter protected by scales")

F30 Bare deciduous trees still split in hard frost: frost cracks, vertical splits in the bark and wood on the sunny side, appear in winter, mostly in deciduous trees with thin bark such as maples, oaks and beech. https://www.chicagobotanic.org/plant-information/pests/frost-cracks ("Deciduous trees, especially young trees with thin bark"; "Vertical cracks appear in the winter in the bark of the sunny side of trees"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frost_crack (crab-apple, beech, walnut, oaks, maples, sycamore and others "prone to developing frost crack")

F31 Leaves pull water up: the water a leaf loses into the air makes the pull that draws water up from the roots, so a tree with no leaves draws up less; and when hardwoods drop their leaves in autumn, water stops moving through them for the winter. https://grokipedia.com/page/Transpiration ("Transpiration lowers the water potential in leaf tissues (Ψ_leaf), creating tension that pulls water from the roots"); https://extension.psu.edu/silent-survivors-the-winter-life-of-trees ("as the days grow shorter, colder, and drier in the fall, hardwoods respond by dropping their leaves"; losing the leaves halts water movement through the tree for the winter)
