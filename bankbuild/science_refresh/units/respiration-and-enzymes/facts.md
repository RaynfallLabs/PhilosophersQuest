## Cellular respiration: the reaction and where it happens

F1. Cellular respiration in sum: C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy, about 30-32 ATP (adenosine triphosphate) per glucose (Grokipedia). Older textbooks give 36-38; current estimates are about 29-30 (Wikipedia). The reaction gives out about 2,880 kJ per mole of glucose (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cellular_respiration ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration

F2. Where: glycolysis, the first stage, runs in the cytosol (cytoplasm); the citric acid (Krebs) cycle runs in the mitochondrial matrix; oxidative phosphorylation, the electron transport chain, sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane (the cristae). Chemiosmosis there makes about 90 percent of the ATP of aerobic glucose breakdown (OpenStax).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cellular_respiration ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/7-4-oxidative-phosphorylation

F3. Oxygen's job: "oxygen is the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain"; it takes up electrons and then protons and forms water (OpenStax). The chain "is the only part of glucose metabolism that uses atmospheric oxygen" (OpenStax). Wikipedia: the electrons "are finally transferred to exogenous oxygen", which combines with protons to form water. So the oxygen breathed in ends up, in the reactions of respiration, in water, not in the carbon dioxide breathed out. MISCONCEPTION: that the oxygen breathed in turns into the carbon dioxide breathed out.
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/7-4-oxidative-phosphorylation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration

F4. The carbon dioxide comes from the fuel's carbon: each turn of the citric acid cycle oxidises two carbon atoms to CO2, four CO2 per glucose from the cycle (Grokipedia, Wikipedia); the cycle runs in the mitochondrial matrix. Cyanide stops the chain by blocking cytochrome c oxidase (OpenStax).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Citric_acid_cycle ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citric_acid_cycle ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/7-4-oxidative-phosphorylation

F5. Respiration and photosynthesis: respiration "complements photosynthesis by recycling the CO2 and water produced" (Grokipedia); its products are photosynthesis's raw materials and the reverse, so the scope's "roughly photosynthesis run in reverse" holds at the level of the overall equation (the pathways themselves differ). Wikipedia: respiration is "technically a combustion reaction", an unusual one "because of the slow, controlled release of energy".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cellular_respiration ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration

F5a. Slow or fast, the same heat: respiration and the burning of glucose are the same overall reaction (C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O, about 2,880 kJ per mole, Wikipedia), and the energy a reaction gives out is set by what goes in and what comes out, not by how fast or by what path: OpenStax, "enzymes do not change the reaction's ∆G". So the fair comparison of an animal with burning charcoal is heat per amount of carbon dioxide made, which is how Lavoisier and Laplace made it (F13), not heat per hour. MISCONCEPTION: that slow burning gives out less heat in all.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/6-5-enzymes ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Lavoisier

## Without oxygen: fermentation

F6. Fermentation: "an anaerobic metabolic process that regenerates NAD+ from NADH produced during glycolysis" (Grokipedia), so glycolysis can keep making ATP (OpenStax). Net yield "two ATP per glucose molecule" (Grokipedia; Wikipedia "2 molecules of ATP per 1 molecule of glucose"). Aerobic metabolism "is up to 15 times more efficient" (Wikipedia). Lactic acid fermentation "predominates in skeletal muscle during intense anaerobic exercise" (Grokipedia); OpenStax: in "skeletal muscle that has an insufficient oxygen supply to allow aerobic respiration to continue"; lactate is carried to the liver and turned back to pyruvate. Alcoholic fermentation in yeast turns pyruvate into ethanol and CO2 "under oxygen-limited conditions" (Grokipedia; OpenStax: a carboxyl group is removed, "releasing carbon dioxide as a gas").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cellular_respiration ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/7-5-metabolism-without-oxygen ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration

F7. A word of care: strictly, "anaerobic respiration" uses an inorganic final electron acceptor such as sulfate or nitrate, and fermentation, which passes electrons to an organic molecule, "is not respiration" (Wikipedia; OpenStax). Many school books call fermentation anaerobic respiration. The questions therefore say "without oxygen" or "fermentation" and never key the term "anaerobic respiration".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/7-5-metabolism-without-oxygen

F8. The Pasteur effect: Louis Pasteur showed that "aeration of yeasted broth causes cell growth to increase while the fermentation rate decreases" (Wikipedia, dated 1857; Grokipedia says first observed in 1861 and describes a preliminary note of 3 August 1857, so the questions say "in the late 1850s" or give no year). Without oxygen yeast consumes sugar faster; in Saccharomyces cerevisiae anaerobic glucose use "can be 5 to 10 times faster than aerobic" (Grokipedia). The reason: fermentation yields 2 ATP per glucose, aerobic respiration many more (Grokipedia: about 30-36 in animal cells, up to 18 in yeast).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasteur_effect ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pasteur_effect

## Lactic acid and next-day soreness

F9. Lactic acid "was once believed to cause muscle stiffness, fatigue, and soreness", but "more recent research disputes this hypothesis" (OpenStax 7.5).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/7-5-metabolism-without-oxygen

F10. Delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) begins 12-24 hours after the exercise and peaks between 24 and 72 hours (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: "felt most strongly 24 to 72 hours after the exercise"). It "arises primarily from microscopic damage to muscle fibers", mainly in eccentric (lengthening) contractions, and the inflammation that follows (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: eccentric exercise, microtrauma). Lactic acid was long blamed until the 1980s, when studies showed lactate "return[s] to baseline within an hour after exercise" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: the lactic acid theory "has been largely rejected", lactic acid returns to normal within about an hour (Cheung et al. 2003), and concentric exercise also makes lactic acid without causing DOMS (Armstrong 1984).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Delayed_onset_muscle_soreness ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delayed_onset_muscle_soreness

F11. Schwane and colleagues (Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise 15:51-56, 1983): seven subjects ran 45 minutes on the level (at 78 percent of their maximum oxygen uptake) and 45 minutes down a 10 percent slope (at 57 percent). After the easier downhill run they had significant delayed soreness in several leg muscles; after the harder level run, no significant soreness. The authors tie the soreness to structural changes in muscle from eccentric contractions. (The abstract reports no lactate figures, so none is used.)
    https://scilit.com/publications/c2e6c819b5cb637f854f39fff251e1dd

## How we know: Lavoisier and Laplace's guinea pig

F12. Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace built an ice calorimeter, around the winter of 1782-1783 (Wikipedia; Grokipedia "around 1782-1783"; the Memoir on Heat was printed in the Academy's volume for 1780, and Wikipedia's Calorimeter page dates a guinea-pig use to 1780, so the questions say "in the winter of 1782 to 1783"). It "quantified thermal energy by the mass of melted ice" (Grokipedia). The outer shell was packed with ice or snow to keep outside heat out; the inner ice was melted by the object; the meltwater was collected, and "Its weight exactly measures the heat emitted by the object" (Memoir on Heat; Wikipedia: "The outer shell of the calorimeter was packed with snow").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antoine_Lavoisier ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Lavoisier ; https://erenow.org/common/history-philosophy-science-reader/68.php ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorimeter

F13. The guinea pig: one run lasted 5 hours 36 minutes and melted about 7 ounces; another lasted 10 hours 36 minutes and melted 14 ounces 5 gros; the animal "melted 13 ounces in ten hours" (Memoir). They estimated 224 grains of fixed air (carbon dioxide) breathed out in ten hours, and from burning carbon, "the formation of 224 grains of fixed air must melt 10.38 ounces"; they judged the agreement as good as could be hoped. Conclusion: respiration is a combustion, very slow, but like that of carbon (Memoir; Wikipedia: they compared the animal's heat "with the heat from burning enough carbon to produce the same amount of carbon dioxide", and Lavoisier wrote "la respiration est donc une combustion"). Grokipedia: heat per unit of carbon dioxide was "comparable between guinea pig respiration and the oxidation of vegetable matter or flames"; respiration as "slow oxidation akin to burning".
    https://erenow.org/common/history-philosophy-science-reader/68.php ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Lavoisier ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Antoine_Lavoisier

F14. The older ideas of animal heat: "According to Aristotle and Galen, the essential function of the respiration phenomenon was to cool the blood" (general-anaesthesia.com history page). "Galen imagined that heat is innate in the heart", while "the chemic-physicians attributed it to fermentations" and "the mechanic-physicians to frictions" (Popular Science Monthly, February 1873, "Heat and Life"). (Where Lavoisier placed the burning, the lungs or the blood, is told differently by these two sources, so it is left out.)
    https://www.general-anaesthesia.com/respiration.html ; https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_Science_Monthly/Volume_2/February_1873/Heat_and_Life

## Enzymes: what they are and what they do

F15. Enzymes are "primarily proteins but also including RNA molecules known as ribozymes" (Grokipedia). They lower a reaction's activation energy, work "without being consumed or permanently altered", and "do not alter the position of equilibrium" (Grokipedia); speed-ups reach about 10^17-fold for orotidine 5'-monophosphate decarboxylase (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: "Enzymes increase the reaction rate by lowering a reaction's activation energy, often by factors of millions." OpenStax: "enzymes do not change the reaction's ∆G. They only reduce the activation energy"; they "remain ultimately unchanged by the reactions they catalyze". MISCONCEPTIONS: that an enzyme supplies the energy to start a reaction, is used up, or makes a reaction give out more energy.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Enzyme ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/6-5-enzymes

F16. Activation energy: "Even exergonic reactions require a small amount of energy input before they can proceed"; burning fuel "will take place at a negligible rate unless sufficient heat from a spark overcomes their activation energy"; OpenStax asks whether sugar could be stored at all if its breakdown needed no activation energy. For most reactions in a cell, heat high enough to drive them would make "the cell's essential components ... disintegrate", so cells lower the activation energy by catalysis instead (OpenStax 6.2).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/6-2-potential-kinetic-free-and-activation-energy

## How we know: enzymes work outside the cell (Buchner, 1897)

F17. Before Buchner: Pasteur held fermentation to be "an exclusively vital phenomenon", tied to intact living yeast (Grokipedia, Eduard Buchner); he wrote that "alcoholic fermentation is an act correlated with the life and organization of the yeast cells" (Wikipedia, Enzyme). Wilhelm Kühne coined "enzyme" in 1877, from Greek for "in yeast" or "in leaven" (Grokipedia; Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Eduard_Buchner ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Enzyme

F18. Buchner's 1897 experiment (his paper, in translation): yeast was "rubbed with an equal weight of quartz sand" and kieselguhr and pressed "under a pressure of 4 to 500 atmospheres", giving about 300 cm3 of juice; filtration "through a sterilized Berkefeldt kieselguhr filter, which safely holds back all yeast cells" barely reduced its fermenting power; mixed with cane sugar it gave "a regular evolution of carbonic acid which lasts for days"; glucose, fructose and maltose fermented, while lactose and mannitol did not; with sugar it kept its power "at least two weeks in the icebox"; boiling coagulated it. He concluded that "such a complicated apparatus as represented by the yeast cell is not required" and that the agent is "a dissolved substance, doubtless a protein", which he named zymase. Grokipedia: "robust fermentation in the press juice without any living yeast cells". Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1907 "for his biochemical researches and his discovery of cell-free fermentation" (Grokipedia).
    https://web.lemoyne.edu/~giunta/buchner.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Eduard_Buchner ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduard_Buchner

F19. Later: zymase is "a soluble enzyme complex" (Grokipedia), and Buchner's guess that yeast secretes it was wrong: "it was later found that fermentation occurs inside the yeast cells" (Wikipedia). The work "dealt yet another blow to vitalism" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Eduard_Buchner ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduard_Buchner

## How we know: enzymes are proteins (Sumner, 1926; Northrop, 1930)

F20. James Sumner lost his left arm below the elbow at 17 when a companion shot him by accident while hunting; he had been left-handed (Wikipedia; Encyclopedia.com). He began enzyme work at Cornell in 1917 and in 1926 crystallised urease from jack beans by mixing purified urease with acetone and chilling it; chemical tests showed the crystals were protein, "the first experimental proof that an enzyme is a protein" (Wikipedia). Urease breaks urea into ammonia and carbon dioxide (Nobel Prize facts page). Grokipedia: the 1926 crystallisation of urease "from jack bean, confirming enzymes as proteins". Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1946, shared with John Northrop and Wendell Stanley.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_B._Sumner ; https://www.encyclopedia.com/science/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/james-batcheller-sumner ; https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1946/sumner/facts/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Enzyme

F21. The objection: Richard Willstätter (Nobel Prize 1915, for chlorophyll) held that enzymes were low-molecular-weight substances adsorbed on carrier colloids such as proteins, and "contended that Sumner had merely crystallized the carrier of the enzyme rather than the enzyme itself"; because of Willstätter's eminence, Sumner's claim was not generally accepted (Encyclopedia.com, via search excerpts and page). Wikipedia: Willstätter argued "proteins were merely carriers for the true enzymes".
    https://www.encyclopedia.com/science/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/james-batcheller-sumner ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme

F22. John Northrop's crystalline pepsin (Journal of General Physiology 13:739, 1930): "The composition, optical activity, and proteolytic activity of this protein remain constant through seven successive crystallizations"; "No evidence for the presence of a mixture or of a solid solution is found"; "the conclusion seems justified that pepsin itself is a protein." In 1931 he restored denatured pepsin and recovered a protein of the same crystal form and specific activity. When Northrop crystallised pepsin and other enzymes, it became clear Sumner had a general method; "the opponents gradually admitted" it, "Willstätter last of all" (Nobel and Encyclopedia.com excerpts). Pepsin is the stomach's protein-digesting enzyme, most active at pH 1.5-2.5 (Grokipedia, Pepsin).
    https://rupress.org/jgp/article/13/6/739/11252/CRYSTALLINE-PEPSIN-I-ISOLATION-AND-TESTS-OF-PURITY ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2141071 ; https://www.encyclopedia.com/science/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/james-batcheller-sumner ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pepsin

## How we know: each enzyme fits its own molecule (Fischer, 1894; Koshland, 1958)

F23. Emil Fischer, "Einfluss der Configuration auf die Wirkung der Enzyme" (Berichte 27, 1894, 2985-2993): the lock-and-key idea was "first formulated in studies of yeast enzymes that hydrolyze glucosides" (Grokipedia, Emil Fischer), and he wrote that "enzyme and glucoside have to fit to each other like a lock and key" (Grokipedia; Science History Institute: "must fit together like lock and key"). Hammarsten's Text-Book of Physiological Chemistry (1914): "of two isomeric glucosides, one methyl-d-glucoside, the (α) was attacked by yeast and the other (β) only by emulsin", and the enzyme "must fit the substrate somewhat like a key fitting a lock".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Emil_Fischer ; https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/emil-fischer/ ; https://runeberg.org/physchem/0076.html ; https://historyofinformation.com/detail.php?entryid=4404

F24. The α and β forms (anomers) differ only "in the configuration at the anomeric carbon": in α-D-glucose the group on carbon 1 points below the plane of the ring, in β above it (Grokipedia, Anomer). Emulsin "originally was the term applied to the extract of sweet almonds" and "remains as the historical prototype of β-glucosidase" (Worthington enzyme manual).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Anomer ; https://www.worthington-biochem.com/E/default.html

F25. Induced fit: Daniel Koshland's 1958 model holds that the enzyme changes shape when the substrate binds (Grokipedia, Enzyme); Fischer's model treated the enzyme as rigid and failed when "a smaller analogue could bind and still not react"; Koshland likened the fit to "a hand in a glove"; in 1978 Bennett and Steitz found the cleft of yeast hexokinase closing around glucose (Grokipedia, Daniel E. Koshland Jr.). OpenStax: lock-and-key was the view "for many years", while "Current research supports a more refined view scientists call induced fit."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Daniel_E._Koshland_Jr. ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Enzyme ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/6-5-enzymes

## When enzymes fail: heat and pH

F26. Temperature and pH: activity rises with temperature until denaturation, giving "an optimal rate of reaction at an intermediate temperature"; "Enzyme structures unfold (denature) when heated" (Wikipedia, Enzyme). "Most mammalian enzymes operate optimally near 37°C, but exceeding this threshold leads to thermal denaturation"; pH activity is "a bell-shaped activity curve" (Grokipedia, Enzyme). OpenStax: "High temperatures will eventually cause enzymes ... to denature", and "Extreme environmental pH values (acidic or basic) can cause enzymes to denature"; heat or pH outside the optimal range alters the active site.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Enzyme ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/6-5-enzymes

F27. Dangerous heat, told carefully: a fever is a regulated rise in the brain's set point; hyperthermia, including heat stroke (core above 40°C with nervous-system failure), is unregulated (Grokipedia, Hyperthermia; Wikipedia: "Hyperthermia differs from fever in that the body's temperature set point remains unchanged"). "At core temperatures exceeding 40°C, proteins begin to denature, leading to loss of enzymatic function" (Grokipedia, Hyperthermia). Hyperpyrexia is a temperature above 41 or 41.5°C; very high heat "induces direct cellular damage through protein denaturation, membrane instability, and disruption of enzymatic functions", and "A sustained temperature of 42°C or higher risks brain damage, organ failure, and death if not rapidly corrected" (Grokipedia, Fever). Wikipedia: in heat stroke, enzymes "fail to work effectively at higher temperatures" and further rises "can lead them to denature". (Brief, moderate fevers are thought to help the defences: see the T2 unit; not re-asked here.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hyperthermia ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fever ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperthermia

## How we know: the Krebs cycle (1937)

F28. Albert Szent-Györgyi found in 1933 that small amounts of four-carbon acids (fumarate, succinate, malate) added to minced pigeon breast muscle "dramatically accelerated oxygen uptake", the compounds "acting catalytically rather than as fuels, as they were not consumed in the process" (Grokipedia, Szent-Györgyi); Wikipedia: pigeon breast muscle "was very well qualified for the study of oxidative reactions"; his 1937 Nobel Prize covered work on fumaric acid. Krebs's Nobel lecture (search excerpt): Stare and Baumann showed in December 1936 that very small quantities of the acids acted catalytically, "as opposed to the oxidation in which the acids serve as substrate".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Albert_Szent-Gy%C3%B6rgyi ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citric_acid_cycle ; https://nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/krebs-lecture.pdf (excerpt via search)

F29. Hans Krebs and his student William Arthur Johnson, at Sheffield in 1937, found that citrate acts as a catalyst in the same way as succinate (Nobel lecture excerpt), and proposed a cyclic pathway "based on experiments with minced pigeon breast muscle" (Grokipedia, Citric acid cycle): citrate and related acids sped pyruvate oxidation, "indicating a regenerative cycle rather than a linear degradation"; earlier models had favoured straight-chain oxidation "without regeneration of catalysts" (Grokipedia). Krebs and Johnson (Enzymologia, 1937, as quoted in the eCampusOntario textbook Biochemistry, read through a search excerpt): "Since citric acid reacts catalytically in the tissue, it is probably removed by a primary reaction and then regenerated by a subsequent reaction." Nature replied on 14 June 1937 that it had "already sufficient letters to fill correspondence columns for seven or eight weeks", and the paper went to Enzymologia (Wikipedia). Krebs shared the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Fritz Lipmann; Martius and Knoop identified parts of the cycle independently in 1937 (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Citric_acid_cycle ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Krebs_(biochemist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citric_acid_cycle ; https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/bioc2580/chapter/the-citric-acid-cycle/ (quotation via search excerpt)

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- The oxygen breathed in becomes the carbon dioxide breathed out (F3: it becomes water).
- Slow burning gives out less heat than fast burning of the same fuel, so an animal and a fire should be compared hour for hour (F5a: per amount burned).
- Enzymes supply energy, are used up, or make a reaction give out more energy (F15).
- Enzymes speed reactions by warming the molecules (F15, F16).
- Lactic acid causes next-day soreness (F9, F10, F11).
- Without oxygen a cell makes no ATP at all (F6: fermentation makes 2 per glucose).
- Heat "uses up" enzymes, or body water boils at a high fever (F26, F27: enzymes unfold; water boils at 100°C).
- A specific enzyme picks its molecule by how loosely its atoms are held, or by how readily it dissolves (F23, F24: by shape).
- Breathing exists to cool the blood; body heat is inborn in the heart, or comes from the friction of the blood (F14: the respectable old ideas Lavoisier's calorimeter answered).
- The steps of breaking down fuel run as a one-way chain (F29: the earlier model).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-27)

F30. Pasteur's yeast and air, as he told it (Studies on Fermentation, 1876; English 1879): on 16 April 1860 "we used flat basins having glass bottoms and low sides", "in which the depth of the liquid is not more than a few millimetres", with "a thin layer of some saccharine juice impregnated with a trace of pure yeast"; in such thin layers open to the air the yeast grew far more for the sugar it used, a ratio of yeast to sugar up to "1/4" (Fordham sourcebook excerpt). Grokipedia (Pasteur effect): he gave yeast oxygen "by agitating the mixture or using open vessels", used sealed vessels for the airless controls, and aerated yeast made about 1 part yeast per 4-10 parts sugar against about 1 per 60-80 without air; it cites his 1860 memoir. His communication of 1861 (Comptes Rendus 52, 1260): yeast in contact with air grows actively while its fermenting power falls; protected from air it gives "the most energetic fermentation" (Le Moyne). No source describes air bubbled through the yeast. Told as "around 1860"; "Spread thin in a shallow dish open to the air ... shut away from air in a sealed flask ... far more sugar for each gram of yeast it grew".
    https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/1879pasteur-ferment.asp ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pasteur_effect ; https://web.lemoyne.edu/Giunta/pasteur.html

F31. Dating Szent-Györgyi's pigeon-muscle finding: a 1934 paper (National Library of Medicine, Szent-Györgyi papers) found that in finely chopped pigeon breast muscle succinic and fumaric acids sped respiration, "the first indication of their role as catalysts in the process"; the group paper Annau, Banga, Gözsy, Huszák, Laki, Straub and Szent-Györgyi, "Über die Bedeutung der Fumarsäure für die tierische Gewebsatmung", Hoppe-Seyler's Zeitschrift 236 (1935), followed; Krebs's Nobel lecture calls it "an important development" from "the laboratory of Szent-Györgyi of Szeged in 1935", with final proof of the catalytic effect by Stare and Baumann in December 1936 (search excerpts; the nobelprize.org PDF refuses automated fetches). Only Grokipedia gives 1933 (F28). Told as "in the mid-1930s".
    https://profiles.nlm.nih.gov/spotlight/wg/catalog/nlm:nlmuid-101584924X105-doc ; https://nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/krebs-lecture.pdf (search excerpt)

F32. Lactate is made all the time and builds up in hard exercise: "Muscles continuously produce lactate, even at rest; resting blood lactate levels are usually in the 1–2 mmol/L range"; above the lactate threshold "the lactate production exceeds the rate at which it can be broken down" and lactate accumulates in the blood; endurance training "raises the threshold at which lactate levels will rise" (Wikipedia, Lactate threshold). Grokipedia (Lactate threshold): "Exceeding the LT, however, triggers rapid lactate buildup"; "Endurance training shifts the LT to higher intensities in trained individuals." So trained runners make lactate too, and blood lactate does rise in a hard race (both used as wrong choices).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactate_threshold ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lactate_threshold

F33. DNA's two strands part only when very hot: in the polymerase chain reaction the denaturation step heats the mix "to 94–98°C for 20–30 seconds", where the hydrogen bonds between base pairs break and the double strand separates (Grokipedia, Polymerase chain reaction). So DNA does not split into its two strands at a body temperature of 42 degrees (a wrong choice).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Polymerase_chain_reaction

F34. Heat makes membranes more fluid, not stiffer: "higher temperatures increase molecular motion and disorder, enhancing fluidity", while "lower temperatures generally rigidify membranes by slowing molecular motion" (Grokipedia, Membrane fluidity); very high body heat brings "membrane instability" (Grokipedia, Fever, F27). So "membranes stiffen and let nothing pass" is wrong (a wrong choice).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Membrane_fluidity ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fever

F35. Lavoisier and Laplace measured the guinea pig's fixed air in separate trials, not inside the ice chamber: in a bell jar of pure air over mercury, and "in a jar through which we had set up a current of atmospheric air", weighing the alkali that took up the fixed air; from several runs "we have obtained an estimate of 224 grains as the amount of fixed air produced in ten hours", which by the charcoal measurement "must melt 10.38 ounces" (Memoir on Heat).
    https://erenow.org/common/history-philosophy-science-reader/68.php

F36. An ATP holds the same energy however it was made: its hydrolysis, ATP + H2O -> ADP + Pi, uses water, not oxygen, with a standard free energy change of "-30.5 kJ/mol"; ATP is "the primary energy currency in cells", made both anaerobically ("Anaerobic production of ATP occurs primarily through glycolysis", a net 2 ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation) and, in bulk, by oxidative phosphorylation (Grokipedia, Adenosine triphosphate). So "Each ATP it makes does less work without oxygen" is wrong (a wrong choice).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Adenosine_triphosphate
