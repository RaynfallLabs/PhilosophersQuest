## Light from an energised atom

F1. When an atom takes in energy (from heat, an electric discharge or light), its electrons are raised to higher energy levels; when an electron drops back, the atom gives off a photon whose energy equals the difference between the two levels (E = hv), so one drop gives light of one exact frequency, which the eye sees as one exact colour. Grokipedia (Flame test): heat makes electrons "jump to higher energy levels" and, falling back, they "emit photons of light at characteristic wavelengths unique to each element."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Emission_spectrum ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Flame_test ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spectral_line

F2. Each element's pattern of lines (its emission spectrum) is its own, "unique to each chemical element or compound due to the distinct quantized energy levels of its electrons" (Grokipedia). Each element leaves its own set of lines, so the lines identify the elements present (OpenStax).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Emission_spectrum ; https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/15-1-the-structure-and-composition-of-the-sun

F3. Kirchhoff's three laws of spectra: a hot solid, liquid or dense gas glows at all wavelengths, a continuous spectrum (a smooth rainbow); a thin hot gas gives off light only at particular wavelengths, bright lines; continuous light passed through a cooler thin gas comes out with dark lines exactly where that gas's bright lines would be. A white-hot filament, or the magnesium sparks of a firework's white stars, glow by incandescence, a broad spectrum from hot solids, not by lines.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Kirchhoff ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_spectroscopy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Spectral_line ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pyrotechnic_colorant

F4. Hydrogen's visible lines (the Balmer series) all come from electrons dropping to its second energy level: from level 3, 656.28 nm, red (H-alpha); from level 4, 486.13 nm, blue-green (H-beta); from level 5, 434.05 nm, violet-blue (H-gamma); from level 6, 410.17 nm, violet (H-delta). As the upper level rises, the drop is bigger and the wavelength shorter. Johann Balmer fitted a formula to these four lines in 1885.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Balmer_series ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Emission_spectrum

F5. A photon's energy rises with its frequency (E = hv = hc/wavelength): a red photon carries about 1.65 electronvolts, a violet one about 3.26, so violet light carries about twice the energy per photon of red. (The T4 electromagnetic-spectrum unit keys the idea; it is used here only as a step.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Photon_energy

## Neon signs

F6. A neon sign is a sealed glass tube of gas at low pressure (3 to 20 torr) with an electrode at each end. A high voltage drives free electrons through the gas; they collide with neon atoms and excite them, and the excited electrons drop back to lower levels and emit photons at wavelengths set by the energy differences. Pure neon glows reddish-orange; its strongest visible lines are at 640.23 nm (red) and 585.25 nm (orange-yellow). Other colours come from other gases (argon, helium, mercury vapour mixed in) and from phosphor coatings. Georges Claude showed the neon sign at the Paris Motor Show in December 1910.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Neon_lighting ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neon_lighting ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Neon

## Flame tests and fireworks

F7. Flame-test colours (Grokipedia table): sodium intense yellow-orange; copper blue-green; strontium crimson red; lithium crimson red; potassium lilac; barium apple green; calcium brick red or orange-red. Sodium is common in many compounds and gives "a persistent yellow flame that can obscure weaker emissions from other elements"; cobalt blue glass is used to absorb sodium's yellow.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Flame_test

F8. Sodium's yellow is a pair of lines at 589.0 nm (D2) and 589.6 nm (D1), the same place as Fraunhofer's dark D lines in sunlight.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fraunhofer_lines ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraunhofer_lines

F9. Firework colours come mainly from atomic emission: heat from the burning star excites the metal, which emits at "discrete wavelengths characteristic of the ion." Red comes from strontium (strontium nitrate, strontium carbonate) and lithium; green from barium; blue from copper (copper(II) chloride); yellow from sodium. White and silver stars come instead from incandescence of hot solid particles such as magnesium.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pyrotechnic_colorant ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Flame_test

F10. Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff, at Heidelberg, built their spectroscope in 1859 (Kirchhoff suggested in the summer of 1859 that Bunsen spread his flame colours with a prism). Their 1860 paper "Chemical Analysis by Observation of Spectra" compared each metal's chloride with its bromide, iodide, oxide, sulphate and carbonate, in flames of sulphur, aqueous alcohol, illuminating gas, carbon monoxide, hydrogen and detonating gas, and reported that "the variety of the compounds in which the metals were used, the differences in the chemical processes of the flames, and the great difference between their temperatures had no influence on the position of the spectral lines corresponding to the individual metals." The paper adds that the method "can easily be applied to the atmosphere of the sun and the bright stars."
    https://chemteam.info/Chem-History/Kirchhoff-Bunsen-1860.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bunsen ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Flame_test

F11. The same paper's test of sensitivity: they exploded 3 mg of sodium chlorate with milk sugar in a corner of their 60-cubic-metre room, and the sodium line showed in a flame across the room; "the eye can perceive quite distinctly less than 1/3 millionth mg. of the sodium salt." They discovered caesium (1860) by its blue lines and rubidium (1861) by its red lines.
    https://chemteam.info/Chem-History/Kirchhoff-Bunsen-1860.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gustav_Kirchhoff ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bunsen

## Joseph von Fraunhofer's dark lines

F12. Joseph von Fraunhofer, a Munich optician who made glass for telescope lenses, sent sunlight through a narrow slit and a prism in 1814 and found the band of colours crossed by dark lines. He mapped 574 of them and lettered the strongest A to K. William Hyde Wollaston had noted a few dark features in 1802. Fraunhofer's aim was to measure how his glass bent each colour; he could not explain the lines' origin. He died in 1826.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fraunhofer_lines ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Joseph_von_Fraunhofer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_von_Fraunhofer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraunhofer_lines

F13. Contemporaries at first suspected the lines were imperfections of the prisms (Grokipedia); repeated observations with several set-ups, and later a diffraction grating, showed the same lines, so they belonged to sunlight. Roy (2025), on Fraunhofer's memoir: "The lines seemed to be invariant of the refracting object, i.e., the locations of the bands did not change with substance."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fraunhofer_lines ; https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21747v1

F14. Fraunhofer then fitted a prism to a telescope and looked at the Moon, the planets and bright stars (his 1823 paper). Huggins and Miller (1864) summarise: "Fraunhofer recognized the solar lines D, E, b, and F in the spectra of the Moon, Venus, and Mars;" he found the line D in Capella, Betelgeuse, Procyon and Pollux; "Castor and Sirius exhibited other lines." Wikipedia: he found the spectra of bright stars, Sirius among them, differed from the Sun's and from each other. The Moon and the planets shine by reflected sunlight.
    https://jimclifford.ca/philosophical-transactions-ocr-1665-1869/articles/jstor-108875/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_von_Fraunhofer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_spectroscopy ; https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21747v1

F15. Huggins and Miller found no hydrogen lines C or F in Betelgeuse's spectrum, though strong in Aldebaran's, and argued that this seemed "to prove that the lines C and F are due to the luminous bodies themselves", not to Earth's air.
    https://jimclifford.ca/philosophical-transactions-ocr-1665-1869/articles/jstor-108875/

F16. Some dark lines are made in Earth's own air: the A band (759.4 nm) and B band (686.7 nm) at the red end come from oxygen in the atmosphere (telluric lines).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Fraunhofer_lines ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraunhofer_lines

F17. Fraunhofer also saw that flames (lamplight) show a bright doublet at the same position as the solar D lines, and did not explain the match.
    https://joachimweise.github.io/post/2020-10-20-fraunhofer-lines ; https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21747v1 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_von_Fraunhofer (a bright fixed line in the orange when light came from fire)

## Kirchhoff, sodium in the Sun, and the philosopher's impossible

F18. In 1859 Kirchhoff sent bright sunlight through a flame coloured by sodium into his spectroscope: the dark D lines did not fill in but grew darker ("the dark line became even darker", Roy 2025, on Kirchhoff and Bunsen). Kirchhoff concluded that coloured flames whose spectra hold bright, sharp lines weaken rays of those same colours passing through them, so dark lines appear in place of the bright ones when a bright enough light shines from behind; hence the Sun's dark D lines mean "that sodium is present in the solar atmosphere" (his words, 1859). Wikipedia: Kirchhoff showed "in 1859 that the Sun contains sodium."
    https://joachimweise.github.io/post/2020-10-20-fraunhofer-lines ; https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21747v1 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Kirchhoff ; https://lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/gustav-kirchhoff

F19. The mechanism of a dark line: atoms in a gas take up photons from a continuous source whose energy matches a jump between their levels, which lifts their electrons and removes those wavelengths; so an element's dark lines fall at "those same characteristic wavelengths" as its bright ones, and "a cooler gas overlying a hot continuum source results in absorption lines."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Spectral_line ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_spectroscopy

F20. Kirchhoff compared the Sun's lines with the spark spectra of about 30 elements and concluded that iron, calcium, magnesium, sodium, nickel and chromium are present in the Sun's cooler outer layers. Huggins and Miller (1864) found sodium, magnesium, calcium, iron and hydrogen in the light of Aldebaran.
    https://joachimweise.github.io/post/2020-10-20-fraunhofer-lines ; https://jimclifford.ca/philosophical-transactions-ocr-1665-1869/articles/jstor-108875/

F21. Auguste Comte, in his Cours de philosophie positive (1835), held that of the stars "we can conceive of the possibility of determining their shapes, their sizes, and their motions," but "we shall never be able by any means to study their chemical composition or their mineralogical structure"; their true mean temperatures, too, were "forever denied to us" (AIP translation). Wikipedia: "In thirty years people were beginning to learn the composition of stars through spectroscopy." Comte died on 5 September 1857, two years before Kirchhoff's sodium result.
    https://history.aip.org/exhibits/cosmology/tools/tools-comte.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Comte ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Auguste_Comte

## Helium: found in the Sun before it was found on Earth

F22. Pierre Janssen observed a total solar eclipse at Guntur, India, on 18 August 1868 and recorded a bright yellow line at 587.49 nm in the Sun's chromosphere (the glowing layer at its edge). The line was at first assumed to be sodium's.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Helium ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium

F23. Norman Lockyer observed the yellow line on 20 October 1868 and called it D3, as it lay near sodium's D1 and D2 but not on them. He concluded that it came from an element in the Sun unknown on Earth and named it helium, from the Greek word for the Sun (helios).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Helium ; https://blog.sciencemuseum.org.uk/150-years-since-the-discovery-of-helium/

F24. "There remained much doubt (and even mockery) over Lockyer's discovery" (Science Museum); the chemist Edward Frankland publicly renounced his part (Science Museum) and doubted the element existed (Wikipedia). The question was "finally settled" in 1895 (Science Museum).
    https://blog.sciencemuseum.org.uk/150-years-since-the-discovery-of-helium/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium

F25. On 26 March 1895 William Ramsay, looking for argon, treated cleveite, a uranium mineral, with acids and collected a gas whose spectrum showed a bright yellow line matching the Sun's D3 line; Lockyer and William Crookes identified it as helium. Per Teodor Cleve and Abraham Langlet in Uppsala isolated it from cleveite the same year and found its atomic weight. Luigi Palmieri had seen the D3 line in material from Vesuvius in 1881 without recognising a new element, and William Hillebrand saw odd lines in a uranium mineral but put them down to nitrogen.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Helium ; https://blog.sciencemuseum.org.uk/150-years-since-the-discovery-of-helium/

F26. Not every unmatched line was a new element: a green line at 530.3 nm in the Sun's corona, seen in 1869 by Charles Young and William Harkness, was blamed on an unknown element, "coronium"; in the 1930s Walter Grotrian and Bengt Edlén showed it came from iron stripped of 13 electrons, "the high ionization being due to the extreme temperature of the solar corona."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronium

## Cecilia Payne and the hydrogen stars

F27. Cecilia Payne's 1925 doctoral thesis, Stellar Atmospheres (Radcliffe College, Harvard; the first astronomy PhD there), applied Meghnad Saha's 1920 ionization theory to stellar spectra. She showed that "the great variation in stellar absorption lines was due to differing amounts of ionization at different temperatures," not to different amounts of the elements, and concluded that stars are composed primarily of hydrogen and helium. Her silicon, carbon and metals came out in about Earth's proportions; hydrogen came out about a million times more plentiful.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Payne-Gaposchkin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cecilia_Payne-Gaposchkin ; https://physicsworld.com/a/cecilia-payne-gaposchkin-the-woman-who-found-hydrogen-in-the-stars/

F28. Hydrogen's lines are strongest in A stars with atmospheres of about 10,000 K. In the hottest stars hydrogen is completely ionized and makes no lines; in cooler stars "practically all of the hydrogen atoms are in the lowest energy state," and few photons can lift them, so the lines are weak (OpenStax). Grokipedia: A stars have "the strongest hydrogen Balmer absorption lines among all stellar classes"; in O stars hydrogen is almost entirely ionized; in K stars the lines fade almost to nothing. OpenStax: "The primary reason that stellar spectra look different is because the stars have different temperatures"; most stars have nearly the same composition as the Sun.
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/17-3-the-spectra-of-stars-and-brown-dwarfs ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Stellar_classification

F29. Sirius A is an A1 star of about 9,900 K whose spectrum shows prominent hydrogen Balmer lines.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sirius

F30. Before Payne, astronomers assumed stars were made much as Earth is, with metals such as iron prominent (Grokipedia); her result went against "the prevailing belief that stars had a similar elemental composition as on Earth" (Wikipedia). Henry Norris Russell of Princeton "strongly supported the idea that the Earth and the Sun had the same composition" (Physics World).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cecilia_Payne-Gaposchkin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Payne-Gaposchkin ; https://physicsworld.com/a/cecilia-payne-gaposchkin-the-woman-who-found-hydrogen-in-the-stars/

F31. Russell, an outside examiner of her thesis, wrote to her: "It is clearly impossible that hydrogen should be a million times more abundant than the metals" (Physics World); he urged her not to assert that the Sun was mostly hydrogen, as it contradicted the consensus (Wikipedia). In the published thesis she wrote: "The enormous abundance derived for these elements in the stellar atmosphere is almost certainly not real."
    https://physicsworld.com/a/cecilia-payne-gaposchkin-the-woman-who-found-hydrogen-in-the-stars/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Payne-Gaposchkin ; https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/15-1-the-structure-and-composition-of-the-sun

F32. Russell reached the same conclusion by a different method in 1929 ("On the Composition of the Sun's Atmosphere") and wrote that "the most important previous determination of the abundance of the elements by astrophysical means is that by Miss Payne"; he cited her, though he never said he had first rejected her result, and for years he was generally credited with the discovery. Otto Struve later called her thesis "the most brilliant PhD thesis ever written in astronomy."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Payne-Gaposchkin ; https://physicsworld.com/a/cecilia-payne-gaposchkin-the-woman-who-found-hydrogen-in-the-stars/

F33. Today about 73 percent of the Sun's mass is hydrogen and 25 percent helium; by number of atoms, 92.0 percent hydrogen and 7.8 percent helium.
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/15-1-the-structure-and-composition-of-the-sun

## Misconceptions a 9th grader brings

- A flame's or firework's colour comes from how hot it burns, as red is "cool" and blue "hot" in a fire (F9, F10: the colour belongs to the metal; Kirchhoff and Bunsen's flames of very different heat left the lines where they were).
- Coloured light is white light dyed or filtered by the substance, as paint colours work (F1, F6: the atoms give the light off themselves).
- A neon tube burns its gas, or glows because the gas is white-hot like a filament (F3, F6).
- Red light is the strongest because red means hot (F5: violet photons carry about twice the energy).
- The dark lines are flaws or shadows in the glass (F13, F14), or all of them are made in Earth's air (F14, F15, F16: some are, most are not).
- A stronger line always means more of that element (F27, F28).
- Stars are made of the same stuff, in the same proportions, as Earth: the respectable belief before 1925 (F30).
- A line that matches no known element must be a new element (F26: coronium was iron).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-27)

F34. Hydrogen's energy levels are "En = -13.6 eV/n2" (Grokipedia, Hydrogen spectral series), and "the emitted photon's energy is the difference" between the two levels, so the fall from level 3 to 1 gives off about 12.1 eV, from 3 to 2 about 1.9 eV (H-alpha, 656.3 nm, red) and from 2 to 1 10.2 eV. The Lyman series, the falls to the first level, lies "in the ultraviolet region": Lyman-alpha (2 to 1) at 121.57 nm, 10.2 eV; Lyman-beta (3 to 1) at 102.57 nm (Grokipedia, Lyman series).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_spectral_series ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lyman_series

F35. Walther Ritz proposed the combination principle in 1908, "an empirical rule": an element's lines include "frequencies that are either the sum or the difference of the frequencies of two other lines." Bohr's 1913 levels explain it: "A transition in one step therefore has the same energy change as the sum of two transitions through an intermediate level" (Wikipedia). Grokipedia: Ritz's principle linked lines "without a physical interpretation" until Bohr.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rydberg%E2%80%93Ritz_combination_principle ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydrogen_spectral_series

F36. MISCONCEPTIONS for the two-step fall: two photons must carry more energy than one; a red photon's lower energy makes the two-step total smaller; energy is lost at the stop between falls (F34, F35: the total is the same).

F37. Helium in natural gas came later than 1895: Kansas gas wells near Dexter were "discovered in 1903 and analyzed in 1905" (Grokipedia, Helium); Hamilton Cady and David McFarland of the University of Kansas found that "1.84% of the gas sample was helium" (Wikipedia, Helium). "Helium extraction primarily occurs as a byproduct during natural gas processing" (Grokipedia). Ramsay's cleveite gas of 26 March 1895 came first (F25).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Helium ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium

F38. OpenStax Table 17.2, spectral classes: A, "Strongest hydrogen lines"; G (the Sun), "Weaker hydrogen lines"; K, "Very weak hydrogen lines"; M (Betelgeuse), no hydrogen feature listed, "Strong lines of neutral metals and molecular bands of titanium oxide dominate." "The primary reason that stellar spectra look different is because the stars have different temperatures"; the composition hypothesis "turned out to be wrong." Spectral classes measure surface temperature.
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/17-3-the-spectra-of-stars-and-brown-dwarfs

F39. Neon gives many lines, not a few: "The average color of this light to the human eye is red-orange due to many lines in this range", with a strong green line seen only in a spectroscope (Wikipedia, Neon); the NIST handbook lists about 60 strong neon lines between 450 and 750 nm, and 640.2 nm is one bright red line among several of equal or greater intensity, not the strongest (fact-check). A neon tube is "a form of cold cathode gas-discharge illumination" whose light "arises from the de-excitation of excited neon electrons" at "discrete wavelengths" (Grokipedia, Neon lighting).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neon ; https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/Handbook/Tables/neontable2.htm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Neon_lighting
