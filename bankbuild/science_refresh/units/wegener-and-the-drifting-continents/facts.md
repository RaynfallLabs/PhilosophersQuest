## Who Wegener was

F1. Alfred Wegener was born in Berlin on 1 November 1880. He took a doctorate in astronomy at the University of Berlin (1905 by Wikipedia; NASA and UCMP give 1904) but turned to meteorology, the science of weather and the air: he studied the upper air with kites and balloons, and in April 1906 he and his brother Kurt set a world endurance record in a balloon (52.5 hours aloft). NASA calls him "a German meteorologist and polar explorer". He went on four expeditions to Greenland (1906-08, 1912-13, 1929, 1930-31).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alfred_Wegener ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Wegener ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/wegener.html

F2. Geologists saw him as an outsider to their field: American geologists reportedly took the theory as an intrusion by a meteorologist (Grokipedia); "geologists, who viewed Wegener as an outsider" (Wikipedia); attacks often targeted him personally as an outsider (NASA). He could not get a professorship in Germany; in 1924 the University of Graz in Austria made him professor of meteorology and geophysics.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alfred_Wegener ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Wegener ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener

## The proposal, the fit and Pangaea

F3. Wegener first set out his idea in a lecture on 6 January 1912 to the Geological Association (Geologische Vereinigung) at the Senckenberg Museum in Frankfurt, and expanded it in the book Die Entstehung der Kontinente und Ozeane (The Origin of Continents and Oceans), first published in 1915, with later editions in 1920, 1922 and 1929 (first English edition 1924).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alfred_Wegener ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Wegener ; https://www.environmentandsociety.org/exhibitions/wegener-diaries/biography-alfred-wegener

F4. His claim: all of today's continents were once joined in a single supercontinent, which broke apart, its pieces slowly moving (drifting) to where they lie now.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pangaea ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Continental_drift ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_drift

F5. Pangaea: the 1915 edition called the lost supercontinent the "Urkontinent" ("primal continent"); Wegener used the name "Pangaea" once, in the 1920 edition ("the Pangaea of the Carboniferous"), and the name spread in German (1922) and English (1926) science after that. The name joins the Greek pan, "all", and Gaia, "Earth" or "land"; USGS glosses it "all lands". Pangaea was surrounded by one world ocean (Panthalassa); it existed from about 300 to 200 million years ago and its breakup began about 200 million years ago (Grokipedia: about 201 million; USGS: 225-200 million).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangaea ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pangaea ; https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/dynamic/historical.html (quoted in search results; the page itself refused the fetch)

F6. The geologists' views of the day: most held that continents and oceans were permanent features (USGS: "the scientific community firmly believed the continents and oceans to be permanent features"; James Dwight Dana's permanence theory); critics "favored fixed continents with only vertical movement" (Grokipedia); some invoked sunken continents and land bridges (F10). Lost continents that sank, such as Philip Sclater's "Lemuria" (1864), were proposed to explain shared fossils.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Continental_drift ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_drift ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Land_bridge

F7. The idea began with the fit of the coasts. In December 1910 Wegener wrote to his future wife (Else Koppen): "Doesn't the east coast of South America fit exactly against the west coast of Africa, as if they had once been joined?" (NASA's translation). On 6 December 1911 he wrote to her father, Wladimir Koppen, that he had come to the idea through "the correspondence between the coastlines", and that the proof would have to come from geological observation. Others had noticed the fit before him: Abraham Ortelius (1596), Antonio Snider-Pellegrini (1858).
    https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener ; https://www.environmentandsociety.org/exhibitions/wegener-diaries/biography-alfred-wegener ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Continental_drift

F8. The fit is better along the continental shelves than along today's shores: Wegener's evidence included that "Continent outlines, especially continental shelves, appear to fit together like puzzle pieces" (Scientific American). In the 1960s Edward Bullard, J. E. Everett and Alan Smith used a computer to fit the continents along the 914 m (3,000 ft) depth line, which "marks the continents' true edge", and got "a near perfect fit". (NGSS MS-ESS2-3 names "the shapes of the continents (including continental shelves)".)
    https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/history-of-geology/january-6-1912-continental-drift/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Bullard ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ess2-3-earths-systems

F9. In the autumn of 1911 Wegener came across papers that argued for a sunken land bridge between Africa and South America, citing fossils of identical animals on both; his idea "became a conviction". His evidence came to include the fit, fossils (Mesosaurus, Glossopteris, Lystrosaurus), matching rocks and mountain belts, and ancient climates. (The evidence itself is keyed in fossils-layers-and-a-changing-earth; this unit does not re-ask it as evidence.)
    https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Continental_drift

## The critics' case, and Wegener's answers

F10. Land bridges: "Orthodox science explained the matching organisms by postulating sunken land bridges between continents" (UCMP); scientists of the day held that Africa and South America had once been linked by a continent-sized land bridge that later sank (NASA). Eduard Suess proposed such connections in the 1880s (Grokipedia). Land-bridge maps failed to account for the ancient glaciation in India, Australia and South Africa (Wikipedia). Plate tectonics later superseded land bridges as the explanation for these old shared fossils (Grokipedia).
    https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/wegener.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Land_bridge ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_drift

F11. Mesosaurus was "an extinct genus of small, aquatic reptile", about 0.7-1 m long, of coastal lagoons and inland seas; its fossils lie in Early Permian rocks (about 299-270 million years old) in South Africa and Namibia and in Brazil and Uruguay. Wegener cited it in 1912; its way of life made crossing an open ocean impossible (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mesosaurus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_drift

F12. Wegener's answer to the land bridges (isostasy): continents are made of lighter granite and the sea floor of denser basalt; continents float on the denser rock, so a land bridge forced under the sea would have risen again (NASA). Land bridges of light continental crust "could not sink into the much denser oceanic basalts and vanish without a trace" (Scientific American's summary).
    https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/history-of-geology/january-6-1912-continental-drift/

F13. Wegener's mechanism: he pictured the continents moving through the denser ocean floor, "as enormous ice floes drifting through the denser oceanic crust" (Scientific American); "they plowed through the crust" (UCMP). His driving forces: a "polar-fleeing" force from Earth's rotation (Polflucht, a centrifugal effect pushing continents away from the poles) and tidal forces from the Moon and Sun (Grokipedia; UCMP: "centrifugal and tidal forces").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Continental_drift ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/wegener.html ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/history-of-geology/january-6-1912-continental-drift/

F14. Harold Jeffreys, a British geophysicist (also mathematician and seismologist), calculated in The Earth (1924) that Wegener's forces were far too weak: tidal friction would have to be "ten thousand million times" stronger than observed (Grokipedia). UCMP: critics argued that "centrifugal and tidal forces were far too weak to move continents", and a tidal force strong enough "would cause the Earth to stop rotating in less than one year". Scientific American: Jeffreys showed the forces "were far too weak, or, if strong enough, would have halted Earth's rotation". The missing driving force was the main objection to drift (Wikipedia; Grokipedia Plate tectonics: "primarily for lacking a plausible physical mechanism").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Harold_Jeffreys ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alfred_Wegener ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/wegener.html ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/history-of-geology/january-6-1912-continental-drift/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_drift

F15. Jeffreys never accepted drift or plate tectonics: he was still rejecting it in 1982 at 91 and died in 1989 without endorsing it (Grokipedia); "a stubborn opponent of the theory to his death" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Harold_Jeffreys ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Jeffreys

F16. Other objections: at the 1926 symposium of the American Association of Petroleum Geologists in New York, Franz Kossmat argued that the oceanic crust was too firm for continents to "simply plough through" (Wikipedia; NASA also places the symposium in New York, while Grokipedia's Wegener page says New Orleans, so no question names the city); critics argued that ploughing through oceanic crust would distort the continents (UCMP); Wegener's estimated speed (about 250 cm a year) was far too high (Wikipedia, UCMP). Wegener himself admitted the gap: in his 1929 revision he wrote, "The Newton of drift theory has not yet appeared" (NASA).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Wegener ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/wegener.html ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener

F17. The ridicule: critics called the theory "mere geopoetry" (NASA); one American scientist said accepting it would mean forgetting everything learned in the previous 70 years (NASA). CAUTION: the line "utter, damned rot" is given to Rollin Chamberlin on Grokipedia's Wegener page, to William Berryman Scott on Grokipedia's Continental drift page, and to "the president of the American Philosophical Society" by NASA; no question or context quotes it.
    https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alfred_Wegener ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Continental_drift

## His death

F18. Wegener's last expedition (1930-31) set out to "systematically study the great ice cap and its climate" (NASA), with a station in the middle of the ice sheet (Eismitte) from which its thickness could be measured and year-round weather observations made (Wikipedia). He reached Eismitte with supplies on 30 October 1930 and left for the coast on 1 November 1930, his 50th birthday, with the Greenlander Rasmus Villumsen. He died on the ice, apparently of heart failure brought on by overexertion; his body was found on 12 May 1931, and Villumsen was never found (NASA; Grokipedia; Wikipedia). (Sources differ on Villumsen's age, 22 or 27; not used.)
    https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Alfred_Wegener ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Wegener

F19. He died long before his idea won: "His hypothesis was not accepted by mainstream geology until the 1960s" (Wikipedia); "His recognition came only after his death" (NASA). In the 1950s the idea of drifting continents was "largely ridiculed" (Science News). CAUTION: Wikipedia also says its main tenets gained wide acceptance among European researchers in the 1920s, so no question says "most geologists" rejected it at his death.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Wegener ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener ; https://www.sciencenews.org/article/fluxgate-magnetometer-submarine-plate-tectonics

## The sea floor speaks

F20. Marie Tharp (1920-2006), an American geologist and ocean mapmaker, joined Columbia University's Lamont Geological Observatory in 1948 and worked with the geophysicist Bruce Heezen. Women were barred from the research ships at the time, so she drew her maps from depth soundings that ships' echo sounders had collected; she first went to sea in 1968.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Marie_Tharp ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Tharp ; https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/60481/how-one-womans-discovery-shook-foundations-geology

F21. In 1952 (Grokipedia: late 1952) Tharp lined up six profiles across the North Atlantic and found that each showed a V-shaped notch at the crest of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge: a rift valley running down the ridge, which she believed had formed where the sea floor was being pulled apart.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Marie_Tharp ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Tharp ; http://www.earthdate.org/episodes/first-map-of-the-ocean-floor

F22. Heezen's first doubt: by Tharp's own later account he "groaned and said 'It cannot be. It looks too much like continental drift.'" and he dismissed her interpretation as "girl talk" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia; Earthdate; Mental Floss). What changed his mind: Howard Foster, working nearby, had plotted the centres (epicentres) of undersea earthquakes for a project to route transatlantic cables (Wikipedia: Heezen commissioned the map), and when the two maps were laid together the earthquakes followed Tharp's valley, showing an active rift; Heezen agreed in 1953 (Earthdate). Grokipedia: Heezen was convinced by "plotting earthquake epicenters along the rift." Heezen at first explained the rift by an expanding Earth (Wikipedia, Mental Floss).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Tharp ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Marie_Tharp ; http://www.earthdate.org/episodes/first-map-of-the-ocean-floor ; https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/60481/how-one-womans-discovery-shook-foundations-geology

F23. Tharp and Heezen's maps: the 1957 physiographic map of the North Atlantic, and The World Ocean Floor (1977), painted with the Austrian artist Heinrich Berann; their work showed a continuous ridge system encircling the globe (Grokipedia Plate tectonics). The rift supported Wegener's drift and later Hess's sea-floor spreading (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Marie_Tharp ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Tharp ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics

F24. Harry Hess, an American geologist, proposed sea-floor spreading in 1960 and published it in 1962 as "History of Ocean Basins": magma wells up along the mid-ocean ridges, makes new ocean floor, and the floor spreads out both ways. "In this scenario, the continents moved apart because the ocean floor between them was growing" (Linda Hall Library); Wegener had "envisioned that the continents plow through the ocean floor, driven by some unknown force". Grokipedia: sea-floor spreading answered Wegener's mechanism problem with continents carried on "diverging crustal slabs".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Seafloor_spreading ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/harry-hess/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Hammond_Hess

## The magnetic stripes

F25. After the Second World War, ships towed magnetometers (instruments that measure magnetism, first built to hunt submarines) over the sea floor; the maps "revealed a curious zebra-stripe pattern of magnetic polarity on the seafloor" (Science News). The stripes run parallel to the ridges (Grokipedia). In the 1950s Ronald Mason and Arthur Raff mapped such stripes off western North America but did not read them as a record of reversals.
    https://www.sciencenews.org/article/fluxgate-magnetometer-submarine-plate-tectonics ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Vine%E2%80%93Matthews%E2%80%93Morley_hypothesis

F26. How the stripes form: new basalt at a ridge cools, and grains of magnetite (an iron-bearing mineral) line up with Earth's magnetic field; once the rock cools, that direction is locked in. Earth's magnetic field reverses from time to time (magnetic north and south swap; a compass needle's north-seeking end would then point to geographic south); there have been more than 180 reversals in the last 83 million years, the last about 780,000 years ago. Reversals come at irregular times ("reversals occur irregularly", with "no strict periodicity"; some spans of 10 million years or more had none), so stripes made at a steady spreading rate are of uneven widths, not evenly spaced. The stripes "record reversals of Earth's magnetic field, not changes in its intensity" (Grokipedia), although a towed magnetometer reads them as alternating highs and lows.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Vine%E2%80%93Matthews%E2%80%93Morley_hypothesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Geomagnetic_reversal ; https://www.sciencenews.org/article/fluxgate-magnetometer-submarine-plate-tectonics ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plate_tectonics

F27. Frederick Vine and Drummond Matthews, two British scientists at Cambridge, published in Nature on 7 September 1963 the idea that the stripes are a record of reversals carried away from the ridge as the sea floor spreads; Lawrence Morley, a Canadian geophysicist, reached the same idea earlier in 1963, but his papers were rejected. Because the crust moves away equally on both sides, the two sides carry mirror-image records: the ridge is a "twin-headed tape recorder" (Wikipedia). It was the first key test of sea-floor spreading (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Vine%E2%80%93Matthews%E2%80%93Morley_hypothesis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vine%E2%80%93Matthews%E2%80%93Morley_hypothesis

F28. The test passed: the Eltanin-19 profile, measured by the research ship Eltanin across the Pacific-Antarctic Ridge in 1965 and published by Walter Pitman and James Heirtzler of Lamont in December 1966, showed such symmetry that the pattern from west to east "nearly matches the pattern from east to west"; it became known as the "magic profile" and "one of the most convincing lines of evidence for seafloor spreading" (Science News). Vine later said results from this period convinced most Earth scientists (Grokipedia).
    https://www.sciencenews.org/article/fluxgate-magnetometer-submarine-plate-tectonics ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Vine%E2%80%93Matthews%E2%80%93Morley_hypothesis

## How the story ended

F29. Geologists turned round fast once the sea-floor evidence came: by 1967 plate tectonics had become "axiomatic to modern geophysics" (Wikipedia); sea-floor spreading "gained widespread acceptance" by 1968 (Grokipedia); "By the late 1960s, plate tectonics was well supported and accepted by almost all geologists", on the evidence of mapped ocean ridges, earthquake belts and the magnetic data (UCMP). USGS: Wegener's evidence, with "compelling evidence provided by post World War II technology", led to acceptance.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_drift ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Seafloor_spreading ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/wegener.html ; https://www.usgs.gov/educational-resources/plate-tectonics (quoted in search results)

F30. Right about the movement, wrong about the mechanism: "We now know that Wegener's theory was wrong in one major point: continents do not plow through the ocean floor"; continents and ocean floor together form solid plates that move (UCMP). "Wegener's mechanism was wrong. Plates move, and the driving force comes from within the planet" (Scientific American). He was right about most of his major concepts, and plate tectonics confirmed many of his reconstructions (NASA). Wegener believed the continents were still moving in his own day (his speed of about 250 cm a year was too high; the Atlantic widens about 2.5 cm a year) (Wikipedia).
    https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/wegener.html ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/history-of-geology/january-6-1912-continental-drift/ ; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/alfred-wegener ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Wegener

F31. Expanding Earth: Heezen favoured for a time the idea that Earth was swelling and its crust splitting at the ridges (Wikipedia, Mental Floss); satellite measurements later found no growth (the plates-at-work unit's context).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Tharp ; https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/60481/how-one-womans-discovery-shook-foundations-geology

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. That Wegener was rejected because he had no evidence. He had a great deal (F9); what he lacked was a force able to move continents (F14, F16).

M2. That only the continents move, pushing through or floating on the ocean: "Only continents move (Wegener's original concept...)" is on SERC's list of Earth-science misconceptions. The sea floor moves with them (F30).
    https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/intro/misconception_list.html

M3. That the mantle under the plates is liquid, a sea of melted rock (SERC lists "Asthenosphere is liquid" and "Lower Mantle is liquid").
    https://serc.carleton.edu/NAGTWorkshops/intro/misconception_list.html

M4. That once Wegener was shown right, his forces were shown right too. Jeffreys was right that they are far too weak (F14), and the drive comes from inside Earth (F30).

M5. That the sea-floor stripes record the field growing weaker and stronger: the first maps read them as highs and lows of intensity, but they record flips of direction (F25, F26).

M6. That a scientific argument is settled by a vote or by the most respected voices: the critics included the most respected geophysicist of the day, who never changed his mind (F15), and the change came with new evidence (F29).

M7. Earlier theories told honestly as wrong choices: permanent continents and oceans, vertical movement only (F6), sunken land bridges (F10), an expanding Earth (F31).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26; read on the web that day)

F33. The continents fit best along their true edges under the sea, not their shorelines. Edward Bullard, a British geophysicist (Wikipedia), with J. E. Everett and Alan Smith, published in 1965 in the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society computer fits of the continents around the Atlantic "at the 500 fathom line (approx. 900 m), not the shorelines"; "Snug and convincing fits were achieved" (Dewey's 2015 Royal Society commentary, which glosses the margins Wegener and Du Toit fitted as "the edge of the continental shelf not the shorelines"). The paper itself: the best fit "is at the 500 fm. contour which lies on the steep part of the continental edge". Linda Hall Library: they chose the 500-fathom depth for the continental edges rather than shorelines, because sea-level changes shift shorelines; the South America-Africa join was "remarkably good". Geological Society of London: "The best fit is obtained if the coastlines are matched at a depth of 1,000 metres below current sea level", and gaps or overlaps of the coastlines may be explained by coastal erosion, coastal deposition and sea-level or land-level changes since the continents parted. Wikipedia: Bullard used "a depth of 914 meters (3000 ft) below sea level" rather than coastlines and got "a near perfect fit".
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4360082/ ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/edward-bullard/ ; https://www.geolsoc.org.uk/Plate-Tectonics/Chap1-Pioneers-of-Plate-Tectonics/Alfred-Wegener/Jigsaw-Fit.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Bullard ; https://earthguide.ucsd.edu/eoc/eoc_teachers_hs_earth/content_tectonics/pdf/Bullard_fit.pdf (abstract quoted in search results)

F34. The continental shelf is "the gently sloping, submerged prolongation of a continent's continental crust", a "relatively shallow platform" from the coast out to where the sea floor steepens (the shelf break, at about 100-250 m, average about 135 m); it is "underlain primarily by continental crust". In the ice ages the sea stood lower (a Pleistocene lowstand about 120 m below today's level) and shelves lay bare.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Continental_shelf

F35. Wegener's forces after Jeffreys: Grokipedia names Jeffreys, a British geophysicist, a leading opponent, whose 1924 book The Earth dismissed Wegener's tidal forces (tidal friction would need to be "ten thousand million times" stronger). Whether Wegener's 1929 edition named mantle convection as a possible cause is unsettled in what I could read (the fact-check cites an EGU 2015 abstract; a search credits convection in 1929 to Arthur Holmes), so the questions say only that Wegener "could show no force strong enough", which every source supports (F14, F16). Reception: "widespread skepticism and outright rejection", especially in the United States and Britain, with neglect lasting until the mid-1950s (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Alfred_Wegener ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/history-of-geology/january-6-1912-continental-drift/

F36. Plates came after Hess: Hess announced sea-floor spreading in 1960 and published it in 1962 ("the continents moved apart because the ocean floor between them was growing"), but "the idea that the Earth's surface consists of discrete and separate plates was a concept not yet formulated in 1960" (Linda Hall Library). Plate tectonics, in which whole plates of continent and sea floor together move, was accepted by almost all geologists by the late 1960s (F29, F30).
    https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/harry-hess/ ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/wegener.html

F37. Satellites measured the plates moving only in the 1980s: during the 1980s satellite laser ranging and very long baseline interferometry improved until baselines could be measured below a centimetre, which "made possible, for the first time, direct measurement of the motion of the Earth's tectonic plates" (USGS, on NASA's Crustal Dynamics Project).
    https://www.usgs.gov/publications/measuring-contemporary-crustal-motions-nasas-crustal-dynamics-project

F38. The first Moon rocks reached Earth with Apollo 11, which splashed down on 24 July 1969 with about 21.5 kg of lunar rock and soil (Grokipedia), after plate tectonics had become "axiomatic" (by 1967) and sea-floor spreading had won wide acceptance (by 1968) (F29).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Apollo_11

F39. No measurement finds Earth swelling: a NASA-led study (2011) found the average change in Earth's radius about 0.1 mm a year, statistically insignificant (the plates-at-work unit's F10).
    https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-research-confirms-its-a-small-world-after-all/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Expanding_Earth

M8. Lures added in the revision: that the continents' true edges lie at the mid-ocean ridge (where the sea floor between them is made, F24) or on land along the coastal cliffs or mountains; that Jeffreys found the spin force pushed the continents toward the poles (it acts away from them, F13); satellite measurements, Moon rocks and a swelling Earth as what changed minds in the 1960s (F37-F39).
