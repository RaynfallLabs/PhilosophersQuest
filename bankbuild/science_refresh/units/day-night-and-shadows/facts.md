# Day, Night and Shadows: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; NASA, NSTA, UCAR, ORISE and Wikipedia to corroborate. Curriculum map line G5.E1 (NGSS 5-ESS1-2).

## Facts

F1 Earth spins (rotates) on its axis: an imaginary line through its centre, whose two ends are the North Pole and the South Pole. https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_rotation ("an internal axis passing through the North and South Poles"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Geographical_pole ("the imaginary line around which Earth spins once every 24 hours"; the poles are "the points where Earth's axis of rotation intersects the planet's surface"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ess1-2-earths-place-universe (ESS1.B: "the rotation of Earth about an axis between its North and South poles")

F2 One spin of Earth, measured against the Sun (a solar day), takes about 24 hours on average; measured against the stars it is a little shorter, 23 hours 56 minutes. https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_rotation ("an apparent solar day of exactly 24 hours on average"; sidereal day of 23 h 56 min); https://grokipedia.com/page/Day (the solar day "averages 24 hours in length"); https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/days/en/ ("On Earth, a solar day is around 24 hours"; sidereal day "almost exactly 23 hours and 56 minutes"); https://askdruniverse.wsu.edu/2024/01/31/go-sleep-night-side-world-morning/ (Washington State University: "The Earth completes one full spin on its axis about every 24 hours")

F3 Earth's spin makes day and night: as Earth turns, each place is carried into the Sun's light and then away from it, over and over. https://grokipedia.com/page/Night ("This phenomenon results directly from Earth's rotation"; a location "faces away from the Sun due to its axial rotation"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_rotation ("This daily cycle produces alternating periods of daylight and darkness"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ess1-2-earths-place-universe (ESS1.B: rotation causes "day and night")

F4 At any moment the half of Earth facing the Sun has day and the half facing away has night; the line between them (the terminator) sweeps across Earth as it turns. https://grokipedia.com/page/Night (rotation alternately exposes "different hemispheres to sunlight"; "the terminator, sweeps across the surface"); https://askdruniverse.wsu.edu/2024/01/31/go-sleep-night-side-world-morning/ ("it's daytime for me because my side of the planet is facing the sun")

F5 When it is noon at one place, it is about midnight at the place on the exact opposite side of Earth (its antipode), which faces away from the Sun; the two places are 12 hours apart and their days and nights are reversed. https://grokipedia.com/page/Antipodes ("when the Sun is at its zenith (noon) over one point", the antipode is at midnight; "inverted day-night cycles"; "a consistent 12-hour time difference"); https://cse.unl.edu/~astro/naap/lps/lunarPage3.html (University of Nebraska-Lincoln: on the side closest to the Sun "it's noontime, or close to it"; "On the other side of the earth the time is midnight")

F6 Earth's trip around the Sun takes about 365 days (a year), not a day, so it is not what makes day and night or the Sun's daily path across the sky. https://science.nasa.gov/earth/facts/ ("It takes 365.25 days to complete one trip around the Sun"); https://www.nsta.org/science-and-children/science-and-children-novemberdecember-2022/q-do-you-have-activity-daily-and (NSTA: "Earth's revolution around the Sun is not why the Sun appears to move across the sky over the course of the day"; it "is due to the spinning, or rotation, of Earth")

F7 Earth spins from west to east (counterclockwise, seen from above the North Pole). https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_rotation ("The direction of Earth's rotation is from west to east, as viewed from above the North Pole"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sunrise ("the planet's rotation on its axis from west to east")

F8 Because Earth spins toward the east, the Sun (and the Moon and stars) seem to rise in the east, cross the sky, and set in the west. The Sun rises due east only at the equinoxes; on other days it rises somewhat north or south of east, so "rises in the east" is true in the plain sense. https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_rotation (rotation "causes the Sun, Moon, stars, and planets to appear to rise in the east and set in the west"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Diurnal_motion (objects "rise in the eastern horizon, traverse the sky, and set in the western horizon each day"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sunrise (the Sun appears "typically in the east"; due east at the equinoxes); https://orise.orau.gov/K12/documents/lesson-plans/sun-shadows-and-science.pdf (ORISE grade 4 lesson: "The sun 'rises' in the east and 'sets' in the west")

F9 The Sun does not travel around Earth. Its daily movement across the sky is only apparent: Earth's spin makes it seem to move. https://www.thenational.academy/pupils/programmes/science-primary-year-5/units/earth-sun-and-moon/lessons/why-the-sun-appears-to-move-across-the-sky/video (Oak National Academy, Year 5: "We know that the Sun does not move around the Earth"; "it's the fact that the Earth is rotating that makes it look like the Sun is moving across the sky"); https://scied.ucar.edu/activity/sunshine-shadows (UCAR: "the sun itself is not moving. Earth is moving. It's spinning on its axis"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Diurnal_motion ("the apparent daily movement of celestial objects")

F10 For a long time most people believed the Sun, Moon and stars circled a still Earth, because the Sun appears to go round Earth once a day and Earth does not seem to move; from the late 1500s the Sun-centred model replaced that view. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_model ("from anywhere on Earth, the Sun appears to revolve around Earth once per day"; "Earth seems to be unmoving from the perspective of an earthbound observer"; "gradually superseded by the heliocentric model of Copernicus and Kepler"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_rotation (ancient models put "the apparent daily motion of the stars and Sun" down to a celestial sphere turning round a still Earth)

F11 The Sun is highest in the sky at solar noon, around midday; solar noon is often not exactly 12:00 on the clock. https://grokipedia.com/page/Noon ("Solar noon is the precise astronomical moment when the Sun reaches its highest elevation in the sky"; it "deviates" from clock noon); https://grokipedia.com/page/Day (solar noon is "when the Sun reaches its highest point in the sky")

F12 Outside the tropics the Sun is never straight overhead, even at noon, so an upright object there always casts some shadow while the Sun is up. In the continental United States and Alaska the Sun is never overhead; only Hawaii lies in the tropics. https://static.nsta.org/pdfs/UncoveringAstronomyp47.pdf (NSTA, Uncovering Student Ideas in Astronomy, probe "No Shadow", grades 3-8: "The Sun is never directly overhead except on certain occasions for locations between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn"; "for the continental United States and Alaska there is always a shadow whenever the Sun is visible in the sky"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun_path (overhead passages only at tropical latitudes)

F13 A shadow is a dark area where light from a source is blocked by an object. (Grokipedia's "Shadow" page is about a calypso singer, so Wikipedia is used here.) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow ("A shadow is a dark area on a surface where light from a light source is blocked by an object"); https://scied.ucar.edu/activity/sunshine-shadows

F14 A shadow falls on the side of the object away from the light, pointing in the opposite direction from the Sun. https://orise.orau.gov/K12/documents/lesson-plans/sun-shadows-and-science.pdf (ORISE: "shadows being cast in the opposite direction of the sun's location"); https://www.nsta.org/science-and-children/science-and-children-novemberdecember-2022/q-do-you-have-activity-daily-and (NSTA: does a pencil's shadow point "toward or away from" the flashlight; answer "away"); https://www.varsitytutors.com/practice/subjects/5th-grade-science/lessons/collect-data-on-shadow-changes ("a shadow forms on the side opposite the Sun")

F15 So a shadow's direction changes through the day: with the Sun in the east in the morning, shadows point roughly west; with the Sun in the west in the afternoon, they point roughly east. On a horizontal sundial in the Northern Hemisphere the shadow moves from west to east. https://www.varsitytutors.com/practice/subjects/5th-grade-science/lessons/collect-data-on-shadow-changes ("shadow direction shifts from roughly west in the morning to roughly east in the afternoon"); https://orise.orau.gov/K12/documents/lesson-plans/sun-shadows-and-science.pdf ("When the sun was positioned in the east, what direction did the tower's shadow point?"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sundial (the shadow "moves from west to east (clockwise)"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ess1-2-earths-place-universe ("daily changes in the length and direction of shadows")

F16 A shadow's length depends on how high the Sun is: a low Sun makes long shadows and a high Sun makes short ones. https://www.nsta.org/science-and-children/science-and-children-novemberdecember-2022/q-do-you-have-activity-daily-and (NSTA: "Your shadow is long when the Sun is low in the sky"; "Your shadow is short when the Sun is high in the sky"); https://scied.ucar.edu/activity/sunshine-shadows (UCAR: "When the Sun is low in the sky, a student's shadow will look longer. When the Sun is higher in the sky, a student's shadow will be shorter"; a Sun "directly, or nearly directly, overhead" would "cause little or no shadows"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_zenith_angle ("the gnomon's shadow length inversely relates to the solar elevation"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow (length "proportional to the cotangent of the sun's elevation angle")

F17 Through a sunny day shadows are longest in the early morning and late afternoon, near sunrise and sunset when the Sun is low, and shortest around midday, when the Sun is highest. https://orise.orau.gov/K12/documents/lesson-plans/sun-shadows-and-science.pdf ("shadows are longest in the early morning and late afternoon when the sun is closer to the horizon, and shortest around noon when the sun is highest in the sky"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Noon ("solar noon occurs at the moment when the shadow is shortest"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sundial ("shortest at solar noon"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow ("Near sunrise and sunset ... shadows can be extremely long"); https://www.sciencefriday.com/educational-resources/a-human-sundial/ ("It might start off in the morning long, get shortest some time around lunchtime")

F18 A sundial tells the time from the shadow of a pointer (a gnomon); the shadow moves as the Sun seems to move across the sky. Simple shadow clocks using sticks were made in ancient Egypt around 1500 BCE. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sundial (the gnomon is "a raised projection such as a rod or style that casts the shadow"; "The apparent motion of the Sun across the sky is primarily due to the Earth's rotation"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Gnomon ("simple shadow clocks employing sticks as gnomons were developed around 1500 BCE")

F19 Three other lines and paths, for the wrong choices: the equator is an imaginary line running around Earth's middle, halfway between the poles (around Earth, not through it); an orbit is the path Earth travels around the Sun; the horizon is the line where land or sea seems to meet the sky. https://grokipedia.com/page/Equator ("an imaginary circumferential line"; at 0 degrees latitude, midway between the poles); https://grokipedia.com/page/Orbit; https://science.nasa.gov/earth/facts/ (one trip around the Sun); https://grokipedia.com/page/Horizon ("The horizon is the apparent line that separates Earth from the sky")

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Baxter (1989) surveyed pupils aged 9-16 in England. Their six notions for day and night: "Sun goes behind hill", "Clouds cover the Sun", "Moon covers the Sun", "Sun goes around the Earth once a day", "Earth goes around the Sun once a day", and the correct "Earth spins on its axis once a day". Baxter, J. (1989), International Journal of Science Education 11(5), 502-513, figures 4-5: https://doi.org/10.1080/0950069890110503 (abstract: https://istardb.org/aerpublications/childrens-understanding-of-familiar-astronomical-events/)

M2 Vosniadou and Brewer (1994) asked 1st, 3rd and 5th graders: younger children said "the sun goes down behind mountains, clouds cover up the sun"; older children built models in which "the sun and the moon revolve around the stationary earth every 24 hours". Cognitive Science 18(1), 123-183: https://istardb.org/aerpublications/mental-models-of-the-day-night-cycle/

M3 Stover and Saunders (2000), Journal of Elementary Science Education, list "The Earth's revolution around the sun causes night and day" and "The sun revolves around the Earth." https://wordpressua.uark.edu/scimap/?p=778

M4 That the Sun is straight overhead at noon, so a noon shadow vanishes. NSTA: "Many people mistakenly think that, at noon, the Sun is directly overhead" (https://www.nsta.org/science-and-children/science-and-children-novemberdecember-2022/q-do-you-have-activity-daily-and); the NSTA "No Shadow" probe (grades 3-8) calls it "a widely held misconception" (https://static.nsta.org/pdfs/UncoveringAstronomyp47.pdf).

M5 That shadows are longest at noon because the Sun is strongest then. Boero (Genoa) asked "Is your shadow longer at 9 a.m. or at noon?"; the prevailing answer, "the sun shadow is longer at noon, because the sun is stronger", came from 41% of 4th graders (630 protocols) and 38% of 6th graders (2,350). In children's drawings "the sun is usually positioned on the same side of the person with respect to his/her shadow" (so: a shadow pointing toward the Sun). https://sites.unipa.it/grim/EBoero9.PDF

M6 That a shadow stays the same size all day. NSTA (Keeley's probe "When Is My Shadow the Longest?"): "Research has found that some children think their shadow stays the same shape and size throughout the day." https://www.nsta.org/blog/having-wonderful-ideas-about-science ; Oak National Academy's Year 5 lesson also answers a pupil who thinks "the Sun stays in the same position in the sky all day".

M7 That one spin takes about 12 hours (the length of daylight), or mixing up the day with the year (M1's "Earth goes around the Sun once a day"). The 12-hour version is the author's judgment, no survey found; it is plainly false (F2).

M8 That a shadow only shortens after sunrise and never lengthens again (lure "Long at first, then shorter all day"): author's judgment, no survey found; false by F17.

## Notes and cautions

- Never say the Sun is straight overhead at noon (F12). Say it is highest around midday.
- Say "about 24 hours" for one spin (F2: the solar day averages 24 hours; the spin against the stars is 23 h 56 min).
- "Rises in the east, sets in the west" is the plain truth for a 5th grader; the exact point shifts north or south of east with the seasons (F8). Morning and afternoon shadow directions are "roughly" west and east for the same reason (F15).
- The noon shadow is the shortest of the day, but in winter far from the equator it can still be long. Say "shortest", not "short", when exactness matters.
- Leave the seasons (axis tilt, longer summer days) and the Moon's phases to their own T2 units.
- how-light-travels already asks why a shadow has the object's shape (straight-line light); this unit does not.
- the-sun-and-its-planets already asks that one trip round the Sun is a year; this unit only uses the year as a wrong choice for one spin.
