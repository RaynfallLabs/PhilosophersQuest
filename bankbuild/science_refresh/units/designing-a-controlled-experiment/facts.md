## The three kinds of variable

F1. The independent variable is the factor the experimenter changes on purpose to see its effect (Grokipedia: "the factor that is manipulated or controlled by the researcher to determine its effect on other elements"; Pearson: "deliberately changed or manipulated by the experimenter"). In a fertilizer test, the amount of fertilizer is the independent variable.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dependent_and_independent_variables ; https://www.pearson.com/channels/biology/study-guides/controlled-experiments-variables-groups-and-experimental-design

F2. The dependent variable is what is measured or observed to see the effect, the outcome that changes in response to the independent variable (Grokipedia: "the outcome or response that is measured or observed"). In a fertilizer test, the plants' growth is the dependent variable. An investigation is set up to find how changing the independent variable affects the dependent variable, so the dependent variable must be a measure of the outcome the question asks about.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dependent_and_independent_variables ; https://www.doe.mass.edu/stem/ste/g6-g8.pdf

F3. Controlled variables (also called control variables or constants) are the factors kept the same throughout an experiment, in every group and trial, so that the independent variable's effect can be isolated (Grokipedia: "factors that researchers intentionally hold constant throughout an experiment"; Pearson: "all other factors that are kept the same between experimental and control groups", such as water, sunlight and planter size in a daisy-fertilizer test).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dependent_and_independent_variables ; https://www.pearson.com/channels/biology/study-guides/controlled-experiments-variables-groups-and-experimental-design

## The control group

F4. A control group is the group that does not receive the experimental treatment and is used as a baseline for comparison; it gets the same conditions as the experimental group except the factor being tested (Grokipedia, Scientific control: "a standard or baseline used to isolate the effect of a single independent variable"; Pearson: "The group that does not receive the experimental treatment; used as a baseline for comparison"). In Pearson's example the daisies given no fertilizer are the control group and those given fertilizer are the experimental group. The group or trial that gets the treatment is the experimental group.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_control ; https://www.pearson.com/channels/biology/study-guides/controlled-experiments-variables-groups-and-experimental-design

F5. A control group and controlled variables are different things: the control group is a whole group left without the treatment, while controlled variables are the conditions held the same for every group. The shared word "control" makes students mix them up (Pearson defines the two separately; a test that compares brands with one another, with no group left untreated, has controlled variables but no control group).
    https://www.pearson.com/channels/biology/study-guides/controlled-experiments-variables-groups-and-experimental-design ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_control

F6. Without a control, a change cannot be pinned on the treatment. Grokipedia, Scientific control: when controls are missing, "it becomes impossible to confidently determine whether changes in the dependent variable result from the intervention". Running the same trial again with every subject treated (another season, or another garden or class given the treatment too) still leaves nothing untreated to compare with.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_control

## Flaws

F7. Two things changed at once: if a second factor differs between the groups along with the independent variable, the effect cannot be put down to either one. Grokipedia calls such a factor a confounder, one "causally associated with both the exposure and the outcome", which can make an effect look "stronger, weaker, or even reversed". Controls work by "holding all other variables constant except the one being tested".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Confounding ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_control

F8. Light is a factor in plant growth that a plant test must hold the same: in Pearson's daisy example, sunlight is one of the controlled variables; in Grokipedia's plant-growth example a control group is kept in darkness to test the effect of light.
    https://www.pearson.com/channels/biology/study-guides/controlled-experiments-variables-groups-and-experimental-design ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Scientific_control

F9. Scores on a test taken again tend to rise from practice alone, the practice effect (Wikipedia: "the outcome/performance change resulting from repeated testing"; participants become "familiarized with testing conditions, thus increasing responsiveness and performance"). So marks that rise through a term need not come from anything new done in the room.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practice_effect

## What a result does and does not show

F10. A result applies to what was tested. External validity is "the extent to which the results of a research study can be generalized or applied to individuals, settings, and times" beyond those studied (Grokipedia). Francis Galton, checking Charles Darwin's maize results, wrote that a crossed series would always exceed a self-fertilised one "within the range of the conditions under which the present experiment has been made".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/External_validity ; https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1876_CrossandSelfFertilisation_F1249/1876_CrossandSelfFertilisation_F1249.html

## Many trials, a large sample, and chance

F11. Living things vary, and a small sample can be swayed by chance; the average of many trials comes close to the true value (Grokipedia, Law of large numbers: "with sufficiently many independent repetitions, the sample average reliably approximates the true expectation", though a proportion "may vary in small samples due to chance"; Sample size determination: sampling error "decreases as sample size increases").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Law_of_large_numbers ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sample_size_determination

F12. Not every seed sprouts, even in good conditions: some seeds are dead (non-viable) or dormant ("the inability of viable seeds to germinate under otherwise favorable environmental conditions"), and germination capacity is "often reaching 80-95% for high-viability orthodox seeds", so one seed in five to twenty fails even in a good batch.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Germination

F13. Charles Darwin, in The Effects of Cross and Self Fertilisation in the Vegetable Kingdom (1876), wrote that "When only from two to six pairs of plants were measured, the results are manifestly of little or no value"; and "As only a moderate number of crossed and self-fertilised plants were measured, it was of great importance to me to learn how far the averages were trustworthy." Galton wrote, "We ought to have measurements of at least fifty plants in each case, in order to be in a position to deduce fair results."
    https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1876_CrossandSelfFertilisation_F1249/1876_CrossandSelfFertilisation_F1249.html ; https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/agridat/html/darwin.maize.html

## Real experiments

F14. Broadbalk: John Bennet Lawes and Joseph Henry Gilbert started the Broadbalk winter wheat experiment at Rothamsted, England, in autumn 1843; the first crop was harvested in 1844. Its strips get various combinations of inorganic fertilizers (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, magnesium) and organic manures, and "a control strip has received no fertilizer or organic manures since 1843". "Wheat is grown every year on all or part of the experiment." Rothamsted calls it "the longest running scientific experiment in the world" (Grokipedia: "the world's longest-running continuous agronomic trial").
    https://era.rothamsted.ac.uk/Broadbalk ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rothamsted_Research

F15. Broadbalk yields: the unfertilised control has historically yielded under 1 tonne of grain a hectare (Rothamsted's guide: around 1 t/ha, like the early yields), while modern well-fertilised plots give above 10 to 13 tonnes a hectare. The control is what each treatment is measured against.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rothamsted_Research ; https://era.rothamsted.ac.uk/eradoc/OCR/Guide2012-10-20

F16. Darwin's method for his crossed and self-fertilised plants: seeds were germinated on damp sand, and "as often as a pair germinated at the same time, they were planted on opposite sides of a pot, with a superficial partition between the two"; the partition "was always kept directed towards the chief source of the light, so that the plants on both sides were equally illuminated"; the soil was "well mixed, so as to be uniform in composition"; the plants were watered at the same time and as equally as possible. A crossed plant grew from a flower fertilised with pollen from a distinct plant; a self-fertilised plant from a flower fertilised with its own pollen. He compared their heights.
    https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1876_CrossandSelfFertilisation_F1249/1876_CrossandSelfFertilisation_F1249.html

F17. Darwin's maize (Zea mays): Galton's analysis of the table found "Out of the fifteen cases in the table, there are only two exceptions" to the rule that the crossed plant was taller, so in 13 of 15 pairs the crossed plant grew taller. Ronald Fisher used the same data in The Design of Experiments (1935).
    https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1876_CrossandSelfFertilisation_F1249/1876_CrossandSelfFertilisation_F1249.html ; https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/agridat/html/darwin.maize.html

F18. Christiaan Eijkman, a Dutch physician and pathologist, set up chicken colonies at the Pathological Laboratory in Batavia (now Jakarta) on Java, Dutch East Indies, in 1889. Chickens fed polished white rice developed polyneuritis, a nerve disease like human beriberi, within 3 to 4 weeks; unpolished whole rice prevented it "in control groups" or cured sick birds. Between 1890 and 1897 he ran trials with over 100 chickens: nearly all birds on polished rice fell ill, while those on whole rice were unaffected. At first he thought beriberi was infectious. The missing substance was later identified as thiamine (vitamin B1). He shared the 1929 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Frederick Gowland Hopkins. (Wikipedia tells of a change of rice supply, by a new cook, before the trials; Grokipedia does not, so the questions do not use it.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Christiaan_Eijkman ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Eijkman

## The grade

F19. The middle-school (grades 6-8) practice "Planning and Carrying Out Investigations" asks students to identify independent and dependent variables and controls, the tools needed, how measurements will be recorded, and how many data are needed to support a claim; Massachusetts' grade 6-8 guidance: "Investigations are setup to determine how changing an independent variable affects a dependent variable. Other variables should be controlled to make the relationship between the independent variable and the dependent variable clear." England's Oak National Academy teaches variables in Year 7. (Curriculum map line G7.M1; TEKS 7.1B.)
    https://www.doe.mass.edu/stem/ste/g6-g8.pdf ; https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/18290/chapter/12 ; https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/variables

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Swapping the independent and dependent variables. Oak National Academy's Year 7 lesson lists it: "Pupils tend to confuse independent and dependent variables."
    https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/variables

M2. Calling the thing the experimenter sets a "controlled" variable because the experimenter controls it, and confusing the control group with controlled variables (F5).
    https://www.pearson.com/channels/biology/study-guides/controlled-experiments-variables-groups-and-experimental-design

M3. Difficulty identifying the control: Shi, Power and Klymkowsky (2011) found "a high percentage of students had difficulty identifying control experiments", even after three university lab courses. Common wrong picks: the group with the most treatment, the middle amount, or the group with the best result.
    https://digitalcommons.georgiasouthern.edu/ij-sotl/vol5/iss2/8

M4. Thinking repeats make a test fair: the Royal Society of Chemistry notes that pupils of 11-14 often think repeating measurements makes a test "fair", while repeats mainly find anomalies and reduce random error; without a control, a repeat still has nothing to compare with (F6).
    https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/how-to-teach-controlled-variable-investigations-at-11-14/4013634.article

M5. Reflex criticisms (too few trials, no control, two things changed) offered whether or not they apply; and treating the control group itself as a flaw (an author's note from M3-M4: the honest wrong choices in this unit use them where they do not apply).

M6. Overgeneralising a result to other kinds, amounts or uses never tested (F10).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/External_validity

F22. A fair test that finds no difference is a result, not a failure. Wikipedia (Null result): "a null result is a result without the expected content: that is, the proposed result is absent"; "This does not imply a result of zero or nothing, simply a result that does not support the hypothesis"; the Michelson-Morley experiment is the famous example. Science Buddies' guidance for school projects: if no specific error can be found in the procedure, the data gathered are still valid, and a result that goes against the hypothesis still teaches something. (Grokipedia has no Null result page.)

## Misconceptions added in the revision

M7. That a test that finds no difference "failed" or "shows nothing", or that unwelcome data must hide a mistake or a masked effect. Chinn and Brewer ("Psychological Responses to Anomalous Data", Cognitive Science Society proceedings, 1992; and "The Role of Anomalous Data in Knowledge Acquisition", Review of Educational Research 63, 1993) set out seven responses people make to data that go against their idea; the first six preserve the idea (among them ignoring, excluding, rejecting the data, with the example of students who "rejected the experiment as methodologically too insensitive to detect the predicted effect", and reinterpreting it), and only the last is real change of the idea.
    https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0d65f9gt ; https://www.causeweb.org/cause/node/6957
