## The scale: 0 to 14, neutral at 7

F1. The pH scale runs from 0 (very acidic) to 14 (very basic), with 7 neutral. (Grokipedia: from "pH 0, corresponding to strong acids like 1 M HCl" to "pH 14, for strong bases"; Museion: "0 is very acidic, 14 is very basic, and 7 is neutral (distilled water)"; USGS: "The range goes from 0 to 14, with 7 being neutral.") Caveat, not taught here: very concentrated solutions can fall below 0 or rise above 14 (Grokipedia: "pH values can be negative for C > 1 M"; "Superbasic media ... extend beyond pH 14").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/PH ; https://www.museion.ku.dk/blog/100-years-with-ph/ ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/ph-and-water

F2. Pure water has a pH of 7 at 25 C and is neutral (Wikipedia: "Pure water has a pH of 7 at 25 °C, meaning it is neutral"). Neutral means the hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions are equal in number (Wikipedia defines neutrality as the state where the two concentrations are equal; Grokipedia: "pure water where [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷ M at 25°C"). So even pure water holds a few of each ion. Neutral pH shifts with temperature (Grokipedia: "neutral pH ≈ 6.14" at 100 C); not used in a key.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH ; https://grokipedia.com/page/PH

F3. An acid, dissolved in water, raises the number of hydrogen ions (Grokipedia, Acid: Arrhenius, "in his 1884 doctoral dissertation", defined an acid as "a substance that increases the concentration of hydrogen ions" in water); acids have a pH below 7 (Grokipedia, PH: "values below 7 indicating acidic conditions"). A base raises the number of hydroxide ions (Grokipedia, Base: "any compound that increases the hydroxide ion (OH⁻) concentration in aqueous solution"); bases have a pH above 7 ("bases in aqueous solutions have a pH greater than 7"), and "Bases turn red litmus paper blue."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Acid ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Base_(chemistry) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/PH ; https://teksguide.org/teks/s86d (glossary: an acid has "a pH less than 7", a base "a pH greater than 7")

F4. Each step on the pH scale is a tenfold change: "a difference of one in pH is equivalent to a tenfold difference in hydrogen ion concentration" (Wikipedia); "Water with a pH of five is ten times more acidic than water having a pH of six" (USGS). So two steps are ten times ten, a hundredfold, and three steps a thousandfold. (Grokipedia gives the whole scale as "a 10¹⁴-fold variation in a single 14-unit interval".)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/ph-and-water ; https://grokipedia.com/page/PH

## Real acids and bases (lab, body, nature)

F5. Battery acid: the electrolyte of a lead-acid (car) battery is "dilute sulfuric acid", about 35 percent sulfuric acid by weight (Grokipedia, Lead-acid battery; Grokipedia, Sulfuric acid: a "Battery acid" grade at 30-36% H₂SO₄); its pH is below 1 (Wikipedia pH table: battery acid "less than 1"). "Sulfuric acid is highly corrosive to human tissues" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lead–acid_battery ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sulfuric_acid ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH

F6. Lye is sodium hydroxide, also called caustic soda, a strong base, pH "approximately 14 for a 1 M solution"; it is "highly corrosive to metals, tissues, and other materials" and causes severe chemical burns ("tissue liquefaction and deep necrosis"); drain cleaners use it to "unclog drains and pipes by dissolving fats, hair, and organic matter" (Grokipedia, Sodium hydroxide). Wikipedia's pH table gives lye as 14. "Bases are corrosive to biological tissues" (Grokipedia, Base).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium_hydroxide ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Base_(chemistry)

F7. Other everyday bases: soap is mildly alkaline, about pH 9-10 for personal-care soaps (Grokipedia, Soap; its sections range from 7.5 to 11); household ammonia is about pH 11.5-12 (Grokipedia, Ammonia solution: "usually has a pH in the range of 11.5–12"; Wikipedia's table: 11.0-11.5); chlorine bleach is pH 11-13 (Grokipedia, Bleach; Wikipedia's table: 12.5). Ammonia is chemically a "weak" base (Grokipedia, Base); at 8th grade, "stronger" means farther from 7.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Soap ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ammonia_solution ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Bleach ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH

F8. Blood is held in a narrow band just above 7: "tightly maintained between 7.35 and 7.45" (Grokipedia, PH); "within a narrow range of 7.35 to 7.45" (Grokipedia, Acidosis); Wikipedia's body-fluid table: 7.34-7.45. Below 7.35 doctors call it acidosis (acidemia), and "below pH 7.2, mortality rises sharply" (Grokipedia, Acidosis).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/PH ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Acidosis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH

F9. Stomach juice (gastric acid) is mainly hydrochloric acid, made by the parietal cells of the stomach lining, at "a pH typically ranging from 1.5 to 3.5" (Grokipedia, Gastric acid); Wikipedia: "In humans, the pH is between one and three."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gastric_acid ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastric_acid

F10. How the stomach wall survives its own acid: the mucus layer "forms a viscous, adherent barrier", and surface cells secrete bicarbonate, so the pH "transitions from approximately pH 2 in the acidic lumen to near-neutral pH 7 at the epithelial surface" (Grokipedia, Gastric acid). Wikipedia: mucus is "a viscous barrier to prevent gastric acid from damaging the stomach", and other stomach cells "produce bicarbonate, a base, to buffer the fluid." Further on, in the duodenum, bicarbonate from the pancreas neutralises the acid, "raising the duodenal pH to 6–7" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gastric_acid ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastric_acid

F11. Acid rain: unpolluted rain has a "natural pH of approximately 5.6" (Grokipedia; USGS: "Normal rainfall has a pH of about 5.6"); acid rain is rain below that (Grokipedia: "a pH below 5.6"; Wikipedia: "a pH less than 5"). Its main causes are "sulfur dioxide (SO2) and nitrogen oxides (NOx)", which form "sulfuric and nitric acids" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: the worst-hit regions "all burn sulfur-containing coal to generate heat and electricity." Heavily hit areas: "pH can drop to 4.2–4.4 or lower"; acid rain brought "pH levels in Swedish lakes dropping below 5.0" (Grokipedia). Why clean rain sits at 5.6 (carbon dioxide) is left out here by the lead's ruling.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Acid_rain ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/ph-and-water

F12. Acid water and fish: "At pH 5, most fish eggs cannot hatch" (EPA); "At pH lower than 5 most fish eggs will not hatch" and "pH lower than 4 will kill adult fish" (Wikipedia). "Most water, including drinking water, has a neutral pH that exists between 6.5 and 8.5" (Wikipedia). (Grokipedia adds that much of the harm is done by aluminium the acid frees.)
    https://www.epa.gov/acidrain/effects-acid-rain ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Acid_rain

## Indicators

F13. Litmus is a dye made from lichens, chiefly of the genus Roccella, a mix of "10 to 15 different natural dyes"; it turns red in acid "(pH below approximately 4.5)" and blue in base "(pH above approximately 8.3)", with "shades of purple" between; it gives "a visual cue for acidity or alkalinity without precise numerical pH values" (Grokipedia, Litmus). USGS: litmus paper gives "a rough estimate of pH." Chlorine bleaches litmus white, and the effect is "irreversible" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Litmus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/PH_indicator ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/ph-and-water

F14. "Around 1300, the Catalan physician and alchemist Arnaldus de Villa Nova was the first known to use litmus"; the word comes from the Old Norse litmosi, "dye moss" (Grokipedia, Litmus).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Litmus

F15. Red cabbage juice is a home-made indicator. Its pigment is an anthocyanin (Wikipedia, Red cabbage: "a pigment belonging to anthocyanins"). Colours: red or pink at pH 2-4, "purple (pH 5-6), blue (pH 7-8), green (pH 9-10), and yellow (pH 11-12)" (Grokipedia, PH indicator); Wikipedia: "red, pink, or magenta in acids", "purple in neutral solutions (pH ≈7)", "blue to green to yellow in alkaline solutions". The sources differ on where purple ends and blue begins; the reliable core is red in acid, purple near neutral, then blue, green and yellow as bases get stronger.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/PH_indicator ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_cabbage

F16. Why an indicator changes colour: indicators are weak acids or bases "that exist in equilibrium between their protonated" (hydrogen-ion-holding) and unprotonated forms, "each form exhibiting a distinct color"; "The color change in pH indicators arises from structural modifications in the indicator molecule" (Grokipedia, PH indicator). For anthocyanin: in acid "the flavylium cation (AH⁺) predominates, imparting a red hue"; losing protons (hydrogen ions) gives "the purple or blue quinonoidal base"; at high pH it changes to "the yellow chalcone", "sometimes appearing green"; the changes are "reversible color changes in response to pH variations" (Grokipedia, Anthocyanin).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/PH_indicator ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Anthocyanin

F17. Universal indicator is "a mixture of several indicators that can provide a continuous color change over a range of pH values" (Wikipedia); "red or pink in strongly acidic conditions (pH 1–3)", "green at neutral pH 7", "violet or purple in strongly basic solutions" (Grokipedia, PH indicator). A pH meter reads the voltage across a thin glass bulb (USGS).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH ; https://grokipedia.com/page/PH_indicator ; https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/ph-and-water

## Neutralising

F18. In neutralisation an acid and a base react: hydrogen ions from the acid combine with hydroxide ions from the base "to produce water molecules"; equal amounts give "a solution with a pH close to 7"; liming "is widely used to raise the pH of acidic soils", and "Liming lakes with calcium-based compounds mitigates acid rain effects" (Grokipedia, Neutralization (chemistry)). Adding a base to an acid makes it less acidic and moves its pH toward the middle of the scale, and adding acid to a base moves it back (ACS Middle School Chemistry, chapter 6, lesson 9, read in the search summary). (What neutralisation makes, a salt and water, is the T5 unit's, not keyed here.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Neutralization_(chemistry) ; https://www.acs.org/middleschoolchemistry/lessonplans/chapter6/lesson9.html

F19. Antacids are bases (aluminium hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide, calcium carbonate, sodium bicarbonate) that neutralise excess stomach acid, "elevating the stomach's pH from its typical acidic range of 1.5 to 3.5 to a less acidic, near-neutral level of about 4 to 5", with a "pH increase above 3.0 within seconds to minutes" (Grokipedia, Antacid). So an antacid moves the stomach's pH toward 7, not to 7. Calcium carbonate antacids give "rapid, symptomatic relief by chemically neutralizing gastric acid" (Grokipedia, Gastric acid).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Antacid ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gastric_acid

F20. Heartburn is "a burning sensation in the chest, typically occurring behind the breastbone"; it comes when stomach acid flows back up into the gullet (oesophagus), past the lower oesophageal sphincter, normally "a barrier to prevent retrograde flow of stomach contents"; the gullet's lining "lacks the protective bicarbonate secretion and tight junctions of the gastric lining"; antacids "can quickly neutralize stomach acid" (Grokipedia, Heartburn).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Heartburn

F21. Rock that neutralises acid rain: lakes on limestone resist it: "watersheds with carbonate-rich soils or limestone bedrock, which neutralize acids via bicarbonate formation"; "In unbuffered granitic regions, such as parts of the Adirondacks and Scandinavia, acidification exacerbates declines" (Grokipedia, Acid rain). Wikipedia: "Lakes hosted by silicate basement rocks are more acidic than lakes within limestone", "due to buffering effects by carbonate minerals." Limestone is "composed primarily of the mineral calcite" (calcium carbonate), "readily effervesces in dilute acid", and "serves as a key soil amendment to neutralize acidity and raise soil pH" (Grokipedia, Limestone). Granite is made mainly of quartz and feldspars and is prized for its "resistance to weathering and acidic solutions" (Grokipedia, Granite): it resists acid, so it does nothing to use the acid up. Calcium carbonate, limestone's mineral, is also an antacid (F19).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Acid_rain ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Limestone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Granite

## Søren Sørensen and the pH scale (how we know)

F22. Søren Peder Lauritz Sørensen became head of the chemistry department of the Carlsberg Laboratory in Copenhagen in 1901 ("In 1901, Sørensen succeeded Johan G. C. Kjeldahl as head of the chemistry department at the Carlsberg Laboratory", Grokipedia; Museion: head of the Chemical Department "in 1901"); the laboratory was "supported by the beer company of the same name" (Science History Institute). He studied amino acids, proteins and enzymes ("an extensive research programme on amino acids and proteins", Museion; "working on the effect of ion concentration in the analysis of proteins", Science History Institute).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/S._P._L._S%C3%B8rensen ; https://www.museion.ku.dk/blog/100-years-with-ph/ ; https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/soren-sorensen/

F23. In 1909, in "Enzymstudier II" (Enzyme Studies II), Sørensen proposed the pH scale (Grokipedia, S. P. L. Sørensen; Grokipedia, PH: "introduced by Danish biochemist Søren Sørensen in 1909 during research on enzymatic processes at the Carlsberg Laboratory"). Why: "Until Sørensen developed the pH scale, there was no widely accepted way of expressing hydrogen ion concentrations", and he introduced pH "as a convenient way of expressing acidity" (Science History Institute); Wikipedia: "the pH-scale as a simple way of expressing it", the concentration factor being "written in the form of a negative power of 10"; Grokipedia: "to simplify the representation of the wide range of hydrogen ion concentrations." Pure water's hydrogen ions, 10⁻⁷ (0.0000001) moles per litre, become simply 7 (F2). What the letter p stands for is disputed (Wikipedia: "the exact meaning of the letter is still disputed"; Museion: power, potential, Potenz, or arbitrary): not used.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/S._P._L._S%C3%B8rensen ; https://grokipedia.com/page/PH ; https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/soren-sorensen/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S._P._L._S%C3%B8rensen

F24. Sørensen also "developed a practical colourimetric method for determining pH": "Using buffer systems covering the whole pH scale, he was able to produce reference solutions of known pH," and with "a series of organic substances that shift colour depending on acidity", "the pH of an unknown solution could be determined" (Museion). He also measured with a hydrogen electrode (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: "electrometric titration"). His wife, Margrethe Høyrup Sørensen, "served as a key assistant and co-author" (Grokipedia). He was nominated 25 times for a Nobel Prize between 1915 and 1935 and never won (Grokipedia).
    https://www.museion.ku.dk/blog/100-years-with-ph/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S._P._L._S%C3%B8rensen ; https://grokipedia.com/page/S._P._L._S%C3%B8rensen

F25. What came before pH (so pH was neither the first acid test nor the first idea of hydrogen ions): litmus has told acid from base since about 1300 (F14), and Svante Arrhenius argued in his 1884 dissertation that an acid increases the hydrogen ions in water (F3; Grokipedia, PH: pH "emerged as a precursor metric post-Arrhenius's 1887 electrolytic dissociation theory").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Litmus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Acid ; https://grokipedia.com/page/PH

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. The pH scale "can, at first, appear 'back to front'" (RSC, Easson): students expect a higher number to mean more acid.
M2. Acids are thought dangerous and bases safe, though "some bases are even more corrosive" (RSC, Easson).
M3. "Neutral is often inadequately defined as pH 7" (RSC, Easson); students think the products of neutralisation always sit at pH 7 (Schmidt 1995, via RSC); an antacid only moves the stomach toward 7 (F19).
M4. Some students think "a base somehow halts the acidic behaviour of an acid" rather than reacting with it (RSC, Easson).
M5. Strong and weak are mixed up with concentrated and dilute, "the classic confusion" (RSC, Easson). Logarithms "can look scary" (RSC, Easson): reading pH 5 against pH 7 as a small or a twofold change, or counting three numbers (7, 6, 5) instead of two steps, are the natural slips.
M6. Students over-rely on universal indicator, "assuming everything green is pH 7" (RSC, 5 ways to teach neutralisation).
M7. Litmus colours swapped (red for base, blue for acid): the commonest slip with litmus, and pure water "has no ions" (F2 shows it holds a few of each).
