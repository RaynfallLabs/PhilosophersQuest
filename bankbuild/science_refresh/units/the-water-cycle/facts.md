## Facts

F1 The Sun is what drives the water cycle: its heat supplies the energy that evaporates water from oceans, lakes, rivers and wet ground. https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_cycle ("This cycle is driven by solar energy, which powers the primary phase transitions"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Evaporation (evaporation is "Driven mainly by solar radiation"); https://water.usgs.gov/edu/watercycle-kids-int.html ("The sun is what makes the water cycle work."); https://floridascience.squarespace.com/sc5e71-weather-the-water-cycle (grade 5: "The heat of the Sun provides energy to evaporate water from these sources.")

F2 Heat flowing out from Earth's interior is tiny beside the Sun's: about 44-47 terawatts, roughly 0.04 percent (less than a thousandth) of the solar energy Earth absorbs. https://grokipedia.com/page/Earth's_internal_heat_budget ("averages about 44 TW"; "44-47 terawatts (TW)"; "about 0.04% of the absorbed solar radiation"; "less than 0.03% of the solar energy absorbed by the planet")

F3 Gravity does the other half of the work: it pulls precipitation down from clouds and makes water flow downhill over the land. Sunlight lifts water up as vapour; gravity brings it down. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ess2-4-earths-systems (MS-ESS2-4: "the cycling of water through Earth's systems driven by energy from the sun and the force of gravity"; ESS2.C: "Global movements of water and its changes in form are propelled by sunlight and gravity"; "precipitation, as well as downhill flows on land"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Precipitation (precipitation "falls from a cloud", under gravity, to the surface)

F4 The Moon's gravity (with the Sun's, and Earth's spin) raises the ocean tides; it is not what evaporates water (F1, F2). https://grokipedia.com/page/Tide (tides are "caused primarily by the gravitational interactions between the Earth, Moon, and Sun, along with the planet's rotation")

F5 Evaporation is liquid water changing into water vapour, a gas, below the boiling point. Water vapour is invisible. https://grokipedia.com/page/Evaporation ("occurring at temperatures below the liquid's boiling point"); https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/atmosphere/hydro ("Evaporation is the change of state in a substance from a liquid to a gas."); https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_vapor ("Water vapor is the gaseous state of water"; molecules "invisibly dispersed among other atmospheric gases"); https://floridascience.squarespace.com/sc5e71-weather-the-water-cycle ("Evaporation is the change of liquid water to a gas: water vapor.")

F6 Almost all the water that evaporates into the air comes from the oceans (Grokipedia gives about 90 percent of atmospheric water vapour from oceanic sources in one place and over 80 percent of total evaporation in another; the rest comes from lakes, rivers, land and plants). https://water.usgs.gov/edu/watercycle-kids-int.html ("almost all the water that evaporates from liquid to water vapor (which forms clouds) comes from the oceans"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_cycle ("about 90% of atmospheric water vapor from oceanic sources"; oceanic evaporation "over 80% of total evaporation")

F7 Glass lets no liquid through, so water on a glass table cannot soak away through it. https://grokipedia.com/page/Glass ("The impermeability of glass to gases, liquids, and contaminants")

F8 Condensation is water vapour changing back into liquid water. Air cools as it rises (it expands into lower pressure), and its water vapour condenses onto tiny particles as droplets or ice crystals; a cloud is a visible crowd of these droplets or crystals (about 1 to 100 micrometres across), not water vapour. https://grokipedia.com/page/Cloud ("Clouds arise when rising air cools to its dew point"; "a parcel of moist air ascends and expands due to decreasing atmospheric pressure"; "visible aggregate of minute particles of water droplets or ice crystals"; "diameters ranging from approximately 1 to 100 micrometers"); https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/atmosphere/hydro ("Condensation is the process whereby water vapor is changed into a liquid state."); https://floridascience.squarespace.com/sc5e71-weather-the-water-cycle ("Tiny droplets of water will form as water vapor cools and condenses.")

F9 NOAA names the most important processes of the water cycle as evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation and runoff. Transpiration is the evaporation of water from plants, mostly through tiny pores in their leaves. https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/atmosphere/hydro ("Of the many processes involved in the hydrologic cycle, the most important are:" evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, runoff); https://grokipedia.com/page/Transpiration ("Transpiration is the evaporation of water from the aerial parts of plants")

F10 Precipitation is water that falls from a cloud: rain, snow, sleet, hail, drizzle and graupel. Fog and dew are not precipitation, because they form on surfaces rather than falling from clouds. https://grokipedia.com/page/Precipitation ("any product of the condensation of atmospheric water vapor that falls from a cloud"; forms listed: rain, snow, sleet, hail, graupel, drizzle; fog and dew "form directly on surfaces via cooling rather than descent from clouds"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_cycle ("rain, snow, hail, or sleet")

F11 Cloud droplets are too small to fall: rising air holds them up. When droplets collide and join (coalescence), a drop grows until it is heavy enough to fall faster than the updraft, and it falls as precipitation. Millions of cloud droplets go into one raindrop. (In cold clouds, ice crystals also grow and fall; the grade 5 picture of droplets joining into heavier drops is the USGS's own.) https://water.usgs.gov/edu/watercycle-kids-int.html ("When the cloud droplets combine to form heavier cloud drops, it can start to rain, snow, hail"); https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/precipitation-and-water-cycle ("millions of cloud droplets are required to produce a single raindrop"; "If enough collisions occur to produce a droplet with a fall velocity which exceeds the cloud updraft speed"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Precipitation (growth "through processes like collision-coalescence or the Bergeron process" until particles overcome updrafts); https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/atmosphere/hydro ("Precipitation results when tiny condensation particles, through collision and coalescence, grow too large")

F12 Rain falls from clouds as fresh water, even though most evaporated water comes from the salty sea. https://floridascience.squarespace.com/sc5e71-weather-the-water-cycle ("rain falls from clouds as fresh water")

F13 Runoff is rain or melted snow that flows over the land surface instead of soaking into the soil. It happens when rain falls faster than the soil can soak it up, or when the soil is already full. Runoff flows into creeks, rivers, lakes and the oceans, where water collects. https://grokipedia.com/page/Surface_runoff (precipitation "that travels over the land surface without infiltrating into the soil"; when "the rate of precipitation exceeds the soil's infiltration capacity"; channels water "directly into streams, rivers, and oceans"); https://water.usgs.gov/edu/watercycle-kids-int.html ("This runoff water reaches creeks, rivers, lakes, and the oceans, keeping the water cycle going."); https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/atmosphere/hydro ("Runoff occurs when there is excessive precipitation and the ground is saturated")

F14 Three other names for water in the cycle, none of them rain flowing over a hillside: meltwater is fresh water from melting snow and ice; dew is small water droplets that condense on cool surfaces such as grass, usually at night or early morning; spring water is groundwater flowing out of the ground at a spring. https://grokipedia.com/page/Meltwater ("Meltwater is freshwater produced by the melting of snow, ice, glaciers, ice sheets, and other frozen water sources."); https://grokipedia.com/page/Dew ("small water droplets that condense on cool surfaces"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Spring_(hydrology) ("a natural opening in the Earth's surface where groundwater flows out to the land surface or into a body of surface water")

F15 Some rain and melted snow soaks (infiltrates) into the ground and sinks down to become groundwater, which fills the pore spaces, cracks and fractures in soil, sediment and rock. https://grokipedia.com/page/Groundwater (water in "saturated zones beneath the land surface", filling "pore spaces, cracks, and fractures"; recharged mainly by rain and snowmelt that soaks in); https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_cycle ("Infiltration occurs when water seeps into the ground through soil pores."); https://water.usgs.gov/edu/watercycle-kids-int.html ("the water sinks (infiltrates) into the ground")

F16 Groundwater does not stay put. It moves slowly, typically centimetres to metres a year, and seeps sideways and upwards into rivers, lakes, wetlands and the oceans, and out at springs, so it rejoins the rest of the cycle. Shallow groundwater may stay underground for years to decades; deep groundwater for thousands of years or longer. https://grokipedia.com/page/Groundwater ("typically at velocities on the order of centimeters to meters per year"; "Discharge manifests as baseflow to perennial streams and rivers"; also springs, seeps and seepage to lakes, wetlands and oceans; residence "years to decades" in shallow aquifers, "millennia to over a million years" in deep confined ones); https://water.usgs.gov/edu/watercycle-kids-int.html ("Water seeps upwards and sideways, too, from the ground into the bottom of rivers, lakes, and the oceans."); https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/follow-a-drip-through-water-cycle (a drip's trip underground "might end up taking tens of thousands of years until it finds its way back out of the ground")

F17 The total amount of water on Earth stays nearly the same: the water cycle moves water from place to place and from one form to another, and the same water that was on Earth millions of years ago is still here, recycled over and over. https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_cycle ("the total volume of water on Earth remains nearly constant at approximately 1.386 billion cubic kilometers"); https://webharvest.gov/peth04/20041014212930/http://ga.water.usgs.gov/edu/earthwherewater.html (USGS, archived 2004: "the same water that existed on Earth millions of years ago is still here"; "the same water is continually being recycled all around the globe"; "the Earth neither, as a whole, gains nor loses much matter, including water")

F18 Water vapour stays in the air for only about 8 to 10 days on average before it falls as precipitation. https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_vapor ("The average residence time of water vapor molecules in the atmosphere" "spans 8 to 10 days"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_cycle ("global average residence time of 8-10 days")

F19 The ocean covers about 71 percent of Earth's surface, and its average depth is about 3,682 metres. https://grokipedia.com/page/Ocean ("covering about 71 percent of Earth's surface"; average depth "3,682 meters (12,080 feet)")

F20 About 97 percent of all Earth's water is in the ocean (96.5 percent in the finer figure), and ocean water is salty, about 35 grams of salts in every litre. https://grokipedia.com/page/Ocean (holds 97 percent of the planet's water; 96.5 percent later; about 35 parts per thousand); https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_cycle ("Oceans constitute about 96.5% of this total"); https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/oceanwater.html ("About 97 percent of Earth's water is in the ocean."); https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/where-earths-water ("over 96% of all of Earth's water exists in the oceans"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ess2-2-earths-systems ("Nearly all of Earth's available water is in the ocean.")

F21 Only about 2.5 percent of Earth's water is fresh. Of that fresh water, about 69 percent (68.7) is frozen in glaciers and ice caps and about 30 percent (30.1) is groundwater; lakes and rivers hold only a tiny fraction (all fresh surface water is a little over 1.2 percent of fresh water, and lakes and rivers are a part of that). https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/where-earths-water ("only 2.5% of Earth's water is freshwater"; "over 68 percent is locked up in ice and glaciers"; "Another 30 percent of freshwater is in the ground"; "Only a little more than 1.2% of all freshwater is surface water"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Water_cycle ("freshwater accounts for only 2.5%"; "Glaciers and ice caps hold the largest share of freshwater at approximately 68.7%"; "Groundwater represents about 30.1% of freshwater"); https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ess2-2-earths-systems ("Most fresh water is in glaciers or underground; only a tiny fraction is in streams, lakes, wetlands, and the atmosphere.")

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Evaporated water has vanished, has been taken by the Sun, or has soaked through the container. The SPACE project's children said the water had "dried up, gone, or disappeared", that "the sun took it away", and that "it leaked through the saucer". https://arbs.nzcer.org.nz/node/7340 (NZCER, "Salient points from the literature about understanding the water cycle")

M2 Clouds are bags of water that rip open when they collide, or sponges holding drops; rain comes from clouds bumping together or bursting. NSTA, citing Bar (1986) in Driver et al. (1994): at ages 5-7 "clouds were often described as bags of water kept high in the sky" and "when they collide, they rip open and the water falls out"; "At ages 7-10, some students visualize a cloud as a sponge that has drops of water in it." NZCER: rain explained as "clouds bumping into each other or bursting". https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/what-are-clouds-made; https://arbs.nzcer.org.nz/node/7340

M3 Clouds are made of water evaporated from puddles (common at ages 9-10), or of vapour from kettles or the sea boiling; clouds are filled with sea water. https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/what-are-clouds-made; https://arbs.nzcer.org.nz/node/7340 (not used as a choice; noted for the context)

M4 Heat from inside Earth (volcanoes, hot springs) or the Moon's pull powers evaporation; gravity is what lifts water up. Author's judgment; no survey found. Corrected by F1-F4.

M5 Most fresh water is in lakes and rivers, the fresh water people see. Author's judgment; corrected by F21 (lakes and rivers are a tiny fraction).

M6 Because oceans cover about 71 percent of the surface, they hold about three quarters of the water. Author's judgment; corrected by F19, F20.

M7 Earth's water is used up (or new water is made) as it goes round; evaporated or soaked-in water is gone. Author's judgment, related to M1; corrected by F17.

M8 Groundwater stays in one place for ever, or is a separate water that is not part of the cycle. Author's judgment; corrected by F16.

## Notes and cautions

- Say the Sun supplies "nearly all" the energy: NOAA notes the energy for evaporation can come from other sources too (the atmosphere, the Earth), but Earth's internal heat is about 0.04 percent of the Sun's (F2).
- Do not say every evaporated drop comes from the ocean: plants (transpiration) and land add some (F6, F9).
- Do not offer "underground" as a wrong answer for where most fresh water is: NGSS teaches "in glaciers or underground", and groundwater is the second store (about 30 percent, F21).
- Lakes and rivers hold only a tiny fraction of fresh water; do not write "about 1 percent" for lakes and rivers alone (the 1.2 percent is all fresh surface water, F21).
- The droplets-join picture of rain (F11) is the warm-cloud process; cold clouds also make rain from ice crystals that grow, fall and melt. Do not offer a freezing or melting cloud as a wrong choice.
- "About the same amount of water" (F17) is nearly constant, not exactly: say "about the same", never "exactly".

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F22 Boiling is a rapid change from liquid to gas that happens once the liquid is heated to its boiling point, with vapour bubbles forming throughout the liquid; evaporation happens only at the surface and below the boiling point. https://grokipedia.com/page/Boiling ("Boiling is the physical process by which a liquid undergoes a rapid phase transition to a gas"; "occurring when the liquid is heated to its boiling point"; "This phase change process occurs throughout the bulk of the liquid once the boiling point is reached"; "evaporation is a surface-only phenomenon that proceeds at any temperature below the boiling point")

F23 Mist is a visible crowd of tiny drops of liquid water hanging in the air, made when water vapour condenses; water vapour itself, the gas, is invisible (F5). So water that left as tiny drops would be seen as a mist, as a cloud is seen (F8). https://grokipedia.com/page/Mist ("consists primarily of tiny suspended liquid water droplets in the atmosphere"; "a visible aggregate of minute water droplets suspended in the air near the Earth's surface"; formed by "the condensation of water vapor into tiny droplets")

F24 Thunder is the sound of lightning, made by the sudden heating and expansion of air along the lightning channel; it does not make rain fall. Many clouds rain or snow steadily with no thunder at all: nimbostratus gives "continuous, moderate precipitation" with no lightning (a nimbostratus-like layer with lightning is reclassed as cumulonimbus). Wind moves clouds but does not start rain: rain begins when drops grow heavy enough to fall faster than the updraft (F11). https://grokipedia.com/page/Thunder ("Thunder is the audible result of a lightning discharge"; "produced by the rapid heating and explosive expansion of air along the lightning channel"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Nimbostratus_cloud ("produce continuous, moderate precipitation such as rain or snow, typically reaching the ground"; "persistent, uniform drizzle or rain without dramatic convective features")

F25 Paved ground makes the most runoff. Water cannot soak through streets, pavement, driveways, roofs and parking lots, so it runs off into storm sewers instead of seeping into the ground, while much of the rain on forested land is absorbed into the soil (USGS). Impervious urban surfaces send 50 to 95 percent of rain into runoff, against 10 to 30 percent for undeveloped permeable soils; of natural ground, bare soil typically produces the highest runoff, and tillage and machinery reduce how well soil soaks water up; dense plant cover cuts runoff by 50 to 90 percent compared with bare soil, and leaf litter by about 25 to 55 percent (Grokipedia). https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/impervious-surfaces-and-flooding ("no longer seeps into the ground, but now runs off into storm sewers"; development brings "roads, buildings, housing developments, and parking lots"; "A significant portion of rainfall in forested watersheds is absorbed into soils"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Surface_runoff ("runoff coefficients (C) of 0.5 to 0.95, far exceeding the 0.1 to 0.3 values for undeveloped permeable soils"; "Bare soil typically produces the highest runoff"; "Tillage and machinery compaction reduce soil infiltration capacity"; "dense plant cover can reduce runoff by up to 50-90% compared to bare soil"; leaf litter "Reduces runoff by approximately 25–55%")
