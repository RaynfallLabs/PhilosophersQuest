# Roots, Stems, Leaves and Flowers: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-25. Grokipedia first; Wikipedia, NSTA, USDA Forest Service, the US Botanic Garden and others to corroborate.
Lane: photosynthesis itself (what a leaf takes in and makes, chlorophyll's work, oxygen given off, where a plant's mass comes from) is the unit how-plants-make-food. This sheet keeps those facts only as background for wrong choices and contexts.

## Facts

F1 Roots anchor a plant firmly in the soil. https://grokipedia.com/page/Root ("Roots provide essential mechanical support to plants, anchoring them firmly in the soil")

F2 Roots take in water and dissolved minerals (nutrients) from the soil. https://grokipedia.com/page/Root ("A primary role of roots is the absorption of water and essential nutrients from the soil"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_hair ("the active uptake of water and minerals through root hairs")

F3 Root hairs are fine outgrowths of single cells of the root's skin, growing a little behind the root tip; they give the root a far larger surface for taking in water and minerals. https://grokipedia.com/page/Root ("Root hairs, fine extensions of epidermal cells, dramatically increase the root's absorptive surface area"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_hair ("found only in the zone of maturation"; "increasing root surface area to volume ratio")

F4 Some roots also store food (carrots are storage roots). So "store food" is never used as a wrong choice for roots. https://grokipedia.com/page/Root ("Roots also serve as storage organs"; "True storage roots, such as those in carrots")

F5 Leaves are a plant's main food-making part (the main organ of photosynthesis); roots are not where the food is made. https://grokipedia.com/page/Leaf ("the primary photosynthetic organ of vascular plants"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Root (contrasts roots with "the aerial, photosynthetic focus of shoots")

F6 The stem holds up the leaves, flowers and fruits, placing the leaves in the light, and carries water, minerals and food between the roots and the rest of the plant. A tree's trunk and branches are woody stems. https://grokipedia.com/page/Plant_stem ("mechanical support for aerial organs like leaves and flowers"; "the elevation and positioning of leaves, flowers, and fruits for optimal light capture"; "the transport of water, nutrients, and photosynthates between roots and other organs"; woody stems form "durable trunks and branches in trees like oaks")

F7 Xylem is the set of tiny tubes that carries water and dissolved minerals up from the roots to the stem, leaves and flowers. https://grokipedia.com/page/Xylem ("unidirectional transport of water and dissolved minerals from roots to shoots and leaves"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Plant_stem ("xylem for water transport and phloem for nutrient distribution"); https://www.scienceworld.ca/resource/water-move/ ("millions of tiny tubes called xylem")

F8 Phloem is the other set of tubes: it carries the sugar made in the leaves to the roots and other parts of the plant, and it can carry it in either direction. In a tree, phloem forms the inner bark. https://grokipedia.com/page/Phloem ("primarily sugars synthesized during photosynthesis"; "from source organs such as leaves to sink organs like roots"; "bidirectional transport"; "forms the inner bark in woody plants")

F9 The classroom dye test: a white carnation (or celery) stood in water coloured with dye shows the colour in its stem and petals within hours to a day or so, because the coloured water rises through the xylem. https://www.scienceworld.ca/resource/water-move/ (white carnation in coloured water; results "after a few hours", full effect "overnight"); https://kansasdiscovery.org/mothers-day-steam-colorful-carnations/ (white carnations in coloured water; the xylem carries it up and tints the petals)

F10 Stomata (one is a stoma; the word is Greek for "mouth") are microscopic pores in a leaf's skin, usually more of them on the underside. Each pore sits between two kidney-shaped (bean-shaped) guard cells that open and close it. https://grokipedia.com/page/Stoma ("a microscopic pore located in the epidermis of leaves"; "surrounded by two kidney-shaped guard cells that control its aperture"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoma ("from Greek στόμα, 'mouth'"; "more stomata on the lower surface of the leaves than the upper surface")

F11 Gases pass in and out through the stomata: carbon dioxide comes in, and oxygen and water vapour go out (losing water vapour this way is transpiration). Stomata close in drought to save water. https://grokipedia.com/page/Stoma (carbon dioxide in; oxygen and water vapour out; "close during drought to conserve water"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoma ("passes through stomata by gaseous diffusion"; "Water vapour diffuses through the stomata into the atmosphere as part of a process called transpiration")

F12 A leaf's outer skin (epidermis) is usually transparent, so sunlight shines straight through it to the cells inside; light needs no pore. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidermis_(botany) ("it is usually transparent")

F13 Chlorophyll is the green colouring (pigment) in a plant's cells that captures light for making food; it is a substance, not a tube or a pore. https://grokipedia.com/page/Chlorophyll ("green tetrapyrrole pigments essential for photosynthesis"; "Located in chloroplasts")

F14 Flowers are the parts of a flowering plant that make seeds (its reproductive parts). Colourful petals, scents and nectar draw in the animals that carry pollen. https://grokipedia.com/page/Flower (flowers "enable sexual reproduction through pollination"; "colorful petals, scents, and nectar-producing nectaries"); US Botanic Garden lesson "Flower to Seed, Seed to Flower", grades 3-5, https://usbg.gov/sites/default/files/attachments/flower_to_seed_seed_to_flower_3rd-5th_usbotanicgarden.pdf ("Flowers are special structures that help flowering plants reproduce")

F15 Pollen is a fine powder made in the anthers of a flower's stamens. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollen ("Pollen is a powdery substance produced by most types of flowers of seed plants"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Pollen ("microscopic grains produced by the anthers of seed plants")

F16 Pollination is the moving of pollen from the anther of a flower to the stigma of the same or another flower. Seeds can form only after pollen reaches a flower of the same kind. https://grokipedia.com/page/Pollination ("Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther of a flower to the stigma of the same or another flower"; "successful pollination is a prerequisite for seed and fruit formation"); https://www.fs.usda.gov/wildflowers/pollinators/What_is_Pollination/index.shtml ("Seeds can only be produced when pollen is transferred between flowers of the same species")

F17 Pollen is carried by insects (bees, butterflies), birds, bats and other animals, and by the wind; pollen sticks to a visitor's body. Grasses and conifers are pollinated by the wind. https://www.fs.usda.gov/wildflowers/pollinators/What_is_Pollination/index.shtml ("wind, water, birds, insects, butterflies, bats"; "when pollen grains attach themselves to the animal's body"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Pollination ("Insects dominate these interactions"; wind pollination "prevails in gymnosperms like conifers as well as open-habitat angiosperms including grasses"); US Botanic Garden grades 3-5 lesson ("pollinators (bees, butterflies, etc.) unintentionally move pollen amongst flowers")

F18 After pollination, the flower's ovary grows into a fruit, and the ovules inside it become the seeds. A fruit's job is to hold and protect the seeds and to help spread them. https://grokipedia.com/page/Fruit ("a fruit is the mature ovary of a flowering plant (angiosperm) that develops after fertilization"; "This structure serves to protect the seeds and aid in their dispersal"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Flower ("After fertilization, the ovary typically develops into a fruit enclosing the seeds"); US Botanic Garden grades 3-5 lesson ("the ovary develops into a fruit. The ovules inside the ovary develop into seeds inside of the fruit")

F19 To a scientist, dry seed-holders that grow from flowers are fruits too: an acorn is the fruit of the oak, a single seed inside a hard, woody shell; a maple's winged "helicopter" (called a key in Britain) is a winged fruit, a samara. https://grokipedia.com/page/Acorn ("An acorn is the fruit of oak trees in the genus Quercus"; "a single seed enclosed within a hard, woody shell"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Samara_(fruit) (a samara is a dry winged fruit with one seed; maples' are called "helicopter seeds", "whirlybirds" or "keys"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Fruit ("winged samaras in maples")

F20 Pines and other conifers bear their seeds on cones and have no flowers; their seeds are not enclosed in a fruit. So a pine cone holds seeds but is not a fruit. https://grokipedia.com/page/Conifer ("woody cones that bear naked seeds not enclosed in an ovary"; angiosperms, unlike conifers, "produce flowers and enclosed seeds")

F21 Seed dispersal is the spreading of a plant's seeds away from the parent. Landing away from the parent means less competition with the parent and the other seedlings, less risk from seed-eaters and disease gathered near the parent, and a chance to reach new places. https://grokipedia.com/page/Seed_dispersal ("mitigate intraspecific competition for resources"; "lower the risk of predation concentrated near the parent"; "facilitating colonization of new areas"); Oak National Academy, Year 5 "Plant life cycles" ("the way seeds are spread out from their parent plant"), https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-primary-ks2/units/reproduction-and-life-cycles-plants/lessons/plant-life-cycles

F22 Wind spreads some seeds: each dandelion seed hangs from a feathery, parachute-like tuft (a pappus); a maple's winged fruit spins as it falls, which slows it so it drifts farther. https://grokipedia.com/page/Seed_dispersal (dandelion pappus "a feathery, parachute-like crown of bristles"; maple samaras "autorotate to generate lift via spinning motion"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Taraxacum ("a parachute-like structure" that lets the seed travel by wind); https://grokipedia.com/page/Samara_(fruit) (spinning slows the fall and lets it drift farther)

F23 Water spreads some seeds: a coconut floats, thanks to air pockets in its husk, and ocean currents carry it to far shores; its embryo can stay alive in seawater for about 110 to 120 days. https://grokipedia.com/page/Coconut ("buoyancy, derived from air cavities in the husk"; dispersal by "ocean currents"; "viability in seawater for up to 110-120 days"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Seed_dispersal ("buoyant, waterproof seeds like coconuts that float across oceans")

F24 Animals spread seeds in three main ways: hooked burrs (burdock's) catch on fur and ride away; animals eat fleshy fruits, and the seeds pass through them intact and drop out in their droppings; squirrels and jays bury acorns to eat later and forget many, which sprout. https://grokipedia.com/page/Seed_dispersal ("external attachment via hooks or burs"; seeds "pass through the digestive tract and are excreted intact"; "scatter hoarding (burial by rodents)"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Arctium (burrs "covered in hooked bracts that readily attach to the fur of mammals such as deer and dogs"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Acorn (squirrels' forgotten caches allow "these forgotten acorns to germinate"; blue jays carry acorns up to about 1.9 km)

F25 A seed has three main parts: a tiny young plant (the embryo), a store of food, and a protective seed coat (its skin). https://grokipedia.com/page/Seed ("an embryonic sporophyte plant, nutritive tissue such as endosperm or cotyledons, and a protective seed coat"; the seed coat protects "the embryo from physical damage and pathogen ingress"); US Botanic Garden grades 3-5 lesson (a seed has "a protective coating and a built-in food source")

F26 In a bean, the two thick halves are the seed leaves (cotyledons), packed with food the parent plant stored there; this food feeds the young plant as it sprouts, until it can make its own food in the light. https://grokipedia.com/page/Germination ("the two cotyledons function as primary storage organs"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Seed (dicots "such as beans and peas, rely on enlarged cotyledons for stored nutrients"); https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/needs-seeds-0 ("This food was made by the parent plant and stored in seeds for later use by the embryo"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seedling ("the seedling lives off the energy reserves stored in the seed" until it reaches the light). Caution: in some beans the cotyledons rise above ground and turn green and make some food (US Botanic Garden lesson), so "make food from sunlight" is never a wrong choice for them; and the root does not grow from them.

F27 Germination is a seed's sprouting into a young plant. The seed first takes in water through its coat and swells. Seeds need water, air (oxygen) and suitable warmth; most do not need light or soil (a few small seeds, such as lettuce, do need light). https://grokipedia.com/page/Germination ("the seed absorbs water through its coat, initiating imbibition, which swells and softens the seed coat"; "germinating seeds rely on aerobic respiration"; light requirement "especially pronounced in very small-seeded species"); https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/needs-seeds-0 ("Sunlight is not needed by most seeds"; "Seeds can sprout without soil as long as they have a source of moisture")

F28 The first part to come out of a sprouting seed is the young root (the radicle); it grows down into the soil and becomes the plant's root. The shoot, the young stem with its first leaves, grows up afterwards. https://grokipedia.com/page/Germination ("the radicle (primary root) protrudes first through the seed coat"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radicle ("the first part of a seedling to emerge from the seed"; "grows downward in the soil"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Root (a root "typically originates from the radicle of the seed embryo"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seedling (the plumule grows up through the soil)

F29 A fruit forms after its flower has been pollinated, so a berry is not where pollen is made or collected; pollen comes from flowers. https://grokipedia.com/page/Fruit (fruit "develops after fertilization"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Pollen (made by the anthers); https://grokipedia.com/page/Flower

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Plants get their food from the soil through their roots. Listed in "Common Misconceptions about Plants" (Ohio State University, Beyond Penguins and Polar Bears, 2009): "Plants get their energy from the soil through roots"; "Plants need 'plant food' to eat". https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290

M2 Leaves take in water. Same OSU list: "Leaves take in water." Used only as background; no choice here says a leaf drinks rain (leaves can take up a little water, so such a choice would be partly true).

M3 Pollination and seed dispersal mixed up. Oak National Academy (Year 5): pupils may confuse the two because "both involve something from the flower being moved from one place to another." (URL under F21.)

M4 Seeds need sunlight (or soil) to sprout. NSTA's "Needs of Seeds" probe (grades 3-8) is written to surface it (F27).

M5 A fruit must be sweet and soft, so acorns and maple helicopters are "seeds", not fruits. Author's judgment; many children's materials do call an acorn a seed, so no question here offers "a seed" against "a fruit" for an acorn.

M6 A sprouting seed sends up its shoot and leaves first. Author's judgment (no survey found); corrected by F28.

M7 The two halves of a bean seed hold water or air for the young plant, or grow into its roots. Author's judgment (no survey found); corrected by F26-F28. "Protect it" is NOT used as a wrong choice: the halves fold round the young shoot, so a careful reader could defend it, though the seed coat is the protector (F25).

M8 Xylem and phloem swapped (phloem for water). Author's judgment; the two are taught as a pair at this grade (G1).

## Notes and cautions

- Never offer "store food" as a wrong job for roots (F4), "let gases in and out" as a wrong job for roots (roots take in oxygen from air in the soil; Grokipedia Root mentions oxygen reaching submerged roots), or "make food from sunlight" as a wrong job for a bean's seed leaves (F26).
- Leaf and petal veins contain xylem and phloem (Grokipedia Leaf: "xylem and phloem tissues arranged in veins"), so "veins" is never a wrong choice for the water tubes.
- Common beans and broad beans germinate differently (Wikipedia Seedling: the broad bean keeps its cotyledons below ground); no question depends on where the seed leaves end up.
- Dye-test timing differs by source (a few hours, overnight, a few days): say "a day later" at most.
- Pollen colour: no source read today states it, so stems say "fine dust" or "powder", not "yellow".

## Revision facts (2026-11-26, the one revision round)

F30 In a sprouting seed the young root (radicle) comes out first, and the shoot and the seed leaves (cotyledons) follow it; so neither the stem, the leaves nor the seed leaves lead the way. https://grokipedia.com/page/Germination ("the radicle (primary root) protrudes first through the seed coat, followed by the shoot and cotyledons (seed leaves)")

F31 A fleshy fruit's food is there to tempt the animals that spread its seeds, not stored for the parent plant: a fruit's jobs are to protect the seeds and help spread them, and plants "invest in attractive structures such as colorful, nutrient-rich fruits ... to entice dispersers". https://grokipedia.com/page/Seed_dispersal; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fruit ("This structure serves to protect the seeds and aid in their dispersal"; "fleshy fruits attract vertebrates and invertebrates that ingest or carry seeds")
