## The fossil record's order

F1. The fossil record is "the collection of fossils and their placement in chronological order" (NGSS LS4.A). Simpler one-celled and sea-invertebrate forms "predominate in older Precambrian and early Paleozoic strata"; jawless fish appear in the Ordovician (about 485-443 million years ago), four-legged land animals in the Devonian (about 419-358 million years ago), and synapsids "precede therapsids and mammals"; the Precambrian ends and the Cambrian begins about 541 million years ago ("Cambrian trilobites starting ~541 million years ago"). The record shows "an orderly progression from ancestral to derived forms without anachronisms" (Grokipedia, Evidence of common descent).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Evidence_of_common_descent ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/dci-arrangement/ms-ls4-biological-evolution-unity-and-diversity

F2. Dates of first appearance: the earliest well-preserved stromatolites, built by microbes, are about 3.5 billion years old; animals with backbones appear about 490 million years ago, the earliest mammals about 225 million years ago, and lagomorphs (rabbits and hares) about 55 million years ago. Mammals appear only in rock younger than the first fish.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Earliest_known_life_forms ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Precambrian_rabbit ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Evidence_of_common_descent

F3. "No advanced mammalian fossils, such as rabbits, occur in Precambrian rocks" (Grokipedia). J. B. S. Haldane "reportedly responded" that "fossil rabbits in the Precambrian" would overturn his confidence in evolution; the story is anecdotal ("no direct record in Haldane's extensive publications"), was popularised by Richard Dawkins in The Greatest Show on Earth (2009), and fits Karl Popper's test of falsifiability. "No authenticated Precambrian rabbit fossils have emerged."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Precambrian_rabbit ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Evidence_of_common_descent

F4. Evolution "produces a tree, not a ladder" (UC Berkeley, Understanding Evolution): an older group goes on living beside the groups that descend from it, so fish, older than mammals in the record, still live today. A living or tip taxon is "the cousin" of the others, "not their ancestor".
    https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/

## Transitional fossils: Tiktaalik

F5. Tiktaalik roseae was found in 2004 on Ellesmere Island, Nunavut, in Arctic Canada, by a team led by Neil Shubin, Edward (Ted) Daeschler and Farish Jenkins Jr., and described in Nature in 2006. It is about 375 million years old (Late Devonian), from the Fram Formation, "a fluvial deposit consisting of ancient river and stream sediments". The team's Arctic expeditions ran from 1999 or 2000 (sources differ); the 2006 UChicago release speaks of "four summers of exploration".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tiktaalik ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiktaalik ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Neil_Shubin ; https://www.uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/news/2006/april/newly-found-species-fills-evolutionary-gap-between-fish-and-land-animals

F6. The find was a prediction kept. Fish with fleshy, lobed fins such as Panderichthys were known from fossils 380 million years old, and the early four-legged animals Acanthostega and Ichthyostega from fossils about 365 million years old (Wikipedia). "Previous research suggested that vertebrates' invasion of land took place about 375 million years ago in a river," so the team "searched for fossils in 375 million year old rocks that had preserved a river delta ecosystem"; "The paleontologists knew what sort of animal they were looking for," and Tiktaalik "had the set of characteristics that they had expected" (Berkeley). Daeschler: "We knew that the rocks on Ellesmere Island offered a glimpse into the right time period."
    https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolibrary/news/060501_tiktaalik ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiktaalik ; https://www.uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/news/2006/april/newly-found-species-fills-evolutionary-gap-between-fish-and-land-animals

F7. Tiktaalik is "technically a fish, complete with scales and gills", with fins edged by thin bony rays (lepidotrichia), "However, it also had the sturdy wrist bones, neck, shoulders, and thick ribs of a four-legged vertebrate" (Berkeley). It was "the earliest-known fish to have a neck"; its skull was low and flat with the eyes on top; it had a lung-like organ. Inside its front fin are a humerus, a radius and an ulna "homologous to the tetrapod forelimb", and wrist-like bones (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tiktaalik ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiktaalik ; https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolibrary/news/060501_tiktaalik

F7b. Reptiles come far later than Tiktaalik: Hylonomus, "the oldest undisputed reptile body fossil", a small lizard-like animal, is about 315 million years old (312-319; a Scottish specimen of about 338 million years may be older).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hylonomus

F8. Tiktaalik "is more accurately described as a transitional form than a missing link" (Berkeley). Trackways from Zachelmie, Poland, older than Tiktaalik (Grokipedia about 395 million years, about 18 million years older; Wikipedia about 12 million years older), are "largely accepted" as tetrapod tracks though a 2015 reanalysis disputed them, so Tiktaalik is best read as a late member of the transitional group, not the walkers' direct ancestor.
    https://evolution.berkeley.edu/evolibrary/news/060501_tiktaalik ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tiktaalik ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiktaalik

## Transitional fossils: Archaeopteryx

F9. The first skeleton of Archaeopteryx (the London specimen) was unearthed in 1861 near Langenaltheim, Bavaria, Germany, in Solnhofen limestone, "just two years after Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species". It is about 150 million years old (Late Jurassic). It had flight feathers, wings and a wishbone, like a bird, but also "jaws with sharp teeth, three fingers with claws, a long bony tail" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: conical teeth in sockets, a long bony tail of at least 20 vertebrae, three clawed digits on each forelimb). Thomas Henry Huxley in 1868 read it as evidence for Darwin's theory; Richard Owen insisted it was a true bird.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Archaeopteryx ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeopteryx

F10. Birds descend from theropod dinosaurs: in a February 1868 lecture Huxley argued that Archaeopteryx's "skeletal resemblances to theropod dinosaurs" showed birds descended from a dinosaur-like reptile stock (Grokipedia, Origin of birds). Archaeopteryx shares features with the small dromaeosaurids and troodontids (Wikipedia). "Theropods were predominantly carnivorous", and "Modern birds (Aves) represent the sole surviving lineage of Theropoda"; the long-necked plant-eating sauropodomorphs are a separate saurischian branch (Grokipedia, Theropoda). The first dinosaurs appear about 235 (or 233) million years ago, in the Late Triassic (Grokipedia, Dinosaur).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Origin_of_birds ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Theropoda ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dinosaur ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeopteryx

F11. Pterosaurs were "not dinosaurs but fellow ornithodirans"; their wing was a membrane "stretched between an elongated fourth finger and the body"; early pterosaurs had teeth and long tails. The ancestors of modern birds were "smaller, bird-like dinosaurs" (Grokipedia, Pterosaur). Flight evolved independently in insects, pterosaurs, birds and bats, and "none of these groups share a recent common flying ancestor" (Grokipedia, Convergent evolution). The bat fossil record "begins abruptly in the early Eocene epoch, approximately 52.5 million years ago" (Grokipedia, Bat), long after Archaeopteryx.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pterosaur ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Convergent_evolution ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Bat

## Transitional fossils: whales with legs

F12. Pakicetus, described by Philip Gingerich and Donald Russell in 1981 from northern Pakistan, lived about 50 million years ago (early Eocene). It was a wolf-sized mammal (about 1-2 m) with four weight-bearing legs suited to running on land; it may have waded in shallows, with "no evidence of sustained swimming" (Grokipedia). Pakicetids are counted as whales mainly by the ear (the auditory bulla), and are "the earliest known true cetaceans" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia, Evolution of cetaceans).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pakicetus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Evolution_of_cetaceans ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_cetaceans

F13. Ambulocetus natans, described by J. G. M. Thewissen and colleagues in 1994 from northern Pakistan, lived about 48 million years ago (Grokipedia; Wikipedia gives about 49). Its name means "walking whale that swims". It was about 3-3.5 m long, with large webbed hind feet that worked as paddles; it moved awkwardly on land and swam by paddling with its hind feet and undulating its spine. It sits "between the more land-based Pakicetus and later, more fully aquatic whales".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ambulocetus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_cetaceans

F14. Basilosaurus lived about 40-34 million years ago (late Eocene); basilosaurids were "fully recognizable whales which lived entirely in the ocean" (Wikipedia). A 16-metre Basilosaurus isis "had 35-centimeter-long (14 in) hind limbs with fused tarsals and only three digits" (Wikipedia, Basilosaurus); its hind limbs "couldn't bear weight and so weren't used in walking" and "were not attached to its backbone" (University of Michigan Museum of Paleontology). Gingerich, Smith and Simons (Science, 1990) described the foot bones and proposed that the legs served as guides in mating. Richard Harlan named it "king lizard" in 1834, taking it for a reptile.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Basilosaurus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilosaurus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_cetaceans ; https://prod.lsa.umich.edu/paleontology/resources/beyond-exhibits/basilosaurus-isis.html ; https://www.scilit.net/publications/2d3726fbb1333fc5d1dc9ac63988ee2e

F15. In age the whale fossils run Pakicetus (about 50 million years), Ambulocetus (about 48-49), later whales such as Rodhocetus (about 45-47) and the basilosaurids (about 41-34): from four legs on land, to paddling feet in shallow water, to a life wholly at sea with tiny hind legs. Whales sit inside the even-toed hoofed mammals, with hippopotamuses as their closest living relatives.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_cetaceans ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Evolution_of_cetaceans

## Homologous and analogous structures

F16. Richard Owen "coined the term 'homology'" in 1843 (Grokipedia), defining a homologue as the "same organ in different animals under every variety of form and function" and contrasting it with analogy, a likeness of function (Wikipedia). In his 1848 report on the vertebrate archetype he described a "hypothetical, generalized skeleton", every real skeleton a modification of it, and took the archetype as "the idea ... answering to [the principle] of the divine intelligence"; he rejected transmutation (Grokipedia, Richard Owen).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Homology_(biology) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_(biology) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Richard_Owen

F17. The forelimbs of "humans, bats, whales, and horses" keep "a core skeletal blueprint of one proximal humerus, paired distal radius and ulna, a series of carpals and radiating metacarpals with phalanges forming digits", modified as "elongation and membrane support in bat wings for flight", "hyperphalangy and flattening in whale flippers" and "fusion and reduction in equine hooves" (Grokipedia, Evidence of common descent). Forelimbs of different shape "all share the same set of homologous bones — the humerus, the radius, and the ulna", and "Such homologies reveal the common ancestry of all these animals" (Berkeley). The forelimbs of cats and dogs are a Berkeley example of homology, and the ancestral forelimb became, among others, "the running forelegs of dogs, deer and horses" (Wikipedia, Homology); horses have "a single digit per limb" (Wikipedia, Pentadactyly). "All vertebrate forelimbs are homologous", including dolphin flippers, human arms, horse forelegs and bat and bird wings (Wikipedia, Forelimb). A whale's flipper has "the major skeletal elements of the forelimbs of land mammals" (SeaWorld). The bones in human, dog, bird and whale limbs "all share the same overall construction" (OpenStax).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Evidence_of_common_descent ; https://evolution.berkeley.edu/examples-of-homology/legs-and-limbs ; https://evolution.berkeley.edu/lines-of-evidence/homologies/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_(biology) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentadactyly ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forelimb ; https://seaworld.org/animals/all-about/beluga-whales/characteristics ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/18-1-understanding-evolution

F18. Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1860 edition): "What can be more curious than that the hand of a man, formed for grasping, that of a mole for digging, the leg of the horse, the paddle of the porpoise, and the wing of the bat, should all be constructed on the same pattern, and should include similar bones, in the same relative positions?" and "Nothing can be more hopeless than to attempt to explain this similarity of pattern in members of the same class, by utility or by the doctrine of final causes." Darwin used homologous structures as evidence for common descent with modification (Grokipedia); he explained them as a body plan inherited from a common ancestor (Wikipedia).
    https://darwin-online.org.uk/Variorum/1860/1860-434-dns.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Homology_(biology) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_(biology)

F19. Analogous structures do the same job but arose separately, by convergent evolution; their likeness is "not inherited from a common ancestor" (Grokipedia, Convergent evolution). Insect and bird wings "evolved independently in widely separated groups, and converged functionally to support powered flight, so they are analogous" (Wikipedia); bat and insect wings are Grokipedia's example; "the wing structure and embryonic origin are completely different" in insects and in bats or birds (OpenStax). "The camera-type eyes of vertebrates, such as humans, and cephalopods, like octopuses, exemplify convergent evolution" (Grokipedia). Bird and bat wings are "analogous as wings, but homologous as forelimbs" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Convergent_evolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_(biology) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Homology_(biology) ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/18-1-understanding-evolution

## Similar early embryos

F20. Every vertebrate embryo forms pharyngeal arches, "evolutionarily conserved across vertebrates"; in a human the first arch appears "at the beginning of the fourth week". In fish the arches (branchial arches) "directly support functional gills". In humans the first arch builds the jaw (with Meckel's cartilage contributing to the malleus and incus of the middle ear), the second arch the stapes, and arch derivatives also form the hyoid and the cartilages of the larynx (the throat). The arches link "fish gill supports to mammalian neck structures". Ribs and limbs are not among the arch derivatives.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pharyngeal_arch ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Stapes ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharyngeal_arch

F21. Human embryos do not have functional gills: "The pharyngeal pouches are closed, without perforations or functional slits", and later form the thymus and parathyroid glands (Grokipedia, Recapitulation theory). (Some textbooks loosely call the grooves "gill slits"; OpenStax does.) Haeckel's biogenetic law, "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny", has been "empirically falsified and discarded". Von Baer's laws (1828): general features appear in the embryo before special ones, and "the embryo of a higher form never resembles any other form, but only its embryo".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Recapitulation_theory ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Von_Baer's_laws_(embryology) ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/18-1-understanding-evolution

F22. Ernst Haeckel published embryo drawings in Natürliche Schöpfungsgeschichte (1868) and Anthropogenie (1874), the latter showing fish, salamanders, chickens, rabbits and humans side by side (Grokipedia, Ernst Haeckel). Critics said he exaggerated the likeness: Ludwig Rütimeyer (1868) noted identical woodcuts used for different species; Wilhelm His (1874) accused him of falsifying evidence; Stephen Jay Gould said he "exaggerated the similarities by idealizations and omissions" (Wikipedia). Michael Richardson and colleagues (Anatomy and Embryology, 1997) photographed real embryos from a wide range of vertebrates and found that embryos at the supposedly matching stage vary in form and more than tenfold in size; Haeckel had added or left out features and fudged the scale. Richardson called the drawings "one of the most famous fakes in biology", yet wrote in 1998 that "on a fundamental level, Haeckel was correct": real vertebrate embryos do share pharyngeal arches and pouches and a tail (Grokipedia, Recapitulation theory).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ernst_Haeckel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embryo_drawing ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Recapitulation_theory ; https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s004290050082

F23. Darwin quoted Karl Ernst von Baer (from the 1861 edition of the Origin): "In my possession are two little embryos in spirit, whose names I have omitted to attach, and at present I am quite unable to say to what class they belong. They may be lizards or small birds, or very young mammalia, so complete is the similarity in the mode of formation of the head and trunk in these animals."
    https://darwin-online.org.uk/Variorum/1861/1861-471-dns.html ; https://darwin-online.org.uk/Variorum/1866/1866-519-dns.html

## Vestigial structures

F24. Vestigiality "refers to biological structures that have lost a major ancestral function", and "vestigial traits need not be entirely functionless". Darwin described "rudimentary, atrophied, and aborted organs" inherited from ancestors in which they worked; such structures support common descent by "similarities in position, embryonic development, and basic composition" with working structures in related species (Grokipedia, Vestigiality).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Vestigiality

F25. A modern whale's pelvic bones are small and "lack articulation with the axial skeleton and serve no locomotor function" (not joined to the backbone); they provide "attachment sites for muscles" used in mating (Grokipedia, Vestigiality; Wikipedia: they most likely support the male genitalia). "Hind leg bones in whales" are a textbook vestigial structure (OpenStax). "Limb buds develop normally in cetacean embryos" and then stop growing (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Vestigiality ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_cetaceans ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/18-1-understanding-evolution

F26. A whale's flippers are its front limbs: "Pectoral flippers have the major skeletal elements of the forelimbs of land mammals" (SeaWorld; Wikipedia, Homology: the front flippers of whales evolved from the ancestral forelimb). Its tail flukes "are flattened pads of tough, dense, fibrous connective tissue - completely without bone" (SeaWorld).
    https://seaworld.org/animals/all-about/beluga-whales/characteristics ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_(biology)

F27. The coccyx (tailbone) is "formed of three, four or five rudimentary vertebrae", "the remnant of a vestigial tail" in tailless primates such as humans (Wikipedia). "Between weeks 4 and 8 of gestation, the caudal eminence undergoes regression" (the embryo's tail shrinks away), leaving the coccyx (Grokipedia). It has "attachments to various muscles, tendons and ligaments", including the levator ani and coccygeus of the pelvic floor and the gluteus maximus (Wikipedia, which doubts how important they are); Grokipedia says these help support the pelvic floor and that the coccyx "forms part of a supportive tripod during sitting".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Coccyx ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coccyx

F28. Goosebumps: "small muscles called arrector pili contract at the base of hair follicles", lifting the hairs, in cold or strong emotion. In furry animals raised fur traps a layer of air for insulation and makes the animal look bigger ("a larger silhouette to deter predators", in cats). In humans the reflex is "largely vestigial due to significant body hair loss".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Goose_bumps

F29. The stapes, in the middle ear, is "the smallest and lightest bone in the human body" (about 3 mm); it evolved from the fish's hyomandibula.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Stapes

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Evolution as a ladder: older forms should vanish once newer ones descend from them, or living kinds are one another's ancestors. Berkeley: "a tree, not a ladder"; a tip taxon is "the cousin ... not their ancestor" (F4).
M2. A transitional fossil is a "missing link" or a direct ancestor (Berkeley: Tiktaalik is "more accurately described as a transitional form than a missing link"; F8).
M3. Vestigial means useless (Grokipedia: "need not be entirely functionless"; the old bank's own topic turned on it; F24, F25, F27).
M4. Human embryos have gills, or pass through the adult forms of their ancestors (Haeckel's recapitulation, F21). Textbooks' "gill slits" feeds it.
M5. Things that look alike or do the same job must be closely related (analogy taken for homology, F19).
M6. Birds descend from pterosaurs, the "flying reptiles" (F10, F11).
    https://evolution.berkeley.edu/teach-evolution/misconceptions-about-evolution/

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F30. The first fish are more than 500 million years old: "Fish represent the earliest vertebrates, emerging approximately 530 million years ago"; the Chengjiang fossils such as Haikouichthys are "dated to approximately 518 million years ago"; Myllokunmingia and Metaspriggina "appeared during the Cambrian period around 520–500 million years ago" (Grokipedia, Evolution of fish). This corrects the 490 million years of F2, which the Precambrian_rabbit page gives for "vertebrates".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Evolution_of_fish

F31. The Cambrian began 538.8 million years ago: the International Commission on Stratigraphy "revised the base of the Cambrian Period to 538.8 ± 0.6 Ma", "a revision from the previously estimated 541 Ma" (Grokipedia, Cambrian). So Precambrian rock is more than about 539 million years old. This corrects the 541 of F1.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cambrian

F32. Reptiles come tens of millions of years after Tiktaalik (about 375 million years): a trackway of an amniote with clawed feet, "most probably a reptile", about 350 million years old, from the Mansfield district of Victoria, Australia (Nature, 2025), 35 to 40 million years older than earlier records; the oldest undisputed reptile body fossil, Hylonomus, is about 315 million years old (F7b).
    https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1083842 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hylonomus

F33. Richard Owen did not coin the word: "The word homology, coined in about 1656", and "The term 'homology' was first used in biology by the anatomist Richard Owen in 1843" (Wikipedia, Homology). So Owen gave homology its meaning in biology in 1843.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_(biology)

F34. Theropods were not all small (from the tiny Anchiornis to Tyrannosaurus; fact-check, Grokipedia Theropoda), so "small theropods, the mostly meat-eating dinosaurs". Lizards are not on the bird line: Lepidosauria holds "Squamata (lizards, snakes, and amphisbaenians) alongside the two species of tuatara" (Grokipedia, Lepidosauria), and lepidosaurs are "non-archosaur diapsids"; Archosauria holds the crocodilians and "Dinosauria, Pterosauria, and other bird-line archosaurs", with birds the "descendants of theropod dinosaurs" (Grokipedia, Archosaur).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Theropoda ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lepidosauria ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Archosaur

F35. Pakicetus (Grokipedia): its "initial classification as an early cetacean stemmed from the specimen's dense tympanic bulla", and the involucrum is "a defining feature of cetaceans"; the nasal opening sits "at the rostral tip" and "The nasal passages lack adaptations for a blowhole"; its fossils were "deposited in freshwater fluvial and deltaic environments"; "there is no evidence for sustained swimming", "without evidence of expanded caudal fins for propulsion"; it was "fully terrestrial, quadrupedal", 1 to 2 metres long, about 50 million years ago. Described by Philip Gingerich and Donald Russell in 1981 from northern Pakistan (F12).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pakicetus

F36. All four-legged animals' forelimbs "are all derived from the same ancestral tetrapod structure" (Wikipedia, Homology), so a lizard's and a horse's forelegs are homologous. The "streamlined body shapes, dorsal fins, and flippers of sharks" and of dolphins and ichthyosaurs "evolved independently as adaptations for efficient swimming" (Grokipedia, Convergent evolution); a dolphin's dorsal fin "is made of dense, fibrous connective tissue, with no bone, cartilage, or muscle" (SeaWorld, bottlenose dolphin).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_(biology) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Convergent_evolution ; https://seaworld.org/animals/all-about/bottlenose-dolphin/characteristics/

F37. In jawed fish, "the first arch pair (mandibular arches) develops into the jaw", "the second gill arches (the hyoid arches) develop into the hyomandibular complex", and "the remaining posterior arches (simply called branchial arches) support the gills" (Wikipedia, Branchial arch): most, not all, of a fish's arches carry gills. Human arch derivatives (Grokipedia, Pharyngeal arch): first, mandible, maxilla, malleus, incus; second, stapes, styloid process, lesser horn and upper part of the hyoid; third, lower body and greater horn of the hyoid; fourth and sixth, the thyroid, cricoid and arytenoid cartilages. "The parts of the skull that arise from the pharyngeal arches are collectively known as the splanchnocranium" (Wikipedia, Pharyngeal arch), while "the neurocranium includes the calvaria or skullcap" (Wikipedia, Neurocranium), its frontal bone from neural crest and its parietal bones from paraxial mesoderm (Grokipedia, Neurocranium). The vertebrae come from the sclerotome of the somites, blocks of paraxial mesoderm "along the neural tube" (Grokipedia, Somite). So neither the neck's vertebrae nor the roof of the skull is built by the arches.

Whale flukes: "Flukes are flattened pads of tough, dense, fibrous connective tissue, completely without bone, cartilage, or muscle" (SeaWorld, bottlenose dolphin); "The two triangles of the adult fluke are located lateral to the caudal vertebrae with a notch in the midline" (Gavazzi, Thewissen, Cooper and colleagues, Developmental Dynamics 253, 2024, beluga embryos); the last tail vertebrae run into the fluke region and end just before the notch (Fish 1998, via the fact-check). So the flukes are boneless blades on either side of the tail's backbone.
