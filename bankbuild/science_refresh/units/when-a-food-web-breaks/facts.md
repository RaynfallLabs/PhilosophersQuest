## Robert Paine's sea stars: the keystone species

F1. From June 1963 Robert Paine, of the University of Washington, pried every ochre sea star (Pisaster ochraceus) off a stretch of rock at Mukkaw Bay (also spelt Makah Bay), Washington, about 8 m long and 2 m tall (Nautilus: "a 25-feet long, 6-feet tall stretch of rock"), with a crowbar, and "hurled them out to sea"; he kept the stretch clear year after year. "On an adjacent stretch, he let nature take her course" (Nautilus).
    https://nautil.us/the-ecologist-who-threw-starfish-235831 ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-man-whose-dynasty-changed-ecology/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Keystone_species

F2. What happened on the cleared rock: within three months acorn barnacles covered 60 to 80 percent of the space; by June 1964 goose barnacles and mussels were displacing them, four kinds of algae had largely gone and the two limpet and two chiton species had left; the removal "reduced the diversity of the intertidal community from the original 15 species to eight" (Nautilus). Scientific American: "Within a year, the total number of species had halved." Wikipedia: the pools held 15 rock-clinging species, later 8; ten years later mussels largely dominated. A small predatory snail (Thais emarginata) increased 10- to 20-fold.
    https://nautil.us/the-ecologist-who-threw-starfish-235831 ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-man-whose-dynasty-changed-ecology/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystone_species

F3. The sea star mainly controlled the California mussel (Mytilus californianus), the strongest competitor for space: "When Paine removed the ochre starfish, the mussels quickly outgrew the other species crowding them out" (Wikipedia). Paine's 1966 paper described a rocky shore at Makah Bay where "top predator species help maintain biodiversity" (Wikipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystone_species ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_T._Paine_(zoologist) ; https://nautil.us/the-ecologist-who-threw-starfish-235831

F4. Paine introduced "keystone species" in 1969 ("A Note on Trophic Complexity and Community Stability", The American Naturalist): a species with "a disproportionately large effect on its environment relative to its abundance" (Wikipedia), named after the keystone of an arch, the stone that "locks the structure in place" (Grokipedia). He also coined "trophic cascade" (Grokipedia: in his 1980 Tansley Lecture).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystone_species ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Keystone_species ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_cascade ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_T._Paine_(zoologist)

## Wolves back in Yellowstone

F5. The last wolves officially killed in Yellowstone were two pups in 1926. In 1995, 14 wolves captured near Hinton, Alberta, were released in the Lamar Valley; in 1996, 17 more from Canada (31 in all). The 1996 releases were the last.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_wolves_in_Yellowstone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_cascade

F6. Elk counted on the northern range fell from 16,791 (winter 1995) to 8,335 (winter 2004) while wolves rose from 21 to 106; elk make up up to 92 percent of wolves' winter diet. Other causes also cut the elk: predation by bears, more human hunting, a severe winter and drought (Wikipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_wolves_in_Yellowstone ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_cascade (elk about 19,000-20,000 in the early 1990s, about 6,000 by the 2010s)

F7. Elk browse young willows and aspens. After the wolves returned, aspen browsing rates were "significantly lower in 2012 than in 1997" (Painter et al., via phys.org), young aspens grew taller and willows along some streams grew back (Grokipedia). Beaver colonies on the northern range rose from one in 2001 to nine in 2011; beavers need willow in winter (Wikipedia). NPS: the beaver increase "is likely related to the resurgence in willow since the late 1990s".
    https://phys.org/news/2014-09-aspen-recovering-wildlife-populations-shift.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_cascade ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_wolves_in_Yellowstone ; https://www.nps.gov/yell/learn/nature/beaver.htm

F8. The honest debate. NPS: the causes of the willows' decline and recovery, and whether aspen are recovering, "are topics of intense debate"; 129 beavers were released north of the park from 1986 to 1999, and "Some of the increase likely came from beavers dispersing from the national forest". Brice, Larsen and MacNulty (Ecology Letters) found that measuring only the tallest young aspens overstated regeneration 3 to 44 times compared with random sampling; random sampling still showed a trophic cascade, "but it was weaker". A 2010 study called the cascade "weaker than is often claimed"; Grokipedia lists drought, fire and other predators as confounders.
    https://www.nps.gov/yell/learn/nature/beaver.htm ; https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10377598-sampling-bias-exaggerates-textbook-example-trophic-cascade ; https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.05.05.079459.full.pdf ; https://insideclimatenews.org/?p=102801 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_cascade

F9. Bison in the Lamar Valley grew more plentiful as elk fell: "Their numbers have increased four-fold as elk have decreased" (phys.org on Painter et al.); bison browse willow clumps (Painter and Ripple 2012). So the wolves did not cut the bison.
    https://phys.org/news/2014-09-aspen-recovering-wildlife-populations-shift.html ; https://trophiccascades.forestry.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/Painter_2012ForEco.pdf

## Killer whales, sea otters, urchins and kelp

F10. Sea otters eat sea urchins, and "By preying on urchins, otters prevent overgrazing of giant kelp" (Grokipedia, Keystone species); without otters, urchins convert "kelp forests into urchin barrens" (Grokipedia, Sea otter). The fur trade cut otters to fewer than about 2,000 by 1911 (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: fewer than 1,000 in the north Pacific). Estes and Palmisano compared Amchitka (otters) with Shemya (no otters): Shemya had urchins and no kelp (Nautilus).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sea_otter ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Keystone_species ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystone_species ; https://nautil.us/the-ecologist-who-threw-starfish-235831

F11. Estes, Tinker, Williams and Doak (Science, 16 October 1998): sea otters in western Alaska "declined by about 25 percent each year during the 1990s"; "With seals and sea lions in short supply in the North Pacific, killer whales are now feeding on sea otters." In Kuluk Bay, Adak Island, otters "declined by 76 percent" from 1993 to 1997, while in Clam Lagoon, which killer whales could not reach, they stayed stable. Urchin biomass rose eight-fold in a ten-year resurvey, and "Exploding sea urchin populations have decreased kelp densities by a factor of 12". "As few as four killer whales" could have driven the decline. Estes: "It took about two years for me to become convinced that killer whales were responsible." Grokipedia, Sea otter: otter numbers "fell by over 90%" from the mid-1980s to the early 2000s.
    https://www1.ucsc.edu/oncampus/currents/98-99/10-19/killer.htm ; https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/1008002 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sea_otter

## Cane toads in Australia

F12. In June 1935, 102 cane toads (one died in transit; Grokipedia gives 101 or 102) were brought from Hawaii to Gordonvale, Queensland, to control cane beetles; there is "no evidence that they have affected the number of cane beetles" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: beetles in under 2 percent of stomachs). Native to South and Middle America; "over 200 million" by 2011; the western front advanced about 40 km a year in 1994 and 60 km a year by 2014.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cane_toads_in_Australia ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cane_toads_in_Australia

F13. Toads carry bufadienolide toxins in glands of their skin (the parotoid glands) that can stop a predator's heart. Native predators are not adapted to the toxin, so the toads have no efficient natural predators (Wikipedia). Northern quolls and yellow-spotted monitors fell by more than 90 percent in affected regions (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: yellow-spotted monitors "up to 90%").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cane_toads_in_Australia ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cane_toads_in_Australia

F14. Doody et al. (Ecology 96: 2544-2554, 2015), in the Kimberley: monitor lizards died from eating toads; declines of the water monitor species "approached 50%" over a five-year period spanning the invasion, "apparently causing fledging success of the crimson finch to increase from 55% to 81%". The monitors had been eating the finches' eggs and fledglings, and finch numbers rose as two goanna (monitor) species declined (phys.org 2015). Wikipedia: monitors that ate toads died, linked to "a boom in their usual prey, crimson finches".
    https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/invasive-toads-shift-predator-prey-densities-in-animal-communitie/ ; https://phys.org/news/2015-11-cane-toad-advance-aids-kimberley.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cane_toads_in_Australia

## Rabbits in Australia, and the viruses

F15. Thomas Austin released wild rabbits for hunting at Barwon Park, Victoria, in 1859 (the number differs: Grokipedia 24, Wikipedia at least 13). Their spread was "the fastest spread ever recorded of any mammal anywhere in the world" (Wikipedia). Rabbits overgraze, ringbark and kill young trees and expose topsoil to erosion. Myxomatosis, released in 1950 after Frank Fenner's research, cut them from an estimated 600 million to about 100 million; genetic resistance let them recover to 200-300 million by 1991.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbits_in_Australia ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rabbits_in_Australia

F16. Rabbit haemorrhagic disease virus (RHDV), tested on Wardang Island, South Australia, escaped quarantine in 1995 and killed about 10 million rabbits within two months. Classic RHDV "only affects adult European rabbits" (some later strains hares and cottontails); predators and scavengers can spread it in their faeces but are not named as infected.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbit_hemorrhagic_disease ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbits_in_Australia

F17. Pedler et al. (Conservation Biology 30: 774-782, 2016), arid South Australia, 1970-2014: arid Australia's mammal extinctions are "largely attributable to competition from introduced herbivores, particularly European rabbits and predation by feral cats and foxes". After RHDV, rabbits fell by 85 percent at one site and from 139 to 22 per square kilometre at another; four native small mammals spread over far more ground: spinifex hopping mouse (extent 180 to 276-356 thousand km2), dusky hopping mouse (23 to 105-130), plains mouse (63 to 217-252), crest-tailed mulgara (1 to 98-133); three previously threatened species now qualify for downgrading. The gains were credited to "decreased competition for food resources and declines in rabbit-dependent predators"; cat and fox numbers "followed rabbit population trends".
    https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65dede246eef00c2cea1600b ; https://conservationevidencejournal.com/individual-study/7793

## Zebra mussels

F18. Zebra mussels are native to the Ponto-Caspian region (Black, Caspian and Aral Sea drainages); first found in North America in the Great Lakes basin in 1988, "likely transported via ballast water"; one mussel filters up to about a litre of water a day; North America's predators of them are too few to suppress outbreaks.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Zebra_mussel

F19. In the Hudson River zebra mussels "first appeared in the Hudson in May 1991"; by September 1992 they made up more than half of the river's heterotrophic biomass, an estimated 50 to 600 billion animals, filtering in summer a volume equal to the river between Troy and Newburgh every one to four days. Phytoplankton biomass "has fallen by 80%"; small zooplankton such as rotifers nearly disappeared and crustacean zooplankton, "a key food for young fish", were roughly halved; the river is about 50 percent clearer; open-water fish (shad, herring) declined in numbers and growth, while shallow-water sunfish increased; the native pearly mussels, once over a billion, may disappear from the river. Clearer water may have let water-celery beds spread.
    https://www.caryinstitute.org/science/research-projects/zebra-mussels-and-hudson-river ; https://www.caryinstitute.org/news-insights/feature/aliens-ate-hudson-river

## Fire and feral cats

F20. In the Kimberley, researchers GPS-collared 32 feral cats; the cats travelled up to 12.5 km beyond their home territory to hunt on land recently burned by intense fires, staying an average of 15 days. After intense fires "Small mammals become more exposed, and easier for cats to detect and capture" (NASA Earth Observatory). McGregor's thesis gives up to 15 km.
    https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/feral-cats-forage-at-burn-scars-88168 ; https://eprints.utas.edu.au/23875/

F21. McGregor, Legge, Jones and Johnson (PLoS ONE 2015) put video cameras on the collars of feral cats in northern Australia (23 deployments on 13 cats): "From 89 hours of footage, we recorded 101 hunting events, of which 32 were successful"; hunting success rose "from 17% in habitats with dense grass or complex rocks to 70% in open areas". They propose "supressing intense fires, reducing populations of feral herbivores, and restoration of dense grass or shrubs".
    https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0133915

## A boom in the Serengeti: wildebeest, fire and trees

F22. Rinderpest, a viral disease of cattle and buffalo that also infects wild hoofed animals such as wildebeest (Grokipedia), kept the Serengeti's wildebeest low; after it was eradicated there in the 1960s the herds rose about six-fold, from about 250,000 in 1960 to about 1.4 million by 1977, then held near 1.3 million. Rinderpest was declared eradicated worldwide in 2011.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rinderpest ; https://open.library.ubc.ca/cIRcle/collections/831/items/1.0099202 ; https://www.pbs.org/wnet/nature/how-wildebeest-saved-serengeti-az7odc/19932/

F23. Holdo et al. (PLoS Biology 2009): "the wildebeest population irruption that followed disease (rinderpest) eradication" led to "a widespread reduction in the extent of fire and an ongoing recovery of the tree population"; grazing lowers the grass fuel and so fire frequency, and "fire tends to remove small trees"; elephants had "only weak support" and rainfall "failed to directly explain" tree density. Tony Sinclair, who arrived in 1965: the wildebeest "were eating up the grass", so "less fuel, and therefore less burning"; trees reached levels that "probably hadn't occurred since the 1800's", with more food for elephants, giraffes and many birds (PBS).
    https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.1000210 ; https://www.pbs.org/wnet/nature/how-wildebeest-saved-serengeti-az7odc/19932/

## DDT through the food chain

F24. Paul Müller found DDT's insecticidal action in 1939 (Nobel Prize 1948); "In the United States and Europe, farmers adopted it en masse from 1945 onward". DDT is strongly fat-soluble and persistent (soil half-life of 2 to 15 years); it is "one of the least soluble chemicals known" in water and "accumulates progressively in adipose tissue"; organochlorines are "lipophilic and not easily degraded" (Wikipedia); persistent pollutants are "retained due to slow elimination rates" (Grokipedia). Othmar Zeidler first made it in 1874 without knowing it killed insects.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/DDT ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomagnification ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Biomagnification

F25. Biomagnification is "the increase in concentration of a substance in the tissues of organisms at successively higher levels in a food chain" (Wikipedia); bioaccumulation is build-up within one organism over time (Grokipedia). Wikipedia explains DDT's rise as predators eating the fat of their prey, where the DDT is stored (paraphrase).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomagnification ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Biomagnification

F26. Woodwell, Wurster and Isaacson ("DDT Residues in an East Coast Estuary", Science 156: 821-824, 1967), Long Island: concentrations increased "with trophic level through more than three orders of magnitude", from about 0.04 ppm in plankton to 75 ppm in a ring-billed gull, and birds had 10 to 100 times more than fish. Time (1969) summarised the chain: water .000003 ppm (3 parts per trillion; Grokipedia: estuarine water "as low as 3 parts per trillion"), zooplankton .04 ppm, plankton-eating fish .5 ppm, larger fish 2.0 ppm, fish-eating birds such as cormorants, mergansers and ospreys 25 ppm, about ten million times the water's (25 / 0.000003 is about 8 million).
    https://scilit.com/publications/c9f74feefc5007da41f8ca250b8ff910 (abstract via search) ; https://content.time.com/time/subscriber/article/0,33009,901002-2,00.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Biomagnification

F27. Rachel Carson's Silent Spring was published on 27 September 1962; she argued DDT can "enter the food chain, where it accumulates in the fatty tissues of organisms" (Grokipedia), with examples such as robins dying from eating contaminated earthworms.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Silent_Spring

F28. Derek Ratcliffe published a note in British Birds in 1958 on "the abnormal number of broken eggs he was finding in Peregrine eyries"; "Parent birds were literally crushing their own eggs" (JSTOR Daily). He weighed and measured eggs from museum (British Museum of Natural History) and private collections and recent surveys, using a shell index (shell weight over length times breadth): "Prior to that shell thickness averaged a steady 1.82 for over 125 years"; "the turning point in Britain was in 1947", and the index fell to 1.53, "an average loss of 16%" (Ratcliffe, Nature 215: 208-210, 1967, "Decrease in Eggshell Weight in Certain Birds of Prey", as summarised).
    https://daily.jstor.org/the-case-of-the-thinning-eggshells ; https://www.birdsoutsidemywindow.org/2017/01/10/how-museums-saved-the-peregrine-falcon/ ; https://www.nature.com/articles/215208a0

F29. Hickey and Anderson (Science 162: 271-273, 1968): "Catastrophic declines of three raptorial species in the United States have been accompanied by decreases in eggshell thickness that began in 1947, have amounted to 19 percent or more, and were identical to phenomena reported in Britain." They compared 1960s eggs with museum sets from 1890-1920; ospreys and bald eagles were among the thinned, while red-tailed hawks, golden eagles and great horned owls served as controls and showed no thinning (Field Museum). DDE, "a common metabolite of DDT", is considered the main chemical behind the thinning; Wiemeyer and Porter reported "DDE thins eggshells of captive American kestrels" (Nature 1970; a 1972 reply questioned the causation shown).
    https://www.usgs.gov/publications/chlorinated-hydrocarbons-and-eggshell-changes-raptorial-and-fish-eating-birds ; https://www.fieldmuseum.org/blog/eggshells-ddt-collections-and-study-design ; https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/5221757

F30. Bald eagles, eating contaminated fish, "bioaccumulated DDT and its persistent metabolite DDE"; there were "417 known nesting pairs remaining in 1963" in the lower 48 states (a Federal Register notice gives about 487). The EPA banned DDT in 1972; illegal shooting and habitat loss also cut eagles, and the Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act and the Endangered Species Act protected them. About 9,789 nesting pairs at delisting on 8 August 2007; about 71,400 pairs by 2019. The American peregrine left the endangered list in August 1999 after captive breeding.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Bald_eagle ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Peregrine_falcon ; https://www.fws.gov/carp/sites/default/files/documents/bald-eagle-fact-sheet.pdf (via search)

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Students expect a change in one population to stay with the nearby links of a food web and miss the ripples that travel several links away (Griffiths and Grant 1985, "High School Students' Understanding of Food Webs", Journal of Research in Science Teaching 22: 421-436; Barman et al. 1995; as summarised by Harvard Project Zero).
    https://clic.gse.harvard.edu/file_url/188 (via search) ; https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/tea.3660220505

Classroom confusions the wrong choices use (no research source read for these; each choice is wrong by the facts cited):
- Taking away a predator must help everything it ate (Paine's result, F2-F3, says otherwise).
- A bigger animal holds more of a poison because it is bigger (concentration in parts per million is per weight, F26).
- A poison multiplies inside a body like a germ (DDT is a chemical; it is stored, F24-F25).
- A virus kills whatever eats or meets its victims (RHDV infects only European rabbits, F16).
- Grazers like wildebeest eat trees (they eat grass, F23).

## Left out, or not sure

- The number of rabbits Austin released (13 or 24) differs between sources: not used.
- Whether elk avoided risky streamsides from fear (the "landscape of fear") as well as falling in number is disputed: the Yellowstone key says only that the elk ate fewer shoots.
- Songbirds in Yellowstone: only Grokipedia gives a figure; not used.
- The British peregrine crash of the late 1950s owed much to other pesticides as well (Grokipedia cites a 2021 British Birds review saying DDT's role was overstated); no question says DDT alone cut the peregrines, only that the shells thinned from 1947.
- Lab doses of DDE were questioned by critics (Grokipedia, DDT); the context says only that captive kestrels fed DDE laid thinner shells.
- The Time figures (F26) are a magazine's summary of Woodwell's chain; the plankton figure (0.04 ppm) matches the paper's abstract.

## Added at revision (2026-11-26)

F31. Leahy, Legge, Tuft, McGregor, Barmuta, Jones and Johnson, "Amplified predation after fire suppresses rodent populations in Australia's tropical savannas", Wildlife Research 42: 705-716 (2015). In northern Australia's savannas they live-trapped the pale field rat (Rattus tunneyi) and the western chestnut mouse (Pseudomys nanus) under a high-intensity burn, a low-intensity patchy burn and an unburnt control, and radio-tracked 38 pale field rats. Both species declined after fire, most sharply after the high-intensity burn. Of five tested causes (direct deaths in the fire, starvation from food lost, poorer breeding, emigration, more predation) only predation was supported: predation rose after fire with the loss of ground cover, feral cat and dingo activity rose on the burns, and predation by feral cats and snakes was found directly. The authors recommend cool, patchy burns and less area burnt. (The study site is not named in the abstract read; the stem says only "northern Australia".)
    https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/amplified-predation-after-fire-suppresses-rodent-populations-in-a/ ; https://publish.csiro.au/WR/WR15011 (abstract via search)

F32. Paine's sea stars at Mukkaw Bay "ate barnacles, chitons, limpets, snails, and mussels"; "Mussels and chitons were the most important contributors to the starfish diet"; on the cleared plot "the two limpet and two chiton species had abandoned the plot" (Nautilus). So the limpets and chitons left rather than multiplying.
    https://nautil.us/the-ecologist-who-threw-starfish-235831

F34. Cane toads are not free of enemies: keelback snakes eat toads without harm and meat ants kill young toads (Grokipedia, as read by the fact-check); Wikipedia: "no efficient natural predators". So: an invader that few native animals can safely eat.
