# Competing for Resources (T2, 6th grade, life strand, G6.L4)

Sources were read on 2026-11-26. Grokipedia first; a second source is given
for anything a child could be taught wrong.

## Resources and competition

F1. Competition is an interaction in which individuals or species vie for the same limited resources; Grokipedia: the resources "must be in short supply relative to demand". The shared resources include food, water, space and mates; CK-12's middle-school text: "Competition occurs when organisms strive for limited resources", such as food, water, light or space.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Competition_(biology) ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Life_Science_for_Middle_School_(CK-12)/12%3A_Ecology/12.07%3A_Competition

F2. Competition lowers the fitness (growth, survival or breeding) of at least one of the competitors.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Competition_(biology)

F3. Intraspecific competition is between members of the same species; interspecific competition is between different species. Intraspecific competition is generally the stronger, because members of one species "share identical resource requirements"; a plant meta-analysis found intraspecific effects 4 to 5 times more intense. It grows fiercer as a population grows denser (OpenStax: "as population size increases, this competition intensifies"). CK-12 gives two trees competing for light, the taller more likely to survive.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Competition_(biology) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Intraspecific_competition ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-3-environmental-limits-to-population-growth ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Life_Science_for_Middle_School_(CK-12)/12%3A_Ecology/12.07%3A_Competition

F4. Competition need not be a fight. Exploitation competition is indirect: each competitor uses up a shared resource, leaving less for the others, and the organisms "may not actually come into contact and only interact via the shared resource indirectly" (Wikipedia). Interference competition is direct: one organism actively keeps another from a resource.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Competition_(biology) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intraspecific_competition

F5. Desert rodents and ants compete for seeds (Brown and Davidson, Science, 1977: overlapping diets, and each group increases when the other is excluded). In the experiment of Brown, Davidson and Reichman (1979), the number of ant colonies rose 71% on plots where rodents were fenced out, and rodents rose 20% in number where ants were removed.
    https://portal.weecology.org/publication/brown1977competition ; https://portal.weecology.org/publication/brown1979experimental/

F6. Harvester ants gather seeds as their main food, foraging "predominantly diurnal" (by day). Kangaroo rats, desert rodents, live mostly on seeds (about 80% of the diet) and forage strictly at night.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Harvester_ant ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kangaroo_rat

F7. A limiting factor is something in short supply that restricts the growth, size or spread of a population. Grokipedia: "Light availability often limits understory plant growth in dense forests"; water scarcity in dry lands "curtails plant productivity". In deserts, chronic water scarcity "limits primary productivity", and plant cover rarely exceeds 10 to 15% of the ground.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Limiting_factor ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Desert_ecology ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Desert

## The scarcest resource sets the limit

F8. Liebig's law of the minimum: growth is controlled "not by the aggregate availability of essential resources, but by the single scarcest resource"; "excess supplies of non-limiting nutrients cannot compensate for deficiencies in the critical one". Carl Sprengel first stated it in 1828; Justus von Liebig popularized it in 1840. It is pictured as a barrel whose water level is set by its shortest stave.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Liebig's_law_of_the_minimum ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Plant_nutrition

F9. Nitrogen and phosphorus are mineral macronutrients that plants absorb from the soil, mainly through their roots.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Plant_nutrition

F10. Nest sites can be a limiting factor. Earlier studies showed that "nest sites are in short supply and limit the populations of several small passerines" that nest in holes; putting up nest boxes "increased the density of breeding Great Tits in patches of all sizes" (Web Ecology, 2006, citing Newton's 1994 review). Felling and clearing have removed many natural tree hollows that hole-nesting birds rely on.
    https://we.copernicus.org/articles/6/37/2006/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nest_box

## Carrying capacity

F11. Carrying capacity is the largest population of a species that an environment can support year after year (Grokipedia: "sustain indefinitely"), set by supplies such as food, water and shelter. OpenStax: "the maximum population size that a particular environment can support".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carrying_capacity ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-3-environmental-limits-to-population-growth ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrying_capacity

F12. With plenty of resources a population grows faster and faster; "as resources begin to become limited, the growth rate decreases", and "growth levels off at the carrying capacity", giving an S-shaped curve (logistic growth; Pierre-François Verhulst's model of 1838).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-3-environmental-limits-to-population-growth ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrying_capacity ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Logistic_function

F13. Carrying capacity is not fixed: ecosystems change over time, "therefore changing the resources available".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrying_capacity

F14. A population can overshoot its carrying capacity for a time; above it, the population shrinks (OpenStax: the size "exceeds the carrying capacity for short periods of time" before falling back).
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/45-3-environmental-limits-to-population-growth ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrying_capacity

F15. St. Matthew Island: the United States Coast Guard released 29 reindeer (24 females, 5 males) on 20 August 1944. With no predators or hunting, the herd grew to about 6,000 by summer 1963. Heavy grazing had used up much of the lichen, its winter food; in the severe winter of 1963 to 1964 deep snow covered what was left and the reindeer starved, a die-off of about 99%: 42 were left by summer 1966 (Wikipedia: 42 "in the next two years"). The study was David Klein's.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/St._Matthew_Island ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Matthew_Island

## Plants compete too

F16. In dense forests, summer light at the forest floor is often between 0.7% and 7% of full sunlight; a tropical canopy intercepts around 95% of the sunlight, and understory plants must be shade-tolerant.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Understory ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Understory

F17. Plants compete above the ground for light ("taller individuals shade subordinates, reducing photosynthetic rates") and below it for water (roots "deplete soil moisture"), as well as for soil nutrients and space.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Competition_(biology) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thinning

F18. Crowded plants compete for light, water and nutrients; crowding causes "stunted individual development and elevated mortality rates", and thinning lets the plants left grow. Extension services: "Crowded vegetables will compete with each other for water, nutrients, and sunlight" (UF/IFAS); not thinning carrots "can result in limited root growth because of crowding" (Extension Master Gardener); carrot "Twisting and intertwining result from seeding too thickly and inadequate thinning of seedlings" (Illinois Extension).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thinning ; https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/escambiaco/2014/09/30/thinning-vegetable-seedlings-2 ; https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=679731 ; https://extension.illinois.edu/gardening-site/carrots

F19. A plant makes its own food by photosynthesis, but it needs light, water and carbon dioxide to do it and takes its mineral nutrients from the soil, so it still depends on (and competes for) its surroundings' resources.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Plant_nutrition ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Competition_(biology)

## When two kinds need exactly the same thing

F20. Competitive exclusion: two species with identical niches, competing for the same limiting resources, cannot coexist indefinitely. Georgy Gause (1934) grew two kinds of paramecium, P. aurelia and P. caudatum, which both fed on bacteria: grown alone, each reached a stable population; grown together, P. aurelia increased while P. caudatum declined and died out. CK-12's middle-school text: the less suited species "will either adapt, move from the area, or die out". Species that live side by side usually reduce competition by using different parts of the resources (niche partitioning).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Competitive_exclusion_principle ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Competition_(biology) ; https://k12.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Science_and_Technology/Life_Science_for_Middle_School_(CK-12)/12%3A_Ecology/12.07%3A_Competition

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Competition means a fight, or at least a meeting; F4 corrects it (exploitation competition works through the shared resource alone).

M2. Living things that share a home simply get along: the middle-school misconception list records "Species coexist in ecosystems because of their compatible needs and behaviors; they need to get along." F1 and F20 correct it.
    https://nsdl.library.cornell.edu/websites/wiki/index.php/MSP_MiddleSchoolPortal/Misconceptions_at_the_Middle.html

M3. Carrying capacity never changes, and a population grows until it reaches its limit and then crashes and dies out (reported as students' most common population-ecology misconceptions in an Ecological Society of America 2009 conference abstract, read only as a search-result summary; the page itself would not load). F12, F13 and F14 correct it.
    https://eco.confex.com/eco/2009/techprogram/P17956.HTM

M4. More of any nutrient means more growth, or the nutrients add up to a total; F8 corrects it. Author's judgment, from the law's own framing ("not by the aggregate").

M5. A plant makes its own food, so it has nothing to compete for; F19 corrects it. Author's judgment.

M6. Two living things that need the same resource always compete, whatever the supply; F1 corrects it (the supply must be short).

CAUTION, not used: the hawk-by-day, owl-by-night pair as an example of the same prey shared in time: Marti and Kochert (1995) found red-tailed hawks and great horned owls "take largely different prey species in the same localities". The ants and rodents of F5 and F6 replace it.
    https://www.usgs.gov/publications/are-red-tailed-hawks-and-great-horned-owls-diurnal-nocturnal-dietary-counterparts

CAUTION, not used: the even spacing of desert shrubs is explained in some sources by root competition for water and in others by chemicals the roots give off (Grokipedia Larrea tridentata), so no question uses it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Larrea_tridentata

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F21. A limiting factor is broader than the scarcest resource: Grokipedia defines it as "any environmental condition, resource, or biological interaction that restricts the growth" of a population; the density-dependent ones include "intraspecific competition for resources, predation, disease transmission, and parasitism", and abiotic ones "temperature, light intensity, water availability, soil pH, and mineral nutrients". Very often it is a resource in short supply (F7, F8).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Limiting_factor

F22. St. Matthew Island, the two views. David Klein (1968, Journal of Wildlife Management 32:350, "The introduction, increase, and crash of reindeer on St. Matthew Island"): food supply, through its interaction with climatic factors, was the dominant regulating mechanism (abstract as given by catalogue listings); Klein saw that the reindeer had trampled and overgrazed lichen mats before the crash (Alaska Science Forum), and Grokipedia says the herd "overshot the island's estimated carrying capacity". Frank L. Miller, Samuel J. Barry and Wendy A. Calvert (2005), "St. Matthew Island reindeer crash revisited: Their demise was not nigh--but then, why did they die?", Rangifer 25(4):185-197: "the winter weather was the all-important factor"; the herd was still growing fast, calf:cow ratios were about 60:100 in both 1957 and 1963, and they reject a food shortage as the main cause.
    https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/rangifer/article/view/1783 ; https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/when-reindeer-paradise-turned-purgatory-0 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/St._Matthew_Island ; https://scilit.com/publications/2e960cf75e8238760ec3c390b5a157a6
