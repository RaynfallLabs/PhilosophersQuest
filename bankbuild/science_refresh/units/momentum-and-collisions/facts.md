## Momentum and impulse

F1. Momentum is the product of an object's mass and its velocity. It is a vector: it has a size and a direction, the direction of the velocity. Grokipedia: "a vector quantity defined as the product of an object's mass and its velocity"; OpenStax: "Linear momentum is defined as the product of a system's mass multiplied by its velocity."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Momentum ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/8-1-linear-momentum-and-force

F2. The trade-off: because momentum is mass times velocity, more mass at the same speed means more momentum (a truck at a bicycle's speed carries far more momentum than the bicycle), and a light object moving fast can carry as much momentum as a heavy one moving slowly. A truck with 20 times a cyclist's mass, at a twentieth of his speed, has exactly his momentum (20 x 1/20 = 1). OpenStax: "a large, fast-moving object has greater momentum than a smaller, slower object."
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/8-1-linear-momentum-and-force ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Momentum (definition, F1)

F3. Impulse is a force acting over a time; it equals the change in momentum (the impulse-momentum theorem). For a constant force acting for a time, the change in momentum is the force times the time. So a steady braking force takes momentum away at a steady rate, and two objects with equal momentum stopped by equal steady forces stop in the same time. OpenStax: "Change in momentum equals the average net external force multiplied by the time this force acts." OpenStax 8.1: "The net external force equals the change in momentum of a system divided by the time over which it changes."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Impulse_(physics) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/8-2-impulse ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/8-1-linear-momentum-and-force

F4. For a given change in momentum, a longer stopping time means a smaller average force, and a shorter time a larger one. Wikipedia (Crumple zone): "the average force applied to the occupants is inversely related to the time over which it is applied"; OpenStax: "A longer collision time means the force on the car will be less." Grokipedia (Impulse) gives as uses "crumple zones in automobiles that extend collision time to reduce average force on passengers" and "athletes bending knees upon landing to minimize impact forces."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crumple_zone ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/8-2-impulse ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Impulse_(physics)

## Conservation of momentum

F5. With no outside (external) force, the total momentum of a system stays constant; the forces the bodies exert on each other cancel in pairs. Grokipedia: "In an isolated system where no external forces act, the total momentum" remains constant. OpenStax: "An isolated system is defined to be one for which the net external force is zero"; "Conservation of momentum is violated only when the net external force is not zero." OpenStax's example: a football player bouncing off a goalpost passes momentum to Earth, which recoils by an immeasurably small amount.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Momentum ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/8-3-conservation-of-momentum

F6. When a moving body strikes an equal body at rest head on in a bouncy (elastic) collision, the two exchange velocities: the moving one stops and the struck one moves off with the first one's velocity. Wikipedia (Momentum): "The bodies have exchanged their velocities." This is the cue ball stopping dead while the struck ball rolls on.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Momentum

F7. In an inelastic collision kinetic energy is not conserved, some becoming "heat, sound, or permanent deformation", yet linear momentum is still conserved; in a perfectly inelastic collision the bodies stick together and move as one unit (Grokipedia). For two equal bodies meeting head on at equal speeds and sticking, the formula on that page gives a final velocity of zero (the momenta, equal and opposite, total zero before and after).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Inelastic_collision

F8. Kinetic energy is one half of mass times speed squared, a scalar with no direction (Grokipedia: it "depends on the object's mass and speed but not on its direction of motion"). The net work done on an object, force times distance for a steady force along the motion, equals its change in kinetic energy, so with equal braking forces the object with more kinetic energy needs a longer stopping DISTANCE (not a longer time). For F2's truck and cyclist, the cyclist has 20 times the kinetic energy (1/2 x 1 x 20^2 = 200 against 1/2 x 20 x 1^2 = 10, in the same units), so he brakes over 20 times the distance in the same time.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Kinetic_energy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Work_(physics)

F9. Recoil: before a gun is fired the system is at rest, so its total momentum is zero; the bullet and the powder gases go forward and the gun goes back with equal and opposite momentum, so the total stays zero. For the same momentum, "a heavier firearm reduces" the recoil speed. In rifles the gases are "often accounting for 30-50% of the total recoil momentum" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Recoil ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recoil

F10. Held tight to the shoulder, the rifle and the shooter's body act as one unit, so the recoiling mass is the rifle and the person together, and "The mass of the rifle and person together is so huge that the speed of the recoil becomes very small" (A Moment of Science, Indiana Public Media). Wikipedia (Physics of firearms) gives the recoil speed as the bullet's momentum divided by the gun-and-shooter mass. Held loosely, the light rifle alone picks up the recoil speed and then strikes the shoulder.
    https://www.ipm.org/amomentofscience/rifles-and-the-law/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_of_firearms

F11. Two skaters pushing off each other from rest: "Momentum before they push apart = 0 = momentum after they push apart"; their momenta are equal and opposite, "the person with the greater mass will move off much more slowly", and "If the mass of the man is double that of the girl she will 'recoil' at twice his speed" (schoolphysics.co.uk).
    https://schoolphysics.co.uk/age16-19/Mechanics/Dynamics/text/Conservation_of_momentum%20_and_skaters/index.html

## How it was found: Descartes, Huygens and the Royal Society

F12. Rene Descartes, in his Principles of Philosophy (Principia Philosophiae, 1644), held that the quantity of motion, each body's size times its speed, summed over the bodies, is conserved. His law "only recognizes a body's degree of motion, which correlates to the scalar quantity 'speed'", so "reversing the direction of B's motion does not alter the total quantity of motion" (Stanford Encyclopedia). His fourth rule says a smaller body, however fast, can never move a larger body at rest, "obviously contradicting common experience." Wikipedia: he "believed that it is speed rather than velocity that is conserved." Counted his way, two equal clay lumps meeting head on and stopping have lost all their quantity of motion.
    https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes-physics/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Christiaan_Huygens

F13. Christiaan Huygens found that Descartes's laws of collision were "largely wrong" and "formulated the correct laws, including the conservation of the product of mass times the square of the speed for hard bodies, and the conservation of quantity of motion in one direction for all bodies" (Wikipedia). Grokipedia: Descartes's rules conserved quantity of motion "without distinguishing direction or elasticity"; Huygens established momentum as "a vector quantity conserved in all collisions." G. E. Smith: Huygens's first rule replaced Descartes's quantity with a conserved directional (vector) quantity, what is now called momentum. The manuscript De Motu Corporum ex Percussione was completed in 1656 and published in 1703; he passed his results to Brouncker and Wren in London in 1661, announced them to the Royal Society in 1668 and published them in the Journal des Scavans in 1669. The mass-times-speed-squared rule fails for soft bodies such as clay, which turn motion into heat and deformation (F7).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Christiaan_Huygens ; https://www.scribd.com/document/206604528/Smith-g-2006-Vis (Smith 2006, as summarised in search) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum

F14. The Royal Society, 1668-1669: Christopher Wren brought his theory of the collision of bodies to the meeting of 17 December 1668, saying he had held it since about 1660 and that he and Lawrence Rooke had made trials before the Society to verify it; Wren treated perfectly elastic (hard, bouncing) bodies and John Wallis inelastic (soft) ones, and their papers were printed together as "A Summary Account of the General Laws of Motion by Dr. John Wallis, and Dr. Christopher Wren," Philosophical Transactions, January 1669. Huygens's tract on the motion of bodies resulting from impact came with his letter of 4 January 1669 and was read on 7 January 1669.
    https://pictures.royalsociety.org/image-rs-16013 ; https://pictures.royalsociety.org/image-rs-14316 ; https://pictures.royalsociety.org/image-rs-16014 ; https://www.scribd.com/document/206604528/Smith-g-2006-Vis

F15. Isaac Newton, Principia (1687), Scholium to the Laws of Motion: Wren, Wallis and Huygens "did severally determine the rules of the congress and reflexion of hard bodies"; "Sir Christopher Wren confirmed the truth of the thing before the Royal Society by the experiment of pendulums." Newton tested it himself "with pendulums of ten feet, in unequal as well as equal bodies," measuring each ball's speed by the chord of the arc it swung through ("the velocity of a pendulous body in the lowest point is as the chord of the arc which it has described in its descent"), and found, "without an error of 3 inches," equal changes in opposite directions in the two bodies' motions. He tried balls of tightly wound wool, which sprang apart at "about 5 to 9" of the speed with which they met; "Balls of steel returned with almost the same velocity," cork somewhat less, glass "about 15 to 16"; and the experiments "do succeed as well in soft as in hard bodies." Corollary III: the quantity of motion, the sum of the motions one way less those the contrary way, "suffers no change from the action of bodies among themselves."
    https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Principles_of_Natural_Philosophy_(1846)/Axioms,_or_Laws_of_Motion

## Surviving the stop: Stapp and Barenyi

F16. John Paul Stapp, a career U.S. Air Force officer, flight surgeon and physician, rode the rocket sled Sonic Wind No. 1 on 10 December 1954, his 29th and last ride at Holloman Air Force Base in New Mexico: it reached 632 miles per hour (1,017 km/h) in five seconds and was stopped in 1.4 seconds, a deceleration of 46.2 g (Grokipedia; Wikipedia, citing Aviation Week, "slowed to a stop in 1.4 seconds"). "Textbooks of the day put 18 Gs as the limit of human tolerance" (Popular Science). Strapped in "with adequate harnessing", he showed that "properly restrained individuals could survive impacts that previously exceeded human tolerance limits" (Grokipedia). Injuries over his runs: retinal bleeding with temporary blindness, wrist fractures, broken ribs. He founded the Stapp Car Crash Conference (1955) and pressed for seat belts. Average deceleration of the stop: 632 mph is about 282 m/s, so about 200 m/s^2 over 1.4 s (about 20 g); the start averaged about 56 m/s^2 over 5 s (about 6 g).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/John_Stapp ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stapp ; https://www.popsci.com/blog-network/vintage-space/john-paul-stapp-real-life-rocket-sled-man

F17. Bela Barenyi joined Daimler-Benz in 1939. His patent for crumple zones at the front and back and a rigid passenger cell, DRP No. 854 157, was filed on 23 January 1951 and granted on 28 August 1952 (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: he first patented the idea in 1937, and "Barenyi questioned the opinion that had prevailed until then that a safe car had to be rigid." "The first Mercedes-Benz carbody developed using the patent was the 1959 Mercedes W111 'tail fin' saloon" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: the W111 "introduced in 1959").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/B%C3%A9la_Bar%C3%A9nyi ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Crumple_zone ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crumple_zone

F18. A crumple zone increases "the duration of the crash event" and reduces "peak deceleration forces experienced by occupants"; "Rigid vehicles stop abruptly, resulting in very high peak deceleration forces"; the benefit "stems from the impulse-momentum theorem": the change in momentum is fixed for a given mass and speed, so a longer collision means a lower average force (Grokipedia). The crumpling is permanent deformation that absorbs energy (it does not spring back). Wikipedia notes a misconception "that they reduce safety for the occupants of the vehicle by allowing the body of the vehicle to collapse."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Crumple_zone ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crumple_zone

## Padding, air bags, helmets, knees

F19. OpenStax: dashboard padding and air bags lengthen the time over which the force acts on the occupants, lowering it; "the force on your legs can be immense if you land stiff-legged on a hard surface"; rolling on a soft mat extends the time; catching a ball while "giving" with it, pulling the hands back, lowers the force. Grokipedia (Impulse): "athletes bending knees upon landing to minimize impact forces." A person landing from the same height arrives at the same speed and ends at rest, so the change in momentum is the same however the landing is made.
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/8-2-impulse ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Impulse_(physics)

F20. A bicycle helmet's expanded polystyrene liner crushes, undergoing "irreversible plastic deformation", "limiting peak accelerations transmitted to the head"; it is single-impact and must be replaced after a crash (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Bicycle_helmet

## Misconceptions a 9th-grader brings (for honest wrong choices)

- M1. Momentum and kinetic energy are the same thing, or kinetic energy behaves like momentum (Singh and Rosengrant found many students thought kinetic energy is conserved in an explosion, and some that it is a vector; arXiv 1602.06497). Hence the lures "since his energy is far greater" (F8) and "the momentum has turned into heat" (F7: energy turns into heat; momentum passes to the table and Earth, F5).
- M2. Heavier means harder to stop whatever the speed, or faster means harder to stop whatever the mass (F2: it is the product).
- M3. Momentum is kept only in bouncy (elastic) collisions and lost in soft ones (F7, F15: Newton's wool balls).
- M4. Conservation means momentum can never change at all, even with friction acting (F5: only with no outside force).
- M5. Descartes's unsigned count: motions in opposite directions simply add (F12, F13).
- M6. Speed itself harms a body; or the same change of speed always means the same force (F3, F4, F16: only the change matters, and its time).
- M7. A crumpling car is weak and unsafe, or crumpling makes the stop quicker (F18).
- M8. The heavier pusher pushes harder (third law, already keyed at T2: forces are equal).
https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.06497

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F21. Stapp's run of 10 December 1954 (NASA history of the Air Force's space biology work): "no windshield at all was used"; "Windblast was as high as 7.7 pounds per square inch, or better than 1,100 pounds per square foot"; "This test was designed to explore both deceleration and windblast". Water brakes stopped the sled in 1.4 seconds, with a plateau of 25 g or more for over a second and peaks of 35 and 40 g (Wikipedia and Grokipedia give the commonly cited 46.2 g, F16). "What the run proved, essentially, was that windblast" was "negligible and unnoticeable in comparison with deceleration effects of g-plateaus of more than 25 g's for 1.1 seconds." So the wind did press on him at top speed, but far less than the stop.
    https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/afspbio/part4-2.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stapp

F22. Huygens's collision rules (De Motu Corporum ex Percussione) "derived rules for elastic impacts between bodies" (Grokipedia, Christiaan Huygens); at the Royal Society in 1668-1669 Wren and Huygens treated perfectly elastic (hard) bodies and Wallis perfectly inelastic (soft) ones (Oeuvres de Huygens XVI; arXiv 2512.22842). That motion turned into heat, heat being a form of energy, was established much later, by James Prescott Joule in the 1840s and 1850s (Grokipedia, Mechanical equivalent of heat: "experimentally established in the 1840s and 1850s by British physicist James Prescott Joule").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Christiaan_Huygens ; https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/huyg003oeuv16_01/huyg003oeuv16_01_0006.php ; https://arxiv.org/html/2512.22842v1 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mechanical_equivalent_of_heat

F23. A crumple zone works during the crash, by folding once the car has hit: "the vehicle's change in momentum is fixed for a given speed and mass", and the crumpling lengthens the time over which that change happens (Grokipedia, Crumple zone; F18). It cannot lower the speed the car is going when it hits; the work-energy formula on the same page takes v as the impact velocity. MISCONCEPTION (for a wrong choice): that a crumple zone lowers the speed of impact.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Crumple_zone
