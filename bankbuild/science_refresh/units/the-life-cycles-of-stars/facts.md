## Born in nebulae, pulled together by gravity

F1. Stars form in nebulae: giant molecular clouds "primarily composed of molecular hydrogen", with helium and a little dust, "the primary birthplace of stars". They are cold, "typically ... 10-20 K" (about 253 to 263 degrees below zero Celsius), the dense cores near 10 K. Stars form by "the gravitational collapse of dense, cold regions within giant molecular clouds".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Molecular_cloud ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Star_formation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Stellar_evolution

F2. Gravity pulls a clump in and the gas's own pressure pushes back: collapse begins where "self-gravity" can "dominate over thermal pressure" (the Jeans mass), which happens in the cold, dense regions. Warmer gas has more thermal pressure, so it resists collapse.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Star_formation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Stellar_evolution ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Molecular_cloud

F3. A collapsing core becomes a protostar, a core that "has collapsed under its own gravity"; it heats as it contracts, its light coming from "the release of gravitational potential energy during contraction", not from nuclear reactions. The protostar stage ends when the core reaches about 10 million K and the star settles on the main sequence (how it then shines is the T5 unit how-the-sun-shines; not keyed here). OpenStax: protostars "derive energy from gravitational contraction".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Protostar ; https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/21-2-the-h-r-diagram-and-the-study-of-stellar-evolution

F4. The Pillars of Creation are "towering columns of cool interstellar gas and dust" in the Eagle Nebula, photographed by Hubble on 1 April 1995; James Webb's infrared images of 2022 showed newly formed stars inside them, hidden from visible-light views. The Orion Nebula Cluster "hosts around 2,000 young stars".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pillars_of_Creation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Star_formation

## Mass decides the life

F5. A star's lifetime varies "inversely with mass". The Sun's main-sequence life is "about 10 billion years" (Grokipedia, Sun and Main sequence). A 10-Sun star lasts "only around 20 million years" (Grokipedia, Main sequence). OpenStax Table 22.1: a B0 star of 16 Suns lives about 10 million years; an O5 star of 40 Suns about 1 million; an M0 star of 0.4 Suns about 200 billion.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Main_sequence ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun ; https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/22-1-evolution-from-the-main-sequence-to-red-giants

F6. Why a bigger star lives shorter: a more massive star needs more pressure in its core, so its core is hotter, and "the rate of fusion depends very strongly on the star's core temperature"; "the higher the temperature in the central regions, the faster the star races through its storehouse of central hydrogen" (OpenStax). Massive stars have more fuel but burn it much faster. Brightness rises steeply with mass, roughly L proportional to M^3.5 (Grokipedia, Main sequence). Massive stars "exhaust their fuel rapidly" (Grokipedia, Stellar evolution). Both are hydrogen-burning main-sequence stars (same fuel).
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/22-1-evolution-from-the-main-sequence-to-red-giants ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Main_sequence ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Stellar_evolution

## A Sun-like star: red giant, planetary nebula, white dwarf

F7. When core hydrogen runs out, a Sun-like star's outer layers expand; "the expansion of a star's outer layers causes the temperature at the surface to decrease", so it looks redder, and it grows more luminous (moving up and right on the H-R diagram): a red giant (OpenStax). The Sun will "expand dramatically into a red giant phase", then "eject the outer envelope as a planetary nebula", leaving "a white dwarf remnant of about half the Sun's original mass" (Grokipedia, Sun). Stars up to about 8 Suns end as white dwarfs (Grokipedia, Stellar evolution; Wikipedia, Supernova: stars under about 8 Suns "never develop a core large enough to collapse").
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/22-1-evolution-from-the-main-sequence-to-red-giants ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Stellar_evolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova

F8. A planetary nebula is "an expanding, glowing shell of ionized gas" thrown off late in life by stars of about 0.8 to 8 Suns; the core left at its centre is a hot white dwarf (above 30,000 K), or will become one. William Herschel coined the name "planetary nebula" in 1785 because these round, hazy objects looked like planets in early telescopes; the name is "a misnomer": "planetary nebulae have no connection to planets". The Ring Nebula (M57) in Lyra is one. They last "typically 10,000 to 50,000 years".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Planetary_nebula

F9. A white dwarf typically holds 0.5 to 0.7 of the Sun's mass in a volume "comparable to Earth's" (density about a million grams per cubic centimetre). It is held up against gravity "by electron degeneracy pressure", the push of tightly squeezed electrons (the Pauli exclusion principle), with no ongoing nuclear reactions; it shines by leftover heat and slowly cools and fades.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/White_dwarf ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chandrasekhar_limit ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrasekhar_limit

F10. Sirius is "the brightest star in Earth's night sky", 8.6 light-years away. Its faint companion Sirius B, inferred by Friedrich Bessel in 1844 from Sirius's wobble, was "visually confirmed on January 31, 1862" by Alvan Graham Clark. Sirius B is a white dwarf: surface about 25,000 K (the Sun's is 5,772 K, so over four times as hot), about 1.0 Sun's mass, radius about 0.0084 of the Sun's, "comparable in size to Earth". Its luminosity is given as 0.024 of the Sun's (Grokipedia) or 0.056 (Wikipedia); both are less than a tenth.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Sirius ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirius ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun

## A massive star: red supergiant, supernova, neutron star or black hole

F11. Stars above about 8 Suns end differently: they swell into supergiants ("red supergiants (cool, with effective temperatures below ~4000 K)"); when the core can no longer support itself it collapses, which "triggers core collapse and a Type II supernova explosion". The remnant is a neutron star "for progenitors between 8 and 20-25 solar masses", a black hole "for more massive stars" (Grokipedia, Stellar evolution). Wikipedia (Supernova): the core can "collapse to form a neutron star, black hole".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Stellar_evolution ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova

F12. Betelgeuse is "a massive red supergiant star situated in the right shoulder of the constellation Orion": "a bright, reddish point of light"; surface about 3,500 K ("about 3,300°C"); 11 to 20 Suns' mass; radius 640 to 1,200 Suns' ("its surface would extend beyond the orbit of Mars"); about 10 million years old; 400 to 724 light-years away; luminosity 95,000 to 200,000 Suns. It is expected to explode as a supernova "within the next 100,000 years", most likely leaving a neutron star.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Betelgeuse

F13. Rigel is "a blue supergiant star" at the hunter's "left foot" in Orion, with a "striking blue-white hue"; surface about 12,100 K; 18 to 21 Suns' mass; luminosity about 120,000 Suns'; radius about 78 to 79 Suns' (far smaller than Betelgeuse); 8 to 10 million years old; about 848 to 863 light-years away (farther than Betelgeuse); expected to end in a core-collapse supernova.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Rigel ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Betelgeuse

F14. A supernova in AD 1054: Song dynasty Chinese astronomers recorded a "guest star" (kexing) on 4 July 1054; it was visible in daylight for 23 days and at night for about 653 days, fading by 1056, in Taurus. Its remnant is the Crab Nebula (Messier 1), "the expanding shell of gas and dust ejected during SN 1054", expanding at up to "1,500 kilometers per second". (Grokipedia notes the Chinese gave the position southeast of Zeta Tauri while the Crab lies northwest, so the record's direction is debated; the identification itself is standard.) At its core is the Crab Pulsar, a young neutron star "roughly 20 km across" that "rotates 30 times per second" (period 33 ms), found in 1968. Wikipedia (Supernova) also says Chinese astronomers recorded SN 1054, which produced the Crab Nebula.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/SN_1054 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Crab_Nebula ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova

F15. "Nova" is Latin for "new": the name meant an apparently new bright star. Supernovae in the Milky Way come about 1.6 to 4.6 times a century; the last one seen in our galaxy was Kepler's Supernova of 1604. SN 1987A, in the Large Magellanic Cloud, was "attributed to an explosion of a blue supergiant star" and gave "the only measurements of astronomical neutrinos other than the Sun's". (SN 1987A is not used in a question.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova

F16. A pulsar is "a rotating neutron star that emits beams of electromagnetic radiation from its magnetic poles", seen as pulses "akin to a lighthouse effect". Jocelyn Bell, a PhD student at Cambridge under Antony Hewish, helped build the radio array over two years and in 1967 noticed a "bit of scruff" on its chart-recorder paper (up to 96 feet of chart a night); the pulses came "every 1.337 seconds"; regularity confirmed 28 November 1967; the source was nicknamed "LGM-1" (Little Green Men), checks ruled out interference from Earth, and the 1968 Nature paper suggested "oscillations of white dwarf or neutron stars". Franco Pacini (1967) and Thomas Gold proposed a rotating neutron star; the Crab Pulsar's 33-millisecond period (1968) was "too short to be consistent with other proposed models", and it gave "direct evidence that pulsars are young neutron stars formed in core-collapse supernovae". The 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Hewish and Martin Ryle, the Swedish Academy noting Hewish's "decisive role in the discovery of pulsars" (Ryle's share was for other work); Bell was not included. She received the 2018 Special Breakthrough Prize ($3 million) and gave it away to fund physics students.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pulsar ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Jocelyn_Bell_Burnell ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jocelyn_Bell_Burnell

F17. A neutron star typically has "around 1.4 solar masses" with a radius of "approximately 10-14 km" (Grokipedia, Pulsar). Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky (1934) proposed that a supernova would leave "a small, dense star consisting primarily of neutrons" (Wikipedia, Pulsar).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pulsar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar

## Colour, temperature and the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram

F18. A star's colour shows its surface temperature: by blackbody radiation (Wien's law) hotter stars give off more of their light at shorter, bluer wavelengths. O stars (30,000 to 50,000+ K) look blue; M stars (2,400 to 3,700 K) look red; G stars such as the Sun (5,200 to 6,000 K) are yellow-white. The Sun's surface is 5,772 K.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Stellar_classification ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Sun

F19. The Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) diagram "plots stars' luminosity (or absolute magnitude) against their surface temperature (or spectral type)"; luminosity rises upward and temperature rises to the left. About 90 percent of stars fall on the main sequence, a diagonal band from hot, luminous blue O stars at the top left to cool, faint red M stars at the bottom right; the Sun (G2V) is on it. Giants and supergiants lie above it (bright and cool); white dwarfs lie below it, hot and faint. A star's place on the main sequence is "determined primarily by mass".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Hertzsprung%E2%80%93Russell_diagram ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Main_sequence ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertzsprung%E2%80%93Russell_diagram

F20. A star's true brightness (luminosity) depends on its surface temperature and on how much surface it has: a red giant is brighter than it is hot because its surface is so large (OpenStax, F7), and a white dwarf is hot but faint because it is so small (F9, F10, F19).
    https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/22-1-evolution-from-the-main-sequence-to-red-giants ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hertzsprung%E2%80%93Russell_diagram

F21. Ejnar Hertzsprung, "a Danish astronomer" trained as a chemical engineer, distinguished giant and dwarf stars in 1905 (red stars could be luminous giants or faint dwarfs) and produced the "first color-magnitude diagrams in 1911, plotting stellar brightness against color" for the Pleiades and Hyades clusters, revealing the main sequence. Henry Norris Russell, at Princeton, "independently produced a comparable version in 1913", "plotting the absolute magnitudes of stars against their spectral types", using nearby stars with measured parallaxes (distances); he presented it to the American Astronomical Society in December 1913 and published it in 1914. Wikipedia: "created independently in 1911 by Ejnar Hertzsprung and by Henry Norris Russell in 1913."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ejnar_Hertzsprung ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Henry_Norris_Russell ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hertzsprung%E2%80%93Russell_diagram ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertzsprung%E2%80%93Russell_diagram

F22. Russell first read the diagram as a path of age, driven by gravity alone: a star starts large, cool and red (at the giant end), contracts and grows hotter and denser, peaks on the main sequence, then cools into a dim, dense red star, so it moves down the main sequence as it ages. He showed the diagram to the Royal Astronomical Society on 13 June 1913 and to the American Astronomical Society on 30 December 1913. The discovery that nuclear energy powers stars "brought about drastic revisions in Russell's scheme" (EBSCO). A star's place on the main sequence is in fact set by its mass (F19).
    https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/russell-announces-his-theory-stellar-evolution ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Main_sequence

## Chandrasekhar's limit and Eddington's objection

F23. Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, born 19 October 1910 in Lahore, worked out the white-dwarf mass limit "during his maiden voyage from India to Cambridge, England" in 1930 (he left Bombay on 31 July; "the 19-year-old scholar"). Adding special relativity (the squeezed electrons' "speeds ... approach the speed of light") gives a maximum mass: "approximately 1.4 times the mass of the Sun", refined to about 1.44. Above it, gravity overcomes the electron pressure and the star collapses into a neutron star or black hole. Without relativity (Ralph Fowler's 1926 treatment) "more massive white dwarfs are smaller" and the ceiling does not appear; Edmund Stoner and Wilhelm Anderson had published related limits in 1929, and Lev Landau computed the value in 1932.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chandrasekhar_limit ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Subrahmanyan_Chandrasekhar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrasekhar_limit ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subrahmanyan_Chandrasekhar ; https://grokipedia.com/page/White_dwarf ; https://physicstoday.aip.org/letters/placing-chandras-work-in-historical-context

F24. At a meeting of the Royal Astronomical Society in London in January 1935, Arthur Eddington ("the established British astrophysicist", Wikipedia; Plumian Professor and director of the Cambridge Observatory from 1913, Grokipedia) spoke straight after Chandrasekhar and attacked the limit: "I think there should be a law of Nature to prevent a star from behaving in this absurd way!" (printed in The Observatory, vol. 58, 1935). Grokipedia: he dismissed "stellar collapse beyond the mass limit as 'absurd' and unphysical"; Eddington called the collapse "absurd in the physical world". His own 1935 paper argued that pressure "increases indefinitely with density", that is, that electron pressure could hold up a white dwarf of any mass. Eddington accepted white dwarfs themselves (St Andrews: he "reinforced the established idea" that stars eventually become white dwarfs, and said Fowler had "rescued" them). Chandrasekhar later spoke of "feeling publicly humiliated".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrasekhar_limit ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chandrasekhar_limit ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Arthur_Eddington ; https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Chandrasekhar_Eddington/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Subrahmanyan_Chandrasekhar

F25. "Niels Bohr, Fowler, Wolfgang Pauli, and other physicists agreed with Chandrasekhar's analysis", but "owing to Eddington's status, they were unwilling to publicly support Chandrasekhar" (Wikipedia). Eddington "never recanted" and died in 1944 (Grokipedia). Chandrasekhar moved to Yerkes Observatory of the University of Chicago, arriving 21 December 1936, and stayed at Chicago for the rest of his career.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrasekhar_limit ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Subrahmanyan_Chandrasekhar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subrahmanyan_Chandrasekhar

F26. The evidence came round to Chandrasekhar: the limit was "initially met with skepticism but later validated" (accepted by the 1940s-1950s); observed white dwarfs "cluster below 1.4" Suns (average about 0.6); neutron stars were found as pulsars from 1967 (F16) and black holes later (LIGO, 2015). In 1983 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics "equally with William A. Fowler", his share "for his theoretical studies of the physical processes of importance to the structure and evolution of the stars". NASA named the Chandra X-ray Observatory after him (launched 1999).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Chandrasekhar_limit ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Subrahmanyan_Chandrasekhar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrasekhar_limit ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subrahmanyan_Chandrasekhar

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. "Most or all stars blow up at the end of their lifetimes. Our Sun will one day blow up." Mass decides; stars under about 8 Suns do not become supernovae, and students confuse the red giant phase with an explosion. (Rubin Observatory teacher guide)
    https://rubinobservatory.org/education/educators/investigations/exploding-stars/teacher-guide/student-ideas
M2. Students often think the hottest stars are the red ones, linking red to a wood fire (IAU Symposium proceedings, "Students' preconceptions in astrophysics", doi 10.1017/S1743921321000491, as summarised in search; the page itself could not be read). The truth is the reverse (F18).
    https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C54354AA584BBBBBB37722D4B453B561/S1743921321000491a.pdf/students-preconceptions-in-astrophysics-how-to-break-them-down.pdf
M3. A planetary nebula has nothing to do with planets; the name is Herschel's, from its look (F8).
M4. A "nova" or "guest star" sounds like a new star being born (F15); SN 1054 was an old star's death (F14).
M5. A bigger star, with more fuel, should last longer: the intuition the true answer breaks (F6: more fuel, burned much faster).
M6. Russell's own early reading of the H-R diagram as an age sequence, stars moving down the main sequence as they age (F22): a respectable wrong theory of the past.

## Left out, or not sure

- Eddington's fuller 1935 words (the star contracting to "a few km. radius") could not be found in any source read; only the "law of Nature" sentence is used, and only in indirect speech.
- Whether Chandrasekhar was first to the limit: Stoner and Anderson (1929) had related results (F23); no question says "first".
- Sirius B's luminosity differs between sources (0.024 or 0.056 of the Sun's): a question says only "less than a tenth".
- Betelgeuse's distance is uncertain (400 to 724 light-years); every estimate is nearer than Rigel's (about 850). A wrong choice rests only on "Rigel is far closer", false by every figure.
- Neutron-star size: Grokipedia's Pulsar page gives a radius of 10-14 km (20-28 km across); its Crab Nebula page says "roughly 20 km across". A context says "about 20 kilometres across".
- The SN 1054 position in the Chinese record is debated (F14); the stem says "near that place in the sky".
- Hertzsprung plotted the magnitudes (brightness) of cluster stars against colour, Russell absolute magnitude against spectral type; the H-R question simplifies both to "true brightness against surface temperature", which is what the axes mean (F19, F21).
- Ryle's share of the 1974 Nobel Prize was not for pulsars, so the Bell context names only Hewish as cited for the discovery.
- Fusion, the Sun's age and element-making are the T5 unit how-the-sun-shines's; spectra are light-from-atoms's; neither is keyed here.
