## The Radium Girls

F1. The United States Radium Corporation (USRC) ran its radium plant in Orange, New Jersey, from 1917 to 1926; the Radium Dial Company was set up in Ottawa, Illinois, in 1922. Young women, often teenagers, painted luminous dials there from about 1917.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_Girls ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Radium_Girls ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Radium

F2. The painters were "instructed to 'point' their brushes on their lips"; supervisors "encouraged their workers to shape the brushes' point with their lips ('lip, dip, paint')" (Wikipedia). "The workers had been told the paint was harmless" (Wikipedia, Radium Girls; the same words for Ottawa in Wikipedia, Radium Dial Company). Management and scientists at USRC "took precautions such as masks, gloves, and screens, but did not similarly equip the workers" (Wikipedia, USRC).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_Girls ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_Dial_Company ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Radium_Corporation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Radium_Girls ("lip-pointing")

F3. Radium belongs to the alkaline earth metals (the same column of the periodic table as calcium); by "its chemical analogy to calcium" it "rapidly deposits in bone mineral" (Grokipedia, Radium). "It looks to the body like calcium, remarkably like calcium. It goes everywhere calcium goes" (Robert Rowland, radium-in-humans researcher, DOE oral history). "The human body mistakes radium for calcium, so it filled their bones as calcium would, irradiating them from within" (Atomic Heritage Foundation, 2017). Radium "is chemically similar to calcium, and is therefore a 'bone seeker'"; once swallowed, radium-226 "accumulates in the long bones" and irradiates nearby cells with "short-distance alpha radiation" (Memorial University biology page).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Radium ; https://ehss.energy.gov/OHRE/roadmap/histories/0461/0461_b.html ; https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/history/radium-girls/ ; https://mun.ca/biology/scarr/Radium_Watch-Dial_Painters.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_jaw ("incorporate the radioactive metal into bone tissue in place of calcium")

F4. Radium-226, the most stable isotope, has a half-life of about 1,600 years, and its damage "stems from radium's alpha-particle decay"; so radium built into bone goes on irradiating the cells around it for the rest of a life. Radium "decays to produce radon gas" (EPA).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Radium ; https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-radium

F5. Wrong-choice anchors for F3 (each true of its own element, not of radium): the thyroid concentrates iodine ("selective concentration in the thyroid gland occurs via the sodium-iodide symporter", Grokipedia, Iodine-131); iron sits in the haemoglobin of red blood cells (each heme group's iron, Grokipedia, Hemoglobin); fat-soluble vitamins "are primarily stored in the liver and adipose tissues" (Grokipedia, Vitamin).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Iodine-131 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Hemoglobin ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Vitamin

F6. Harrison Martland, the Essex County (Newark) physician, "published a report in 1925 that identified the radioactive material the women had ingested as the cause" of their bone disease and anaemia (Wikipedia, USRC). JAMA 1925, vol. 85, "Some unrecognized dangers in the use and handling of radioactive substances" (Martland, Conlon and Knef).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Radium_Corporation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrison_Stanford_Martland ; https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/1153583

F7. Cecil Drinker of the Harvard School of Public Health. "Company President Arthur Roeder contacted industrial hygiene expert Cecil Drinker to investigate"; "the factory was saturated with radium-contaminated dust"; "Drinker was convinced that exposure to continuous doses of radium was causing the women's health problems". Roeder "insisted that a contagious infection contracted outside the factory must be to blame", cited an internal report he would not show Drinker, and threatened to sue when Drinker planned to publish. Alice Hamilton learned through the National Consumers League that "U.S. Radium had submitted Cecil Drinker's report to the New Jersey Department of Labor" with the findings altered; she wrote to Katherine Drinker in 1925 that the department's copy showed "'every girl is in perfect condition'" and asked "Do you suppose Roeder could do such a thing as to issue a forged report in your name?" The Drinkers then published the real report.
    https://hsph.harvard.edu/news/centennial-radium-forged-report/ (Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, 2013, updated 2024) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_Girls (after receiving the full report the labor commissioner ordered the recommended safety measures)

F8. Grace Fryer, Edna Hussman, Katherine Schaub, Quinta McDonald and Albina Larice sued USRC in New Jersey (filed 1927); the case came to court in 1928 and was settled out of court that year: each received $10,000, a $600 yearly annuity, and medical and legal costs. At the trial (April 1928) the physicist Elizabeth Hughes, formerly of the National Bureau of Standards, testified on her breath tests: each woman breathed through bottles that let only air and radon through, and her gold-leaf electroscope moved "at least twice as fast as the normal drift rate she had measured with controls" (NIST, 2022).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Fryer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_Girls ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Radium_Girls ; https://nist.gov/blogs/taking-measure/new-jerseys-radium-girls-and-nist-trained-scientist-who-came-their-aid (Ron Cowen, NIST, 16 March 2022)

F9. Catherine Wolfe Donohue's case against the Radium Dial Company in Illinois: hearing before the Illinois Industrial Commission in 1937; an arbitrator ruled for her on 5 April 1938; she died on 27 July 1938; on 23 October 1939 the Supreme Court declined the company's last appeal.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Radium_Girls ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_Girls ; https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/2019/03/radium-girls-living-dead-women/

F10. Robley Evans of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology "made the first measurements of exhaled radon and radium excretion from a former dial painter in 1933" and collected body-content measurements from dial painters (Wikipedia). "By 1941, Evans with others had set the first standards for a tolerance level for radium in the human body. The first 'tolerance level' for radium was set at 0.1 microgram body burden"; "Evans judged that there would be no bone cancers below 0.1 microgram 226Ra in the skeleton" (DOE footnote). The committee he chaired, formed just before the war mainly at the Navy's request, agreed "that 0.1 microgram fixed in the body should be the 'tolerance level' for radium" (DOE Advisory Committee on Human Radiation Experiments, ch. 5). Wikipedia gives the 1941 National Bureau of Standards level as 0.1 microcurie (about the same amount of radium-226).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_Girls ; https://ehss.energy.gov/ohre/roadmap/histories/0473/footnote.html ; https://ehss.energy.gov/OHRE/roadmap/achre/chap5_1.html

F11. The Manhattan District's first, tentative maximum body burden for plutonium (5 micrograms, 1943 to spring 1945) "was derived by direct comparison of the relative energies of plutonium and radium" (with early animal results); Louis Hempelmann of Los Alamos recalled that the project's doctors visited a radium dial plant in Boston: "So our procedures were patterned after that."
    https://ehss.energy.gov/OHRE/roadmap/achre/chap5_1.html ; https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/history/radium-girls/

## Thalidomide and Frances Kelsey

F12. Chemie Grünenthal launched thalidomide as Contergan in West Germany on 1 October 1957, where it was at first sold without a prescription; it was marketed as a sedative and a remedy for morning sickness and called "non-toxic even at high doses" (Grokipedia). It was "tested for toxicity on rodents only, as was usual at the time", and "never tested on pregnant women" (Wikipedia). Rodent tests showed "no lethal effects even at doses exceeding 10 grams per kilogram"; "Reproductive toxicity testing in pregnant animals was not conducted" (Grokipedia). It was sold or licensed in more than 40 countries (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: prescribed in 46).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalidomide ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thalidomide ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thalidomide_scandal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalidomide_scandal

F13. Rodents "proved insensitive to the drug's impact on limb development at typical doses"; it "induced phocomelia in sensitive species like New Zealand white rabbits and certain primates" in later work (Grokipedia). George Somers of Distillers found fetal abnormalities in rabbits (Wikipedia, Thalidomide scandal).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thalidomide ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalidomide_scandal

F14. The harm (one clause in the bank): taken early in pregnancy (about days 20 to 36 after fertilisation), thalidomide caused babies to be born with shortened or missing limbs; more than 10,000 babies were affected worldwide, about 40 percent of whom died at or soon after birth. Widukind Lenz (Germany) and William McBride (Australia) linked the drug to the defects late in 1961 (Lenz on 11 November 1961; McBride in The Lancet, 16 December 1961); Grünenthal withdrew it in late November 1961.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalidomide ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalidomide_scandal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thalidomide_scandal

F15. Thalidomide commonly causes peripheral neuropathy (nerve damage) in adults: "paresthesias, numbness, tingling, and pain in the extremities" (Grokipedia). "In late 1959, it was noticed that peripheral neuritis developed in patients who took the drug over a period of time" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thalidomide ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalidomide

F16. Frances Oldham Kelsey joined the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a medical officer on 1 August 1960; Richardson-Merrell's application for thalidomide (Kevadon) was submitted on 8 September 1960 and was "one of the first applications she was assigned"; the drug was then "already available in dozens of countries around the world".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Frances_Oldham_Kelsey ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Oldham_Kelsey ; https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/fda-history-exhibits/frances-oldham-kelsey-medical-reviewer-famous-averting-public-health-tragedy

F17. Under the 1938 Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, a company had to show only that a new drug was safe ("The original Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act only required that a new drug be safe", ASU Embryo Project). The FDA "could only withhold approval for 60 days at a time, so she continually requested further information" (Wikipedia); on 10 November 1960 she declared the application incomplete, which reset the 60-day clock (Grokipedia). "In order to deny it, she had to be able to prove it was unsafe" (Scientific American, 2024).
    https://embryo.asu.edu/pages/us-regulatory-response-thalidomide-1950-2000 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Oldham_Kelsey ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Frances_Oldham_Kelsey ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/was-thalidomide-safe-frances-oldham-kelsey-was-not-convinced/

F18. Her grounds were the evidence: she "refused to approve the application because of its inadequate evidence" and "adamantly insisted on scientifically reliable evidence, which she felt the application sorely lacked" (FDA history exhibit); she "noted the reliance on anecdotal testimony in place of clinical data" and "insisted on hard evidence to back Merrell's claims for the drug's safety" (University of Chicago Medicine, 2020); the file had "inadequate clinical evidence relying on testimonials rather than controlled studies", "inconsistent pharmacokinetic data on absorption", and incomplete animal studies (Grokipedia). The link to birth defects came "about a year later" (FDA exhibit), so it was not among her 1960 grounds.
    https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/fda-history-exhibits/frances-oldham-kelsey-medical-reviewer-famous-averting-public-health-tragedy ; https://www.uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/biological-sciences-articles/2020/march/courageous-physician-scientist-saved-the-us-from-a-birth-defects-catastrophe ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Frances_Oldham_Kelsey

F19. A. Leslie Florence's letter in the British Medical Journal (dated 31 December 1960) described four of his patients, each "taking a nightly dose of thalidomide for more than a year", with a strange set of symptoms: numbness and tingling, peripheral neuritis (Scientific American, 2024). Kelsey read it early in 1961. The letter "prompted Kelsey to request, with grim prescience, proof the drug was not harmful to the fetus" (UChicago Medicine); "she also requested animal studies to demonstrate that the drug would not be harmful to the fetus" (Wikipedia); Merrell's claim that the drug did not cross the placenta "lacked substantiation" (Grokipedia).
    https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/was-thalidomide-safe-frances-oldham-kelsey-was-not-convinced/ (Hafner, Wyman, Feder, 19 September 2024) ; https://www.uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/biological-sciences-articles/2020/march/courageous-physician-scientist-saved-the-us-from-a-birth-defects-catastrophe ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Oldham_Kelsey ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Frances_Oldham_Kelsey

F19b. Kelsey's own reading of the letter, as reported (WETA, 2026, from her memoir): the neuritis "did not seem the sort of side effect that should come from a simple sleeping pill" (the blog prints "short", a typo). Her primary text (FDA, "Autobiographical Reflections") could not be read here, so the bank paraphrases and does not quote it.
    https://blogs.weta.org/2026/04/24/frances-oldham-kelsey-how-fda-pharmacologist-prevented-thalidomide-becoming-national

F20. Kelsey wrote to Merrell's Joseph Murray on 5 May 1961: "The burden of proof that the drug is safe ... lies with the applicant" (UChicago Medicine). The company's contact pressed for speed and complained to her superiors, who backed her (Wikipedia; Scientific American).
    https://www.uchicagomedicine.org/forefront/biological-sciences-articles/2020/march/courageous-physician-scientist-saved-the-us-from-a-birth-defects-catastrophe ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Oldham_Kelsey ; https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/was-thalidomide-safe-frances-oldham-kelsey-was-not-convinced/

F21. Merrell withdrew its application in March 1962. Before that it had sent out more than 2.5 million tablets to more than 1,000 doctors as "experimental" samples, given to nearly 20,000 patients; at least 17 US babies were confirmed with thalidomide defects (Wikipedia; UChicago: 17, ten linked to Kevadon). Kelsey received the President's Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service from John F. Kennedy on 7 August 1962, and credited her colleagues Jiro Oyama and Lee Geismar.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalidomide_scandal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Oldham_Kelsey ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Frances_Oldham_Kelsey

F22. The Kefauver-Harris Amendments, signed by Kennedy on 10 October 1962, "introduced a 'proof-of-efficacy' requirement for the first time"; "Informed consent was required of patients participating in clinical trials"; "adverse drug reactions were required to be reported to the FDA" (Wikipedia). The statute defines "substantial evidence" as "evidence consisting of adequate and well-controlled investigations" by qualified experts, on which they could conclude that the drug "will have the effect it purports or is represented to have" (21 U.S.C. 355(d)); the law asks for the claimed effect, not for proof that a drug beats older ones. The grounds on which 355(d) lets the FDA refuse an application are safety, the substantial evidence of effect, manufacturing controls, patent information and labelling (read 2026-11-27); approval elsewhere and price are not among them (wrong-choice anchors).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kefauver%E2%80%93Harris_Amendment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kefauver%E2%80%93Harris_Amendment ; https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/355

F23. Attributed critique: Sam Peltzman, "An Evaluation of Consumer Protection Legislation: The 1962 Drug Amendments", Journal of Political Economy 81 (1973), found a marked drop in new drugs introduced after 1962 and blamed the cost of the new rules; critics call it the "drug lag" (Grokipedia: new chemical entities fell from a pre-1962 average of 43 a year to 16). Defenders answer that the framework improved drug quality and prevents thalidomide-scale harm (Grokipedia).
    https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/260107 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Kefauver%E2%80%93Harris_Amendment ; https://reason.com/1976/03/01/spotlight-9/

## The Tuskegee study (the US Public Health Service, 1932-1972)

F24. The US Public Health Service (USPHS), working with the Tuskegee Institute, began in 1932 "a study to record the natural history of syphilis" in Macon County, Alabama (CDC); its stated purpose was "to observe the effects of the disease when untreated, to the point of death and autopsy" (Wikipedia). It enrolled 600 Black men: 399 with latent syphilis and 201 without, as controls (CDC; Grokipedia). The 399 "were initially recruited because they already had late-latent syphilis" (CDC): the doctors did not give them the disease.
    https://www.cdc.gov/tuskegee/about/index.html ; https://www.cdc.gov/tuskegee/about/timeline.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study

F25. The men were told they were being treated for "bad blood", a local term covering syphilis, anaemia and fatigue; they were given free physicals, hot meals on examination days and funeral expenses; "There is no evidence that researchers obtained informed consent from participants" (CDC).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study ; https://www.cdc.gov/tuskegee/about/index.html

F26. Steel-man of 1932: the treatments of the day, compounds of arsenic and mercury, were "mildly effective and highly toxic" (Wikipedia; Grokipedia: "the inherent toxicities of available treatments"); a Rosenwald Fund treatment programme had lost its money in the Depression (Grokipedia); the researchers "reasoned that the knowledge gained would benefit humankind" (Wikipedia) and framed the work as a comparison for the earlier Oslo study of untreated syphilis.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study

F27. Penicillin: "By 1943, penicillin emerged as an effective antibiotic therapy"; "In 1947, following the formal recommendation of penicillin as standard syphilis therapy" (Grokipedia); CDC timeline: by 1943 penicillin "was the treatment of choice for syphilis", but the men "were not offered treatment"; Wikipedia: standard therapy by 1947 and "None of the infected men were treated with penicillin". USPHS officers gave local doctors lists of the men and asked them not to treat them (Vonderlehr, 1934), and kept them from treatment through the draft in the Second World War (Wikipedia; Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study ; https://www.cdc.gov/tuskegee/about/timeline.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study

F28. "The study was not secret, since reports and data sets were published to the medical community throughout its duration" (Wikipedia; first major report 1936).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study

F29. Peter Buxtun was hired by the USPHS in December 1965 in San Francisco "to interview patients with sexually transmitted diseases" (a venereal-disease investigator); he lodged ethics complaints in 1966 and again in 1968, and both were rejected (Wikipedia, Peter Buxtun; Grokipedia). A committee at the federal Center for Disease Control "decided in 1969 that the study should be continued" (Brandt, Hastings Center Report 1978, as quoted); "decided to continue the study until all study participants had died and been autopsied" (Barrett, DttP); Grokipedia: the February 1969 panel held that the study "warranted continuation" for "the unique opportunity to study untreated late-stage syphilis".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Buxtun ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Peter_Buxtun ; https://alatest.pkpps03.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/dttp/article/download/7213/9852?inline=1 (Laura A. Barrett) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study

F30. Buxtun "leaked information on the Tuskegee experiment to Jean Heller of the Associated Press"; her story ran in the Washington Star on 25 July 1972 and on the front page of The New York Times the next day ("Syphilis Victims in U.S. Study Went Untreated for 40 Years"). After the news, an Ad Hoc Advisory Panel concluded the study was "ethically unjustified" and advised stopping it in October 1972; the study was officially ended on 16 November 1972 (CDC; Wikipedia; Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Buxtun ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study ; https://www.cdc.gov/tuskegee/about/timeline.html ; https://www.cdc.gov/tuskegee/about/index.html

F31. When the study ended in 1972, 74 of the men were still alive; the last participant died in 2004 (Wikipedia). No court ordered the study's end: the class action for the men came in 1973 and was settled for $10 million in 1974 (CDC). At least 40 wives were infected and 19 children were born with congenital syphilis (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study ; https://www.cdc.gov/tuskegee/about/index.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study

F32. The National Research Act, signed on 12 July 1974, "gained traction as a response to the infamous Tuskegee syphilis study"; it "formalized a regulated IRB process through local institutional review boards" and "created the National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research" (Wikipedia). "IRBs were initially created by The National Research Act of 1974" (Barrett). Federal regulations "mandate Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) for oversight of human subjects research funded by federal agencies" (Grokipedia, Belmont Report). Wikipedia (Tuskegee Syphilis Study): "Institutional review boards (IRBs), including laypeople, are established" to review protocols and protect participants.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Research_Act ; https://alatest.pkpps03.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/dttp/article/download/7213/9852?inline=1 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Belmont_Report

F33. The Commission's Belmont Report (1979) set out three principles, "respect for persons, beneficence, and justice"; respect for persons is applied through informed consent, with three elements: information, comprehension and voluntariness ("Voluntariness requires that participation proceed solely from the subject's free choice") (Grokipedia). President Clinton apologised for the study on 16 May 1997 (CDC).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Belmont_Report ; https://www.cdc.gov/tuskegee/about/timeline.html

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Kelsey refused thalidomide because she knew, or foresaw, that it harmed babies. She did not: her 1960 grounds were missing evidence of safety (F18); the birth-defect link came about a year later (F14, F18).
M2. A drug that animals survive in huge doses is safe. Grünenthal's rodent tests found no lethal dose but tested no pregnant animals, and rats and mice were insensitive to the harm (F12, F13).
M3. The FDA requires a new drug to work better than older drugs. The 1962 law requires substantial evidence of the effect claimed (F22).
M4. The Tuskegee doctors infected the men with syphilis. The 399 already had it when recruited (F24). (Deliberate infection belongs to the USPHS's Guatemala experiments of 1946-48, not used here.)
M5. Tuskegee was a secret study. Its reports were published in medical journals for decades (F28).
M6. Swallowed radium works like an ordinary poison that passes through. The body builds it into bone like calcium, where it keeps radiating (F3, F4); other elements have other homes (F5).
M7. A court ordered the Tuskegee study shut, or the men had all died by 1972 (F30, F31).

F34. The 1938 law, as it really worked: under the 1938 Act an application "was to become effective within a fixed period unless the agency after notice and opportunity for hearing refused to permit it to become effective, finding that it could not determine from existing evidence or had not been shown that it was safe", and it "automatically became effective if it was not disapproved" (US Supreme Court, Weinberger v. Hynson, 412 U.S. 609, 1973). The period was 60 days: "At the time, FDA regulators had sixty days to approve or reject a new drug ... And if they didn't do anything, the drug automatically became legal" (Science History Institute); "If the agency was not satisfied the drug was safe, it had a 60-day window in which to reject the application" (UChicago Medicine); her letter of 10 November 1960 "reset the statutory 60-day review period" (Grokipedia). So the FDA could refuse a drug not shown to be safe; F17's last quotation ("she had to be able to prove it was unsafe", Scientific American) is wrong on the law and is no longer used. (The date could also be postponed, which may be ASU's "six months"; the bank says only 60 days.)

F35. Kelsey's own earlier research and the letter: in her wartime search for a synthetic cure for malaria "Oldham learned that some drugs are able to pass through the placental barrier"; the neuritis letter "caused her to recall her earlier work on the mechanism of birth defects", and she "also requested animal studies to demonstrate that the drug would not be harmful to the fetus" (Wikipedia). "Her prior research on quinine's placental transfer informed her skepticism toward such assertions without empirical proof", the assertion being Merrell's that the drug did not cross the placental barrier (Grokipedia). Her wartime antimalarial research "sensitized her to the distinct effect of drugs on fetuses" (UChicago Medicine); "infant rabbits couldn't metabolize the drugs" (Science History Institute). The bank uses only the plain finding (some drugs pass the placenta to the unborn), which these sources share; her own account is still unread, so no detail of the rabbit work is keyed (see "Left out").

F38. Wrong-choice anchors (what the 1962 law does not ask): "Because all medications can have side effects, which can range from mild to severe," "FDA concludes that a medication is 'safe' if its benefits are expected to outweigh its risks" (FDA, benefit-risk guidance recap). With F22 (355(d): substantial evidence that the drug "will have the effect it purports or is represented to have"), the law asks for the claimed effect: not a cure, not a win over older drugs, not freedom from side effects, and not an effect in every patient ("Not everyone responds to a medication in the same way", National Human Genome Research Institute, Pharmacogenomics fact sheet).

F41. The Tuskegee plan: "Researchers initially designed the project to last only six to eight months"; they then "extended the project for an indefinite amount of time" (ASU Embryo Project). So "left untreated" is true of the first plan and the extension; "never treated" and a lifelong plan are not true of 1932.
