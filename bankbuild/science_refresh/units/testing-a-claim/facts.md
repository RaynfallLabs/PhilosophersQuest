## Franz Mesmer and the claim

F1. Franz Anton Mesmer (1734-1815) was a German physician who moved to Paris in early 1778. He taught that an invisible fluid runs through all living things (Grokipedia: "a subtle, invisible universal fluid permeating all matter and living beings"), that illness comes from obstructions or imbalances in its flow, and that a healer can set it flowing again; he called it animal magnetism.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Franz_Mesmer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Animal_magnetism ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Mesmer

F2. Mesmer treated groups at the baquet, a tub with water, iron filings and iron rods that the patients applied to their bodies. The treatment aimed to bring on a "crisis" of convulsions, sweating and fits, which he held restored health. In 1774 he had used real iron magnets on a patient (Francisca Österlin) before deciding the cure came from animal magnetism instead, and he soon stopped using magnets.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Franz_Mesmer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Animal_magnetism ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Mesmer

## The Franklin commission of 1784

F3. Louis XVI appointed the commission on 12 March 1784. Its members included Benjamin Franklin, the chemist Antoine Lavoisier, the astronomer Jean Sylvain Bailly and the physician Joseph-Ignace Guillotin; Franklin headed it. Franklin was 78, and ill health kept him at his house in Passy, where many of the trials were held.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism ; https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/mesmerising-science-the-franklin-commission-and-the-modern-clinical-trial ; https://www.americanheritage.com/franklins-forgotten-triumph-scientific-testing

F4. Mesmer "refused to have any communication with these gentlemen" (Godwin, quoted by Wikipedia). The commission tested instead the practice of Charles d'Eslon, a physician and "the only physician of high standing to become Mesmer's disciple" (Wikipedia, Franz Mesmer), who agreed to help.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Mesmer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Franz_Mesmer

F5. The design: the commissioners took two possible causes of the fits, a physical fluid and the patients' imagination, and tested each alone. Lavoisier's plan "involved magnetizing people without their knowledge, or persuading them they were magnetized when they were not" (Wikipedia). Subjects were blindfolded or screened. The James Lind Library calls these "the first known experiments using blind comparisons"; Grokipedia calls them "one of the earliest documented uses of blinding".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism ; https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/mesmerising-science-the-franklin-commission-and-the-modern-clinical-trial ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/?p=14283 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Blinded_experiment

F6. The results: the effects appeared "only ... when the subjects believed" they were magnetised (Wikipedia). Dame P-, blindfolded and told that d'Eslon was magnetising her, though "he was not even around", fell into a crisis within about three minutes. Mademoiselle B- "was magnetised for a full half hour from behind a paper partition" while she chatted with a commissioner, and showed no reaction; told on another occasion that d'Eslon was behind a door, she fell into a crisis. (Public Domain Review.) Grokipedia: effects "vanished when patients doubted treatment".
    https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/mesmerising-science-the-franklin-commission-and-the-modern-clinical-trial ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism

F7. The tree at Passy: d'Eslon magnetised one tree (an apricot) in Franklin's garden. The trial "was discussed in advance with Deslon, who was to bring a subject already shown to be 'sensitive' to magnetism" (James Lind Library); the boy was about twelve and "of his choosing" (Public Domain Review). The boy was blindfolded (Grokipedia; American Heritage), hugged several trees for two minutes each, none of them magnetised, and fell into a crisis at the fourth (Public Domain Review; Wikipedia: he fainted "at the foot of one of the other four").
    https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/mesmerising-science-the-franklin-commission-and-the-modern-clinical-trial ; https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/?p=14283 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism ; https://www.americanheritage.com/franklins-forgotten-triumph-scientific-testing

F8. Cups: at Lavoisier's house a normal cup of water swallowed by a subject who believed it magnetised produced "magnetic" effects at once (Wikipedia); dame P- had a crisis at the fourth of several cups she believed magnetised (Public Domain Review).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism ; https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/mesmerising-science-the-franklin-commission-and-the-modern-clinical-trial

F9. The conclusion: the public report said "nothing proves the existence of animal magnetism" and put the effects down to imagination, aided by touch and imitation (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: "touch, imagination, [and] imitation"). The courtesy report: "Sound physics does not permit recourse to an unknown and insensible fluid." The report circulated in "at least 20,000 copies" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Animal_magnetism

F10. Afterward d'Eslon "accepted the main findings" (Public Domain Review) and argued that "the imagination thus directed to the relief of suffering humanity" could be of value to medicine (Wikipedia, Franz Mesmer). Mesmer, who had refused to take part, later blamed the negative results on technique (Grokipedia).
    https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/mesmerising-science-the-franklin-commission-and-the-modern-clinical-trial ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Mesmer ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Franz_Mesmer

## Placebo and blinding, in plain words

F11. A placebo is "an inactive substance or sham intervention that simulates an active treatment"; the placebo effect is improvement or relief that comes from "the patient's anticipation of therapeutic benefit" rather than from the treatment itself, mostly in felt symptoms such as pain or nausea (Grokipedia). Grokipedia (Animal magnetism) explains the mesmerists' effects "through the mechanisms of placebo response and psychological suggestion"; Wikipedia (Franz Mesmer) calls the commission's finding the first observation of the placebo effect, while Wikipedia (Royal Commission) warns that the modern placebo framing reads a later idea back into 1784. The unit therefore says only that the fits were the kind of effect now called a placebo effect.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Placebo ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Animal_magnetism ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Mesmer ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_Animal_Magnetism

F12. A blinded experiment withholds information that could sway the result from the participants, the researchers or both; "The primary purpose of blinding is to reduce expectancy effects", and it also limits "observer bias" and "conscious or unconscious influences on the outcomes" (Grokipedia). Single-blind means only the participants are kept from knowing.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Blinded_experiment

## Clever Hans (1904-1907)

F13. Wilhelm von Osten, a retired mathematics teacher in Berlin (Grokipedia; Wikipedia: a gymnasium mathematics teacher), showed his horse Hans across Germany without charging admission. Hans answered by tapping his hoof and was said to add, subtract, multiply, divide, work fractions, tell time, read, spell and understand German. Von Osten was "genuinely convinced of the animal's abilities" with no "apparent intent to deceive" (Grokipedia); "He was not a hoaxer" (Skeptoid). He "was never persuaded by Pfungst's findings" and kept touring (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Clever_Hans ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clever_Hans ; https://skeptoid.com/episodes/633

F14. A panel of 13 led by Carl Stumpf, a philosopher and psychologist, "consisted of a veterinarian, a circus manager, a cavalry officer, a number of schoolteachers, and the director of the Berlin zoological gardens"; it "concluded in September 1904 that no tricks were involved in Hans's performance" (Wikipedia). Grokipedia: "no intentional trickery was involved, though it could not fully explain the horse's successes".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clever_Hans ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Clever_Hans

F15. Oskar Pfungst, a volunteer assistant in Stumpf's laboratory in Berlin, investigated Hans (experiments from late 1904; book 1907, English translation 1911). He isolated horse and questioner from spectators, used questioners other than von Osten, used blinders to vary whether Hans could see the questioner, and varied whether the questioner knew the answer (Wikipedia). Hans answered "queries from strangers", and "Even when von Osten stepped away, the horse continued to provide answers" (Grokipedia); "the horse could get the correct answer even if von Osten himself did not ask the questions", which ruled out fraud (Wikipedia). Written words: right when the questioner knew the word, wrong when not (Skeptoid).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clever_Hans ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Pfungst ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Clever_Hans ; https://skeptoid.com/episodes/633

F16. Hans got the right answer only when the questioner knew it and Hans could see him. With the questioner in sight he answered 50 of 56 (89 percent) rightly; with the questioner out of sight, 2 of 35 (6 percent) (Wikipedia; Grokipedia gives the same 89 and 6 percent, with blinders on in both). On number tests: 98 percent right when the questioner knew the number, 8 percent when he did not (Skeptoid; Grokipedia gives the same pair).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clever_Hans ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Clever_Hans ; https://skeptoid.com/episodes/633
    Blinders (blinkers) are "cup-shaped devices or shields" beside a horse's eyes that block the side and rear of its view (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Blinkers_(horse_tack)

F17. The cue: as the taps neared the right count, the questioner's "posture and facial expression changed in ways that were consistent with an increase in tension, which was released when the horse made the final, correct tap"; Pfungst often saw "a sudden slight upward jerk of the head" at the final tap (Wikipedia). Grokipedia: "forward leans of the body" as the tapping went on and "upward jerks of the head (averaging 1 mm in movement) to signal cessation". PMC (Schmidjell et al. 2012): cues such as "head jerks or body orientation".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clever_Hans ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Clever_Hans ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3530188

F18. The cues were given without knowing: "The trainer was entirely unaware that he was providing such clues" (Wikipedia, Oskar Pfungst). Pfungst, replicating the effect as questioner, found himself "unwittingly providing similar cues himself" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia says he produced them "involuntarily regardless of whether he wished to exhibit or suppress them" (tagged citation needed); Britannica (as quoted in its search excerpt) says that among questioners who knew the answer "not one was able to suppress these tiny physical cues". Pfungst also played the part of the horse in laboratory tests with human subjects (Wikipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Pfungst ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Clever_Hans ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clever_Hans ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Clever-Hans

F19. The "Clever Hans effect" names a subject's response to unintended cues from the experimenter. It "has had a large effect on experimental design": the person who knows the answer is kept from the subject, or replaced by a machine (Wikipedia); animal studies avoid face-to-face contact or use cameras (Grokipedia), or blindfold the handler (PMC 2012).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clever_Hans ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Clever_Hans ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3530188

## Emily Rosa and therapeutic touch (1996-1998)

F20. Therapeutic touch was developed in the early 1970s by Dolores Krieger, a nurse and New York University professor, with Dora Kunz. Practitioners place their hands on or near a patient to "detect and manipulate what they say is the patient's energy field" (Wikipedia), the "human energy field". JAMA called it "a widely used nursing practice".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Emily_Rosa ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapeutic_touch ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9533499/

F21. Emily Rosa was born on 6 February 1987 and designed the test at age nine for her fourth-grade science fair; JAMA: "The first series of tests was conducted when she was 9 years old." First series, 1996: 15 practitioners tested at their homes or offices over several months. Second series, 1997: 13 practitioners, 7 of them from the first, tested in a single day and videotaped for a public-television producer. Her paper appeared in JAMA (Journal of the American Medical Association) on 1 April 1998, when she was 11; she is recorded as the youngest person to publish research in a scientific or medical journal (Guinness, 1999).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Emily_Rosa ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Rosa ; https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/187390

F22. The set-up: "a tall, opaque screen with cutouts at its base was placed over the subject's arms", with "a cloth towel ... draped over them"; "The experimenter flipped a coin to determine which of the subject's hands would be the target"; Emily "hovered her right hand, palm down, 8 to 10 cm above the target"; the practitioner stated which hand it was above. Before testing, "all participants said they could use TT to significant therapeutic advantage", all but one "expressed high confidence in their TT abilities", and each "described sensory cues they used". In 1997 each was first "allowed to 'feel' the investigator's energy field". (JAMA.)
    https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/187390 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9533499/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapeutic_touch

F23. Success set in advance: "We decided in advance that an individual would pass by making 8 or more correct selections" out of 10, and the group's result would not count as positive unless the average score was above 6.7 (at a 90 percent confidence level). (JAMA.)
    https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/187390

F24. The results: "Practitioners of TT identified the correct hand in only 123 (44%) of 280 trials ... which is close to what would be expected for random chance" (PubMed abstract). The authors wrote that a true ability should find the hand "100% of the time" and that "A score of 50% would be expected through chance alone". Fourteen practitioners were tested 10 times and seven 20 times. Scores showed no significant link to years of experience (1 to 27 years). Wikipedia (Therapeutic touch): 44 percent is "lower than, though within the range of, mere chance". Wikipedia (Emily Rosa): an average of 4.4 right in 10.
    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9533499/ ; https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/187390 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapeutic_touch ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Rosa

F25. The authors concluded that "TT claims are groundless and that further use of TT by health professionals is unjustified"; their logic: "Both TT theory and technique require that an HEF be felt in order to impart any therapeutic benefit". The paper's authors were Linda Rosa, Emily Rosa, Larry Sarner and Stephen Barrett. JAMA's editor George Lundberg: "Age doesn't matter. It's good science that matters, and this is good science." Proponents objected (a "parlor game", no double-blinding, 21 subjects; Grokipedia); not used in a key.
    https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9533499/ ; https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/187390 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Rosa ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Emily_Rosa

## A test announced in advance (the general idea)

F26. Preregistration is "publicly registering a study's hypotheses, research design, methods, and analysis plan prior to data collection", with "decision rules for interpreting results"; it guards against changing the claim after the data are in ("hypothesizing after the results are known") (Grokipedia). The James Randi foundation's tests of paranormal claims used protocols "mutually agreed upon in advance", success "determined solely by whether the predefined conditions were met" (Grokipedia). The Rosa pass mark (F23) is a plain case of the same idea.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Preregistration_(science) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/One_Million_Dollar_Paranormal_Challenge

F27. Chance arithmetic (worked here, not quoted): with two hands to choose from, a pure guess is right half the time, so 280 guesses give about 140 right. Ten guesses reach 8 or more right with probability (45 + 10 + 1)/1024 = 56/1024, about 1 time in 18.

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. "If many people feel a treatment working, it must work" (the testimonial). The 1784 fits were real and felt, yet came from belief (F6, F9).
M2. "No fraud found means the claim is true." The 1904 panel was right that no one cheated (F14, F15) and still Hans could not count (F16-F18).
M3. "Blinding is for catching cheats, so honest people need none." Von Osten was honest (F13) and gave the cue all the same; so did Pfungst, who knew it (F18).
M4. "A score near half shows a weak sense, and a score under half a sense working backward." With two choices, guessing alone scores about half, and 44 percent is within chance's range (F24, F27).
M5. "A placebo effect is fake, imagined, or nothing at all." The felt effect is real; only its cause is belief rather than the treatment (F6, F11).
M6. "Setting the pass mark in advance stops luck." It does not: guessing reaches 8 of 10 about 1 time in 18 (F27); what it stops is moving the mark after the result.

## Left out, or not sure

- The tree's distance from the magnetised one, and whether the tree trial had four or five trees: the sources differ (Wikipedia says one of five trees; American Heritage says four trees, three controls). No question rests on it beyond "a tree d'Eslon never touched".
- Whether the commission's work was the FIRST blind test: sources say "first known" (James Lind Library) or "one of the earliest" (Grokipedia). The unit says "among the first" at most.
- Calling 1784 the first observation of the placebo effect: Wikipedia articles disagree; the unit says only that the fits were the kind of effect now called a placebo effect.
- That Pfungst could not suppress the cue even when trying: Wikipedia tags it citation needed; Britannica says no knowing questioner could. The question keys only what Grokipedia states plainly: he gave the cues unwittingly.
- The 89/6 percent pair is tied by some popular sources to the questioner's knowledge rather than to sight; Wikipedia and Grokipedia tie it to sight (blinders), and the unit follows them.
- The retest of the one practitioner who reached 8 of 10 (she scored 6 on retest) is replication, the T5 unit's; not used.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F28. Years of practice and score (JAMA, 1 April 1998): "The reported practice experience of those tested ranged from 1 to 27 years", and "there was no significant correlation between the practitioners' scores and the length of time they had practiced TT" (r = 0.23). Before testing, "all participants said they could use TT to significant therapeutic advantage", and "Each described sensory cues they used to assess and manipulate the HEF", among them "tingling", "pulling", "throbbing", "hot", "cold" and "spongy". (A weak, non-significant positive r means "a clear link" either way is false; no source reports any pattern by middle years, and the authors report none.)
    https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/187390

F29. The stop cue, re-checked: Wikipedia, Clever Hans: Pfungst "frequently observed 'a sudden slight upward jerk of the head'" at the final tap, which "corresponded to the subject resuming the position they had adopted before thinking of the question"; the cue came from posture and facial expression as tension rose and was released. A Macmillan psychology text says the cue varied from person to person but "commonly involved a slight upward movement of the whole head or a facial feature such as the eyebrows". No source read names a step back, a glance toward a crowd or a lift of the hand as the cue; the lures avoid the face for that reason. Pfungst tested horse and questioner apart from spectators (F15).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clever_Hans ; https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/gray7e/gray7e_ch2_2.html

F30. Chance arithmetic (worked here, not quoted): ten guesses at a two-way choice reach 6 or more right with probability (210 + 120 + 45 + 10 + 1)/1024 = 386/1024, about 38 percent, more than a third of the time; 8 or more with 56/1024, about 1 time in 18 (F27). For 280 guesses the central 95 percent band is about 124 to 156, so 123 sits just below it, which is why the card uses the paper's words, "close to what would be expected for random chance" (F24), and not "within the range of chance".

F31. d'Eslon's answer to the report, where sources disagree: the Public Domain Review says he "seems to have acknowledged the power of the commissioners' approach and accepted the main findings" while he "insisted on its clinical utility", and died two years later, "apparently during a magnetic seance"; Wikipedia (Royal Commission on Animal Magnetism) says "D'Eslon immediately published an attack on the commission's reports", faulting them for ignoring longer-term effects and his cures; Grokipedia says he critiqued the experiments as insufficiently rigorous. Mesmer argued that, as he took no part, the conclusions "had nothing whatsoever to do with" his animal magnetism (Wikipedia), and "disavowed d'Eslon's variant" (Grokipedia). No question keys d'Eslon's answer.
