# How Plants Make Food: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-25. Grokipedia first; Wikipedia, NGSS, Arizona State's Ask A Biologist and Ohio State to corroborate. Curriculum line G5.L3 (NGSS 5-LS1-1, 5-PS3-1).

## Facts

F1 Photosynthesis is the process by which plants (and algae and some bacteria) use light energy to make their own food: they turn the energy of light into chemical energy stored in sugar (glucose). https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ("use light energy"; "convert light energy into chemical energy to synthesize organic compounds"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis

F2 The things a plant takes in to make its food are light energy (usually sunlight), carbon dioxide from the air, and water: 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 (glucose) + 6O2. https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ("The primary inputs for photosynthesis are light energy (typically from sunlight), CO2 absorbed from the atmosphere"; the equation); https://askabiologist.asu.edu/recipe-plant-growth ("plants need water, carbon dioxide (from air), and energy from sunlight")

F3 The food photosynthesis makes is sugar (glucose), the plant's food and its store of energy. https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ("the key outputs are carbohydrates such as glucose for energy storage"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis; curriculum line G5.L3 ("leaves use the Sun's energy and green chlorophyll to make sugar (glucose)")

F4 In most plants the leaves are the main food-making part: the leaf is "the principal organ for photosynthesis", and most of a plant's chloroplasts are in its leaves. https://grokipedia.com/page/Leaf; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis ("the majority of those are found in specially adapted structures called leaves")

F5 Chlorophyll is the green substance (pigment) that catches sunlight for photosynthesis. It sits in the chloroplasts and is most plentiful in leaves; leaves are green because of it. https://grokipedia.com/page/Chlorophyll ("the primary photoreceptor for capturing sunlight during photosynthesis"; "most abundant in leaves"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Leaf ("typically green due to the presence of chlorophyll in its cells")

F6 Chlorophyll soaks up mostly blue and red light and takes in little green light, which is why it, and the leaf, look green. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll ("Plants are perceived as green because chlorophyll absorbs mainly the blue and red wavelengths"; "a poor absorber of green"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Chlorophyll ("particularly in the blue and red wavelengths")

F7 In autumn, shorter days stop a tree from making chlorophyll and the chlorophyll already in its leaves breaks down. That uncovers yellow and orange substances (carotenoids: carotenes and xanthophylls) that were in the leaf all along. https://grokipedia.com/page/Autumn_leaf_color ("shortening days that halt chlorophyll production, thereby revealing pre-existing carotenoids"; "the enzymatic degradation of existing chlorophyll molecules"; "unmasks underlying carotenoids, xanthophylls and carotenes"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Chlorophyll ("chlorophyll breakdown unmasks underlying carotenoids and anthocyanins")

F8 The name chlorophyll comes from Greek chloros, green, and phyllon, leaf. https://grokipedia.com/page/Chlorophyll ("from Greek chloros for green and phyllon for leaf"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll

F9 The word photosynthesis joins Greek phos, light, and synthesis, putting together. The American botanist Charles Reid Barnes proposed it in 1893. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis ("derived from the Greek 'phos' ('gleam') and 'synthesis' ('arranging together')", Barnes 1893); https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/photosynthesis (photo- from Ancient Greek phos, light; synthesis, putting together); the curriculum line G5.L3 itself ("'photo' = light, 'synthesis' = putting together")

F10 The word photograph comes from the same Greek phos, light, with graphe, drawing: a photograph is a "drawing with light". John Herschel introduced the term in 1839. https://grokipedia.com/page/Photography

F11 A plant's matter comes mostly from air (carbon dioxide) and water, not from the soil. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ls1-1-molecules-organisms-structures-and-processes (5-LS1-1 clarification: "plant matter comes mostly from air and water, not from the soil"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Plant_nutrition (carbon, hydrogen and oxygen come "primarily from atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O), rather than soil minerals"); https://askabiologist.asu.edu/recipe-plant-growth ("a large majority of that material comes from thin air"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis (much of a plant's gained mass comes from carbon dioxide)

F12 From the soil a plant takes water and small amounts of mineral nutrients (such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium) through its roots. These are not food: the plant makes its own food. Fertilizer sold as "plant food" supplies minerals. https://grokipedia.com/page/Plant_nutrition ("mineral nutrients absorbed mainly through roots from the soil solution"); https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290 (Ohio State: plants "take up water and minerals from soil, but not food"; fertilizer supplies minerals and nutrients)

F13 Background only, not asked at T1: Jan Baptist van Helmont grew a 5-pound willow in 200 pounds of dried soil from 1600 to 1605, watering it only. The tree reached 169 pounds 3 ounces; the soil lost only about 2 ounces. He credited water and overlooked the carbon taken from the air. https://grokipedia.com/page/Jan_Baptist_van_Helmont; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis

F14 The energy in animals' food was once energy from the Sun, caught by plants as they made their food. Photosynthesis turns solar energy into chemical energy and is the basis of food chains. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ps3-1-energy (5-PS3-1: "energy in animals' food ... was once energy from the sun"; PS3.D: "captured by plants in the chemical process that forms plant matter (from air and water)"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ("converting solar energy into chemical energy"; "forming the basis of global food chains")

F15 Photosynthesis is the main source of the free oxygen in Earth's air, the oxygen animals breathe. https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Earth (oxygenic photosynthesis "represents the primary source of free oxygen"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis ("producing and maintaining the oxygen content" of the atmosphere; "supplies most of the biological energy necessary for complex life on Earth")

F16 Dry air is about 78% nitrogen and about a fifth oxygen; carbon dioxide is only about 0.04%. https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Earth (nitrogen "78.08% by volume"; CO2 0.043% in 2025)

F17 Joseph Priestley (1771) burned a candle in a closed jar of air until the flame went out, then put in a sprig of mint. After several days in sunlight the air was restored: a candle could burn in it again, and a mouse could live in it. https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ("He enclosed a burning candle in a glass vessel until the flame extinguished and then introduced a sprig of mint. After several days of exposure to sunlight, the air regained its purity"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis ("a plant could restore the air the candle and the mouse had 'injured'")

F18 Jan Ingenhousz (1779) found that only the green parts of plants, such as leaves, gave off the restoring gas, and only in light: the process stopped in darkness or shade. https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ("only the green portions of plants, such as leaves, released the restorative gas, and the process ceased in darkness or shade")

F19 A fire needs fuel, heat and oxygen at the same time; cut off the oxygen and it goes out. Carbon dioxide puts fires out by pushing the oxygen away. https://grokipedia.com/page/Fire_triangle ("All three elements must be present simultaneously"; "Smothering removes the oxidizing agent by excluding oxygen"; "Carbon dioxide (CO2) extinguishers displace oxygen")

F20 Plants also take in oxygen: they use it in respiration, to break down their sugar for energy. The oxygen they give off is a by-product of photosynthesis. https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290 ("Photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide, respiration uses oxygen"; respiration "breaks down those sugars to release energy"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis (plant respiration "occurred at night")

F21 Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colourless, odourless, poisonous gas made when fuels burn incompletely. It is a different gas from carbon dioxide (CO2), which has two oxygen atoms to its one. https://grokipedia.com/page/Carbon_monoxide ("colorless, odorless, tasteless"; toxic through hemoglobin; "the incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels")

F22 Nitrogen is part of every protein. A plant gets its nitrogen as a mineral nutrient from the soil (or, in peas and beans, through bacteria in its roots), not from water or carbon dioxide, which hold no nitrogen (H2O, CO2). https://grokipedia.com/page/Plant_nutrition ("Nitrogen is a critical component of amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, and chlorophyll"; bacteria in legumes "convert atmospheric dinitrogen (N2) into ammonia"); formulas from https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis

F23 A potato is an underground stem (a tuber) that stores food as starch. The starch is built from the products of photosynthesis in the leaves above ground, carried down to the tuber. https://grokipedia.com/page/Tuber (starch "synthesized from photosynthetic products translocated via the phloem"; energy from "aboveground photosynthesis"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Potato ("edible underground stems that serve as a starchy staple food"; "transport from foliage to storage tissues")

F24 Plants store spare sugar as starch. https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ("starch for energy reserves"; starch grains in chloroplasts and in tubers and seeds)

F25 Pollination is the carrying of pollen from the anther of a flower to the stigma of the same or another flower. https://grokipedia.com/page/Pollination

F26 Germination is a seed (or spore) waking and growing, as a seed sprouts into a seedling. https://grokipedia.com/page/Germination

F27 Transpiration is water evaporating from a plant's leaves and other parts above ground, mainly through tiny pores. https://grokipedia.com/page/Transpiration

F28 Nectar is a sweet liquid, mostly water and sugar, made by glands in flowers to draw pollinators. https://grokipedia.com/page/Nectar

F29 An acorn is the fruit of an oak, a single seed in a hard shell, which sprouts into an oak seedling. https://grokipedia.com/page/Acorn

F30 Cattle are grazers that eat mostly grasses, grazing for about 6 to 9 hours a day. https://grokipedia.com/page/Cattle

F31 As a plant makes its food in light, it gives off oxygen into the air. https://grokipedia.com/page/Photosynthesis ("O2 released into the atmosphere"; the equation's 6O2); https://grokipedia.com/page/Leaf (pores "allow carbon dioxide to enter for photosynthesis while permitting oxygen to exit"); curriculum line G5.L3 ("give off oxygen")

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 Plants get their food from the soil, through their roots; soil is food for plants. Ohio State lists "Soil provides a support structure and food for plants" and "Plants take in all substances they need to grow through their roots": https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290. Used as the lures "From soil and minerals" and "Its roots".

M2 Fertilizer is "plant food". Ohio State: "Plants need 'plant food' to eat" (https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290). Used as the lure "Minerals" for what a leaf makes.

M3 Sunlight is the plant's food, or gives the plant its matter. Ask A Biologist: plants "don't get their carbon from the soil, or from the sun, or from water" (https://askabiologist.asu.edu/recipe-plant-growth); Ohio State: "Plants obtain their energy directly from the sun." Used as the lure "From sunlight and warmth".

M4 Sunlight helps plants grow by keeping them warm. Ohio State: "Sunlight helps plants grow by keeping them warm" (https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290). Behind the word "warmth" in the same lure.

M5 Plants breathe in carbon dioxide and breathe out oxygen, the reverse of animals, and never use oxygen. Ohio State lists it (https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290); corrected by F20. Behind the lure "Oxygen" for the gas taken in to make food.

M6 Plants get their energy from the soil through their roots. Ohio State: "Plants get their energy from the soil through roots" (https://beyondpenguins.ehe.osu.edu/?p=2290). Used as the lure "The soil" for where the cow's energy first came from.

M7 A plant's food is made where it is found, so a potato's food is made underground. Author's judgment, a form of M1 (no survey found for the potato itself).

M8 "Photo" means picture, from the everyday English word photo. Author's judgment (no survey found); the honest correction is F10, a photograph is named for light.

## Notes and cautions

- Kept to photosynthesis itself (what goes in, where, what comes out, where the mass comes from, the Sun's energy in food). The jobs of roots and stems, the stomata, and the words producer and consumer belong to the neighbouring units and are not asked.
- No scientist is named in any stem (T1 rule): the candle-and-mint jar (F17) is told as a scene, not as Priestley's story.
- The potato question keys the leaves as the part that makes the food "in the first place": the starch is assembled in the tuber, but from sugar the leaves made (F23).
- The sugar question's lures (protein, minerals, salt) cannot be made from water and carbon dioxide alone: protein needs nitrogen (F22), minerals and salts come from the soil (F12).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-25, checked on the web now)

F32 A water plant such as the pondweed Elodea gives off oxygen as it makes its food in light, and the oxygen shows as bubbles on and rising from its leaves; this is a standard classroom way to show photosynthesis. https://grokipedia.com/page/Elodea ("vigorous oxygen production under light"; oxygen produced "as a byproduct, often visible as bubbles on leaf surfaces"; "extensively employed in educational laboratories to demonstrate photosynthesis", through "the production of oxygen bubbles from submerged leaves exposed to light"). The process stops in darkness (F18). Caution: the gas in such bubbles is not pure oxygen (some nitrogen from the water and the plant's air spaces joins it; a 2024 Water Resources Research study of submerged plants found bubbles about 21 to 45 percent oxygen, https://doaj.org/article/29c635ba28974fdd83eb41306b1f6b4e, seen in a search summary only), so the question asks which gas the pondweed gives off as it makes its food, never which gas fills the bubbles.

F33 Hydrogen is only a trace in the air: about 0.00005 percent of dry air by volume, about half a part per million. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_composition (dry-air table: hydrogen, H2, 0.00005 percent by volume); NOAA's global air-sampling network measured a global mean of 552.8 parts per billion in 2021 (https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/17/4803/2024/). The hydrogen in a plant's sugar comes from water (F2, F22), not from hydrogen gas in the air.

F34 Cellulose is the main building material of a plant's cell walls, a white substance. https://grokipedia.com/page/Cellulose ("the primary structural component of plant cell walls"; isolated by Payen as "a white, insoluble substance")

F35 Glucose is the simple sugar a plant makes in photosynthesis; as a solid it is colourless crystals or a white powder. https://grokipedia.com/page/Glucose ("In plants, glucose is synthesized de novo primarily through photosynthesis", which "converts carbon dioxide and water into carbohydrates using light energy"; "In its solid form, glucose appears as colorless crystals or a white, odorless powder with a sweet taste")

M9 Author's judgment, from the judge's note: the food a leaf makes (glucose) confused with the green substance that catches the light (chlorophyll); and an unknown leaf word (cellulose) taken for the light-catcher. Used as the lures "Glucose" and "Cellulose"; neither is green (F34, F35).

M10 Author's judgment: bubbles in water taken for water vapour, as in boiling water (the solids-liquids-and-gases unit teaches that boiling bubbles are water as a gas), or for carbon dioxide, as in a fizzy drink, and plants pictured breathing out carbon dioxide (M5). Used as the lures for the pondweed's gas.
