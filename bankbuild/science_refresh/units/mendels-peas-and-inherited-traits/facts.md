## Inherited and acquired traits

F1. An acquired characteristic is a change in a living thing's body or abilities that comes after birth, such as the muscles a bodybuilder gains by training, a scar from an injury, or a skill learned by practice. Acquired characteristics are not passed on to offspring through reproduction. Inherited traits are the ones passed from parents to offspring in their genes.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acquired_characteristic ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lamarckism ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendelian_inheritance

F2. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1744-1829), a French naturalist, set out in Philosophie zoologique (1809) the idea that traits a living thing develops in its lifetime, by use or disuse, are inherited by its offspring (the inheritance of acquired characteristics). Strong forearms built by a blacksmith's years of hammering are a stock example of a trait gained by use (Grokipedia gives it in Lamarck's account and traces it back to Aristotle). Mendel's genetics and later work found no mechanism for passing such changes on, and classical Lamarckism was abandoned. Caveat: modern epigenetics has reopened a narrow debate, but Grokipedia notes the results replicate inconsistently and "do not substantiate Lamarck's core claims"; muscle built by work is not passed on.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lamarckism ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acquired_characteristic

## Mendel, his garden and his method

F3. Gregor Mendel (1822-1884) was an Augustinian friar (Grokipedia also calls him a monk) at the Abbey of St Thomas in Brno (Brünn, then in the Austrian Empire, now in the Czech Republic). Between 1856 and 1863 he bred pea plants in the abbey's garden, over 28,000 of them (OpenStax: "nearly 30,000"). He became the abbey's abbot in 1868 and died on 6 January 1884.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregor_Mendel ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability

F4. Pea plants suited him because their flowers pollinate themselves, and the petals stay sealed until after pollination, so a variety left alone breeds true (a pure line); peas also show sharply contrasting either/or traits, grow from seed to seed in one season, and can be grown in large numbers. He tested 34 varieties for two years (1856-1857) and kept the 22 that bred true.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability

F5. Mendel followed seven either/or traits, among them stem length (tall or dwarf), seed shape (round or wrinkled) and seed colour (yellow or green); the others were flower (or seed-coat) colour, flower position, pod shape and pod colour.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability

F6. To cross two chosen plants, Mendel removed the anthers from the receiving (mother) flower with fine forceps before its pollen was released, so it could not pollinate itself; then he put pollen from the chosen father plant on its stigma, and covered the flower with a bag so that no stray pollen could reach it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability

F7. Flower parts: the anther, at the top of the stamen's stalk (the filament), makes the pollen. The stigma, at the top of the pistil, is the surface where pollen lands; the ovary at the pistil's base holds the ovules, which become seeds. Petals and sepals are the flower's outer parts.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Stamen ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gynoecium

## What the peas showed

F8. When Mendel crossed a pure tall line with a pure dwarf (short) line, the first generation (F1) was 100 percent tall. In every one of his seven traits, the F1 hybrids all showed one parent's form and none of the other's.
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel

F9. When he let the F1 hybrids pollinate themselves, the hidden form came back in the second generation (F2): 787 tall to 277 dwarf plants (2.84 to 1), 5,474 round to 1,850 wrinkled seeds (2.96 to 1), 6,022 yellow to 2,001 green seeds (3.01 to 1). Every trait came out near three to one, so about one offspring in four showed the hidden form.
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel

F10. Mendel named the form that showed in the hybrids "dominant" and the form that hid in them and came back later "recessive"; he coined both terms. OpenStax: "Recessive traits become latent, or disappear, in the offspring of a hybridization"; dominant traits "are inherited unchanged in a hybridization."
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregor_Mendel ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability

F11. Blending inheritance, the prevailing belief of Mendel's day, held that the parents' traits fuse in the offspring into an in-between form that cannot be recovered in later generations (like mixed paints). Mendel's results contradicted it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendelian_inheritance ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability

F12. Mendel explained his results with discrete, unchanging hereditary factors (his word was "elements") rather than blending: each trait is set by a pair of factors, one from each parent, which stay separate and part again when the plant makes its pollen and egg cells. The Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen named these factors "genes" in 1909.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendelian_inheritance ; https://www.genome.gov/25520244/online-education-kit-1909-the-word-gene-coined

## The modern words, and predicting a cross

F13. Alleles are different versions of a gene. A plant or animal carries two copies (alleles) of each gene, one from each parent. Each pollen grain, sperm or egg cell gets only one of the two, chosen at random, so either copy is equally likely (Mendel's law of segregation).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendelian_inheritance ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-2-characteristics-and-traits

F14. A hybrid with one dominant and one recessive allele shows the dominant trait, just like a plant with two dominant alleles. A recessive trait shows only when an offspring has two recessive alleles, one from each parent. So two parents who both show the dominant trait can have an offspring with the recessive trait when each carries a hidden recessive allele (recessive traits "often skip generations"; the parents are "carriers").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendelian_inheritance ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-2-characteristics-and-traits

F15. The genotype is the set of alleles a living thing carries (for a pea's height: TT, Tt or tt); the phenotype is the trait that can be seen or measured (tall or short). A TT plant and a Tt plant are both tall: the same phenotype with different genotypes. Wilhelm Johannsen coined both words in 1909.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Genotype%E2%80%93phenotype_distinction ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-2-characteristics-and-traits

F16. A Punnett square is a grid that pairs each parent's possible alleles to predict the chances of each genotype and phenotype among the offspring. The British geneticist Reginald Punnett devised it; it first appeared in his 1905 textbook Mendelism. Crossing two hybrids (Tt x Tt) gives 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, a 3 to 1 ratio of the dominant trait to the recessive.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Punnett_square ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-2-characteristics-and-traits

F17. Crossing a hybrid (Tt) with a plant that shows the recessive trait (tt) gives about half hybrids and half recessive offspring (1 to 1): this is the test cross, used to tell whether a dominant-looking plant hides a recessive allele. If the dominant-looking parent has two dominant alleles, all the offspring show the dominant trait.
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-2-characteristics-and-traits ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mendelian_inheritance

F18. A Punnett square gives chances, not guarantees: actual counts vary by chance, and small samples stray further from the expected ratio, which is why Mendel's large counts mattered (787 to 277 is 2.84 to 1, near but not exactly 3 to 1).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Punnett_square ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel

## Overlooked, then found

F19. Mendel read his paper, "Experiments on Plant Hybridization" (Versuche über Pflanzen-Hybriden), to the Natural History Society of Brünn (Brno) on 8 February and 8 March 1865, and it was printed in the society's proceedings in 1866. He had 40 offprints made and sent some to colleagues.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experiments_on_Plant_Hybridization ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability

F20. The paper was largely ignored: it appeared in the journal of a regional society with little circulation beyond Moravia and was cited only about three times in the next 35 years. Carl Nägeli, the leading botanist Mendel wrote to, did not appreciate it, and Charles Darwin was apparently unaware of it; Darwin's own idea of heredity was pangenesis (1868), which Mendel's laws later challenged.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregor_Mendel ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pangenesis

F21. Mendel's work was rediscovered in 1900, sixteen years after his death, when Hugo de Vries (Dutch), Carl Correns (German) and Erich von Tschermak (Austrian) published on it within a few months. De Vries and Correns had duplicated his results in their own crosses and both credited him (Tschermak's claim as a rediscoverer is now doubted). Mendel is now called the father of modern genetics.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregor_Mendel ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. Blending: that a cross of tall and short gives in-between offspring, and that a trait once blended is gone for good (F11). It was the expert view before Mendel.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/12-1-mendels-experiments-and-the-laws-of-probability

M2. That traits gained in life (strength, scars, skills) pass to children: Lamarck's respectable wrong theory (F2).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lamarckism

M3. That "dominant" means stronger, better or more common, and so that a parent passes on its dominant copy. "Dominant" says only which allele's effect shows in a hybrid.
    https://knowledge.carolina.com/?p=7147 ; https://teach.genetics.utah.edu/content/change/practice-with-punnett-squares

M4. That a Punnett square says exactly how many offspring of each kind there will be, and that one in four is the answer for every cross (it is only for two hybrids; a hybrid crossed with a recessive gives one half) (F16-F18).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Punnett_square ; https://teach.genetics.utah.edu/content/change/practice-with-punnett-squares

M5. That half of a parent's acquired traits pass on because a child gets half its genes from each parent (an author's note: the half rule is about alleles, not about traits gained in life; F1, F13).

M6. LEGEND, not used: that Mendel began with mice and his bishop made him stop. It comes from later tellings, not Mendel's papers (the old bank said so itself).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-25; sources read today, Grokipedia first)

F23. Mendel's own account of a cross: the bud is opened "before it is perfectly developed, the keel is removed" (the keel is the pea flower's two lower petals, joined), "each stamen carefully extracted by means of forceps", and then "the stigma can at once be dusted over with the foreign pollen". He worked on young buds because in peas "the anthers burst within the bud, so that the stigma becomes covered with pollen even before the flower opens". Grokipedia describes the same step as "selecting immature flower buds, prying open the keel petals, and excising the anthers with fine forceps". CAUTION, correcting F6: Mendel's paper says nothing of covering flowers with bags; no question or context uses the bag.
    https://www-archiv.fdm.uni-hamburg.de/b-online/e08_mend/mendel.htm (Bateson's translation of Mendel 1866) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gregor_Mendel

F24. Mendel's paper was cited only a handful of times before 1900 (Grokipedia: "estimates ranging from four to eleven references"; Olby and Gautrey, "Eleven references to Mendel before 1900", Annals of Science 24, 1968), and his peers sidelined the work "for over three decades"; it was recognised only after its rediscovery in 1900, sixteen years after his death (F21), so it did not make him famous in his lifetime. CAUTION, correcting F20's "about three times": no question or context uses that figure.
