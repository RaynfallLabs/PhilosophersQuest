## Barry Marshall, Robin Warren and the stomach bacterium

F1. Before Marshall and Warren, the prevailing view was that stomach ulcers "resulted from stress and acid rather than infection" (Grokipedia); Wikipedia: "stress, spicy foods, and too much acid". Critics "did not believe that any bacteria could live in the acidic environment of the stomach" (Wikipedia). Karl (Dragutin) Schwarz's dictum of 1910 was "no acid, no ulcer".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Barry_Marshall ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Marshall ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Timeline_of_peptic_ulcer_disease_and_Helicobacter_pylori

F2. The doubters had real reasons. Gastric acid (pH typically 1.5 to 3.5) is "the stomach's primary innate defense mechanism against foodborne and waterborne microbes"; below pH 4 it kills about 99.9 percent of gut bacteria such as E. coli and Salmonella within 30 minutes. In 1954 Edwin D. Palmer examined gastric biopsies and "found no evidence of bacteria" (Grokipedia), attributing earlier sightings to contamination (Wikipedia); his study "effectively established the acid theory as 'dogma'", and a later rejection of contrary results was put down to "the widespread belief that bacteria cannot survive in the human stomach, due to Palmer" (Wikipedia). Acid-suppressing drugs do heal ulcers (proton pump inhibitors: resolution in about 80 percent within 4-8 weeks).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gastric_acid ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Timeline_of_peptic_ulcer_disease_and_Helicobacter_pylori ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_peptic_ulcer_disease_and_Helicobacter_pylori ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Peptic_ulcer_disease

F3. J. Robin Warren, a pathologist at Royal Perth Hospital, Western Australia, saw spiral (curved) bacteria in stomach biopsies from 1979, close to inflamed lining; Barry Marshall, a young doctor, met him in 1981. They first grew the bacterium in culture in 1982 (Grokipedia Timeline: "Easter Monday, April 12, 1982").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Barry_Marshall ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_peptic_ulcer_disease_and_Helicobacter_pylori ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Timeline_of_peptic_ulcer_disease_and_Helicobacter_pylori

F4. A peptic ulcer is an open sore in the lining of the stomach or of the duodenum, "the upper part of the small intestine". H. pylori "accounts for 70-90% of gastric ulcers and up to 90% of duodenal ulcers" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Peptic_ulcer_disease

F5. Marshall's 1982 report was "largely dismissed by the audience" in Perth. "After failed attempts to infect piglets in 1984", Marshall drank "a broth containing cultured H. pylori" (Wikipedia): about 50 ml of a four-day-old culture, after a first endoscopy showed his stomach lining was normal (Grokipedia). The 1985 paper describes him only as "A volunteer with histologically normal gastric mucosa".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Marshall ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Barry_Marshall ; Marshall BJ, Armstrong JA, McGechie DB, Glancy RJ, "Attempt to fulfil Koch's postulates for pyloric Campylobacter", Med J Aust 1985 (PMID 3982345), abstract via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:3982345&resultType=core&format=json

F6. Result of the drink (primary record): "A mild illness developed, which lasted 14 days. Histologically proven gastritis was present on the tenth day after the ingestion of bacteria" (Med J Aust 1985). Grokipedia: the day-10 endoscopy showed "acute gastritis and heavy colonization", not an ulcer; symptoms were nausea, vomiting, bad breath. Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining. Wikipedia: his illness fulfilled Koch's postulates "for H. pylori and gastritis, but not for peptic ulcers". The paper proposed that the disorder "may progress to a chronic infection which predisposes to peptic ulceration".
    PMID 3982345 (as F5) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Barry_Marshall ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Marshall

F7. The 1988 trial: Marshall BJ, Goodwin CS, Warren JR and others, "Prospective double-blind trial of duodenal ulcer relapse after eradication of Campylobacter pylori", Lancet 1988 (PMID 2904568): 100 patients with duodenal ulcer and the infection. "When C pylori was cleared 92% of ulcers healed" (61 percent when it persisted); over a "12 month follow-up period", "only 21% relapsed" where it was cleared, against "84% relapsed" where it persisted. The Royal Society citation: Marshall "showed that antibiotic and bismuth salt regimens that killed H. pylori resulted in the cure of duodenal ulcers".
    https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:2904568&resultType=core&format=json ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Marshall

F8. A US National Institutes of Health consensus conference in 1994 recommended antibiotic eradication for peptic ulcer patients with the infection (Grokipedia) and "demonstrates the general acceptance of H. pylori as cause of PUD in the US" (Wikipedia). Marshall and Warren shared the 2005 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discovery of the bacterium Helicobacter pylori and its role in gastritis and peptic ulcer disease".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Barry_Marshall ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_peptic_ulcer_disease_and_Helicobacter_pylori ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Marshall

F9. Both sides of the record: Marshall was quoted in 1998 as saying "everyone was against me, but I knew I was right"; Wikipedia adds that others argue medical researchers showed proper "scientific scepticism" until the hypothesis was supported by evidence.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Marshall

## Dan Shechtman and the quasicrystal

F10. On 8 April 1982, on sabbatical at the US National Bureau of Standards (now NIST), Dan Shechtman passed an electron beam through a rapidly solidified (fast-cooled) aluminium-manganese alloy (about 86 percent aluminium, 14 percent manganese) and saw a tenfold diffraction pattern. His notebook beside exposure 1725 reads "10 Fold ???" (transcriptions vary). He recalled: "I remember counting the diffraction peaks clockwise, one by one up to 10, and thinking that it couldn't be" (Physics Today).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dan_Shechtman ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Shechtman ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Quasicrystal ; https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/nobel-prize-in-chemistry-honors-the-discovery-of-quasicrystals ; https://www.nist.gov/node/1109896

F11. Why it looked impossible (sound geometry): a periodic crystal was required to have "strict three-dimensional translational periodicity" (Grokipedia), i.e. one small group of atoms repeated over and over in every direction. The crystallographic restriction theorem: "any rotational symmetry of a lattice must have an order of 1, 2, 3, 4, or 6" (Grokipedia), so fivefold and tenfold are excluded; Wikipedia: crystals "can possess only two-, three-, four-, and six-fold rotational symmetries", and "Tiling of a plane by regular pentagons is impossible".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dan_Shechtman ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Crystallographic_restriction_theorem ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicrystal

F12. The reaction: the head of his group told him to "go back and read the textbook", and he was asked to leave the group for "bringing disgrace" on it (Wikipedia). Linus Pauling said: "There is no such thing as quasicrystals, only quasi-scientists" (Grokipedia, Wikipedia; Physics Today: "There is no such thing as quasicrystals," he often said). Pauling opposed the non-periodic interpretation until his death in 1994 (Wikipedia). Pauling won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1954, the nature of the chemical bond) and the Nobel Peace Prize (1962); he died on 19 August 1994.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Shechtman ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dan_Shechtman ; https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/nobel-prize-in-chemistry-honors-the-discovery-of-quasicrystals ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Linus_Pauling

F13. Pauling's explanation was twinning: he attributed the patterns to "multiple twinning of periodic crystals" (Grokipedia), i.e. several ordinary crystal grains grown together at angles, which can mimic one tenfold grain. His letter: L. Pauling, "Apparent icosahedral symmetry is due to directed multiple twinning of cubic crystals", Nature 317, 512-514 (1985); later "So-called icosahedral and decagonal quasicrystals are twins of an 820-atom cubic crystal" (1987). Twinning was "the standard explanation" (Physics Today) and "the common explanation for tenfold patterns" (Wikipedia). Grokipedia (Dan Shechtman) says unnamed colleagues also suggested instrument artifacts.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Quasicrystal ; https://www.nature.com/articles/317512a0 (citation; via search) ; https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/nobel-prize-in-chemistry-honors-the-discovery-of-quasicrystals ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicrystal

F14. The test that answered twinning: Shechtman "ruled out twinning by tracing each diffraction peak to a single grain", and "a 20 nm electron beam also reproduced the full pattern"; he concluded, "It was not twinning" (Physics Today). Wikipedia (Quasicrystal): "Shechtman's experiments ruled that out."
    https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/nobel-prize-in-chemistry-honors-the-discovery-of-quasicrystals ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicrystal

F15. Publication and confirmation: "Metallic Phase with Long-Range Orientational Order and No Translational Symmetry", Physical Review Letters, 12 November 1984, with Ilan Blech, Denis Gratias and John Cahn (rejected first by the Journal of Applied Physics). By mid-1985 several laboratories, including Japanese teams, reported similar patterns (Grokipedia), "ruling out artifacts in multiple laboratories" (Grokipedia, Quasicrystal). Doubts "largely ended in 1987", when An-Pang Tsai and colleagues found a stable phase that could be grown into large, nearly perfect grains (Physics Today).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Dan_Shechtman ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Quasicrystal ; https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/nobel-prize-in-chemistry-honors-the-discovery-of-quasicrystals

F16. In 1992 the International Union of Crystallography changed its definition of a crystal to "any solid with a discrete diffraction diagram" (Grokipedia), focusing on "the ability to produce a clear-cut diffraction pattern", periodic or not (Wikipedia). Shechtman alone won the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of quasicrystals; the committee said the discovery "revealed a new principle for packing of atoms and molecules" (Wikipedia). A natural quasicrystal turned up in 2009 in fragments of the Khatyrka meteorite.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Quasicrystal ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicrystal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dan_Shechtman

## Lynn Margulis and the captured bacteria

F17. Lynn Margulis (signing as Lynn Sagan) published "On the Origin of Mitosing Cells" in the Journal of Theoretical Biology in 1967; it had been "rejected by more than a dozen journals" (Gray 2017; Grokipedia says about 15). She proposed that mitochondria came from engulfed oxygen-using (aerobic) bacteria and chloroplasts (plastids) from photosynthetic cyanobacteria, and that the eukaryotic flagellum came from "an endosymbiotic, spirochete-like organism" (Gray 2017). She received the US National Medal of Science in 1999.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lynn_Margulis ; Gray MW, "Lynn Margulis and the endosymbiont hypothesis: 50 years later", Mol Biol Cell 2017, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5426843/

F18. The doubters' reasons: earlier symbiosis ideas (Mereschkowski 1905 for chloroplasts, Wallin in the 1920s for mitochondria) "did not gain traction" (Wikipedia); Wallin's cultures "failed due to contamination issues" (Grokipedia). Of Margulis's colleagues, "Most scientists simply wanted stronger evidence before accepting the new idea" (Berkeley), and critics "could think up plausible scenarios" in which the cell evolved gradually, reading mitochondria's circular DNA as shared ancestry. The rival "autogenous" ("origin from within") view held that the organelles formed inside the cell; Raff and Mahler (1972, "The non symbiotic origin of mitochondria") and Uzzell and Spolsky (1974) rejected endosymbiosis (Gray 2017). Berkeley: the alternative was "that mitochondria originated step by step inside the cell".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiogenesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Symbiogenesis ; https://undsci.berkeley.edu/cells-within-cells-an-extraordinary-claim-with-extraordinary-evidence/the-community-reacts/ ; https://undsci.berkeley.edu/cells-within-cells-an-extraordinary-claim-with-extraordinary-evidence/examining-the-alternative/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5426843/

F19. Mitochondria and chloroplasts carry their own DNA. Margit and Sylvan Nass (1963) found "DNase-sensitive fibers" inside mitochondria by electron microscopy (Grokipedia). "Some mitochondria and some plastids contain single circular DNA molecules that are similar to the DNA of bacteria" (Wikipedia); nuclear DNA is "bundled up in linear strands" (Berkeley).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Symbiogenesis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiogenesis ; https://undsci.berkeley.edu/cells-within-cells-an-extraordinary-claim-with-extraordinary-evidence/the-community-reacts/

F20. Ribosomes: the organelles "possess 70S ribosomes, smaller and structurally similar to those in bacteria", "sensitive to antibiotics that target prokaryotic ribosomes, such as chloramphenicol" (Grokipedia, Symbiogenesis); Wikipedia: "more similar to those of bacteria (70S) than those of eukaryotes". Chloramphenicol "inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 50S subunit of the ribosome", with "specificity for prokaryotic ribosomes over eukaryotic cytoplasmic ones"; this targeting "extends to mitochondrial ribosomes in eukaryotes", which underlies its dose-dependent bone-marrow suppression (Grokipedia, Chloramphenicol). Ribosomes are the cell's protein builders.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Symbiogenesis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiogenesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Chloramphenicol

F21. "New mitochondria and plastids are formed only through binary fission" (Wikipedia), "analogous to bacterial cell division" (Grokipedia); both have double membranes.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiogenesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Symbiogenesis

F22. The decisive test (the "smoking gun", Berkeley): Michael Gray and W. Ford Doolittle asked "whether the DNA inside plastids was more closely related to bacterial DNA" or to nuclear DNA. By 1982 they reported plastid DNA "much more similar to the DNA of free-living, photosynthesizing bacteria" than to the host's; mitochondrial sequences a year later were "remarkably similar to the DNA of free-living oxygen-using bacteria". Today mitochondrial genes "consistently place their origin within the Alphaproteobacteria" and plastid genes "cluster robustly with those from cyanobacteria" (Grokipedia). Gray and Doolittle's 1982 review was titled "Has the endosymbiont hypothesis been proven?" (Gray 2017).
    https://undsci.berkeley.edu/cells-within-cells-an-extraordinary-claim-with-extraordinary-evidence/the-smoking-gun-support-from-a-new-technology/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Symbiogenesis ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5426843/

F23. Her third claim failed: "no genome has been associated with the eukaryotic flagellar apparatus despite efforts to find one" (Gray 2017, citing Johnson and Rosenbaum 1991). Grokipedia: the spirochete origin of flagella was refuted; "Molecular and phylogenetic data support endogenous development through gene duplication and modification." Berkeley: "most biologists reject the hypothesis that tubule organelles descended from endosymbionts."
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5426843/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Lynn_Margulis ; https://undsci.berkeley.edu/cells-within-cells-an-extraordinary-claim-with-extraordinary-evidence/the-debate-about-tubule-organelles-rages-on/

## J Harlen Bretz, Joseph Pardee and the Ice Age floods

F24. J Harlen Bretz (born 2 September 1882) began fieldwork on the Columbia Plateau in the summer of 1922 and published "The Channeled Scabland of the Columbia Plateau" in the Journal of Geology in 1923, arguing that a single catastrophic flood carved the region's dry channels (coulees), dry waterfalls larger than Niagara, giant potholes and immense gravel bars. He coined "Spokane Flood" in 1925.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/J_Harlen_Bretz ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J_Harlen_Bretz

F25. The doubters' reasons. Uniformitarianism: Charles Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830-1833) explained Earth's features by "gradual, ongoing processes observable in the present"; William Whewell coined the word in 1832; the older catastrophist geology included William Buckland's reading of cave fossils as "evidence of a Noachian deluge" (Grokipedia). Bretz's view ran "against the prevailing view of uniformitarianism" (Wikipedia, Missoula floods); the theory's implication of a "Biblical flood" was a reason the scientific community rejected it (Wikipedia, Bretz); it "smacked of biblical catastrophism" (University of Chicago Magazine); Bailey Willis dismissed it as a return to outdated "catastrophism" (Grokipedia). And Bretz "could not answer: where had so much water come from?" (UChicago Magazine); critics dismissed "floods from nowhere" (Grokipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Uniformitarianism ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missoula_floods ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J_Harlen_Bretz ; https://magazine.uchicago.edu/0912/features/legacy.shtml ; https://grokipedia.com/page/J_Harlen_Bretz

F26. On 12 January 1927 the Geological Society of Washington, D.C., invited Bretz to present his work, and several other geologists presented competing theories; Bretz called his critics six "challenging elders" (Wikipedia). Acceptance came gradually; a 1965 field trip of skeptical geologists sent him a telegram, "Greetings and Salutations. We are all now catastrophists!" (UChicago Magazine). He won the Geological Society of America's Penrose Medal, its highest honour, in 1979 and told his son: "All my enemies are dead, so I have no one to gloat over." He died 3 February 1981.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J_Harlen_Bretz ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missoula_floods ; https://magazine.uchicago.edu/0912/features/legacy.shtml ; https://grokipedia.com/page/J_Harlen_Bretz

F27. Joseph Thomas Pardee of the US Geological Survey published in 1910 a paper that "identified Glacial Lake Missoula" (Wikipedia); he "traced out the strandlines across the landscape" (LibreTexts); strandlines are benches cut by the lake's waves, still seen as horizontal lines on Mount Sentinel and Mount Jumbo above Missoula, recording "various lake levels" (NPS). The Cordilleran ice sheet "formed a glacial dam across the Clark Fork valley" (Wikipedia); the Purcell Trench lobe (Grokipedia); the lake held about 500 cubic miles of water (UChicago; Grokipedia about 530 cubic miles; Grokipedia Missoula floods about 2,100-2,500 cubic kilometres per flood).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missoula_floods ; https://geo.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Geology/Historical_Geology_(Bentley_et_al.)/37%3A_(Case_Study)_The_Channeled_Scablands_of_eastern_Washington/37.05%3A_Glacial_Lake_Missoula ; https://www.nps.gov/places/glacial-lake-missoula-strandlines.htm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Glacial_Lake_Missoula ; https://magazine.uchicago.edu/0912/features/legacy.shtml

F28. In 1940 Pardee presented (Geological Society of America; published 1942 in its Bulletin) giant current ripples on the former lake floor at Camas Prairie, Montana: "actually hills nearly 50 feet high and up to 500 feet apart" (UChicago Magazine); NPS: "ridges 15 to 50 feet high"; PBS NOVA: Pardee "first identified these wavy landforms as ripples", "not unlike those seen in the bed of a stream", unrecognised before "because of their outlandish size"; "perhaps the strongest evidence for monstrous ancient floods". He argued that the ice dam failed and released the lake's water in a sudden torrent (UChicago Magazine; Grokipedia Glacial Lake Missoula: linked to "repeated ice-dam failures"). Pardee estimated flood speeds above 45 miles per hour to move the largest boulders (Wikipedia). The 1940 presentation "tipped" the debate (phys.org).
    https://magazine.uchicago.edu/0912/features/legacy.shtml ; https://www.nps.gov/places/glacial-lake-missoula-national-natural-landmark-camas-prairie-ripples.htm ; https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/megaflood/scab-04.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Glacial_Lake_Missoula ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missoula_floods ; https://phys.org/news/2012-02-legacy-megaflood.html

F29. Giant ripples seen from the air: such gravel ridges "are plainly visible on aerial photographs, but went unnoticed for many years" (USGS, The Channeled Scablands of Eastern Washington), their pattern being almost indistinguishable from the ground because of their immense size (USGS, as quoted in search results).
    https://www.npshistory.com/publications/geology/inf/72-2/sec4.htm

F30. Not one flood but many: "geologists now believe that there were 40 or more separate floods", "between 15,000 and 13,000 years ago" (Wikipedia); Grokipedia gives estimates from dozens to over 100. Bretz's single flood was right in kind but not in number. The floodwaters raced across eastern Washington and through the Columbia River Gorge to the Pacific (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missoula_floods ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Missoula_floods

## Cold fusion: a bold claim that failed the test

F31. On 23 March 1989 the University of Utah held a press conference at which electrochemists Martin Fleischmann (University of Southampton), "one of the world's leading electrochemists" (Wikipedia), and Stanley Pons (University of Utah) announced "excess heat output from palladium cathodes electrolyzed in heavy water", which they attributed to deuterium-deuterium fusion "at or near room temperature" (Grokipedia); heat "of a magnitude they asserted would defy explanation except in terms of nuclear processes", with "small amounts of nuclear reaction byproducts, including neutrons and tritium" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cold_fusion ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_fusion

F32. Why it ran against the consensus: nuclei are positively charged and repel; "The fundamental challenge in achieving fusion is the Coulomb barrier"; the Sun's core is "about 15 million Kelvin" and laboratory fusion needs "plasma temperatures above 100 million kelvin" (Grokipedia, Nuclear fusion). Under known physics 1 watt of fusion power would give about 10^12 neutrons per second, "levels that would have been fatal to the researchers" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Nuclear_fusion ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_fusion

F33. The test: "Many scientists tried to replicate the experiment with the few details available" (Wikipedia). "Other groups, including Caltech and Harwell Laboratory, similarly found no anomalies by mid-1989"; MIT's centre "concluded no excess heat or fusion signatures" (Grokipedia). Most scientists considered the claims dead by late 1989 (Wikipedia). A US Department of Energy panel in 1989 found the results "did not present convincing evidence that useful sources of energy would result" (Wikipedia); its 2004 review reached conclusions "similar to those found in the 1989 review". A Google-funded team's replication, published in Nature in 2019, reported "no cold fusion" (Wikipedia). Research "persisted on the fringes" (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_fusion ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Cold_fusion

F34. What a crystal is, and how crystals form: a crystal is "a highly ordered, repeating three-dimensional pattern that extends throughout the entire structure"; crystals form "during cooling from a melt or precipitation from a solution" (and from vapour); halite (table salt, sodium chloride, NaCl: two elements) forms "by precipitation from concentrated brines", i.e. as salty water evaporates (Grokipedia, Crystal). In metals "positively charged metal ions are arranged in ordered crystal lattices", and metals are shiny from "high reflectivity across visible wavelengths", over 90 percent for polished silver or aluminium (Grokipedia, Metal).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Crystal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Metal

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. "Marshall gave himself an ulcer": popular retellings often say so; the primary record (F6) is gastritis, an inflamed and heavily colonised lining, with no ulcer.
M2. "Acid has nothing to do with ulcers": acid still damages the sore, and acid-suppressing drugs heal ulcers (F2, F4); the bacterium is the main cause, not the only factor.
M3. A crystal is often thought to need flat faces, clear glassy look, or a single pure substance; by the pre-1992 definition it needed only a repeating arrangement of atoms (F11, F16). Metals and alloys are crystalline yet opaque; table salt is a crystal of two elements.
M4. "The rebel is always right": Margulis's flagella claim (F23) and cold fusion (F31-F33) failed the same kind of test that bore out the others.
M5. Bretz's "one flood": there were dozens (F30).

## Conflicts found, and how each was handled

- Day of Marshall's endoscopy: Wikipedia says day eight; the 1985 paper itself says "the tenth day" and Grokipedia agrees. The primary record is used (ten days).
- Month of the drink: Wikipedia's timeline gives 12 June 1984; Grokipedia "late July 1984". No month is used; only 1984.
- Year of the first culture: Grokipedia's H. pylori page says "By 1983"; Wikipedia and Grokipedia's timeline say 1982 (Easter Monday, 12 April). Not used in a stem.
- Margulis's rejections: "about 15" (Grokipedia, her own recollection) against "more than a dozen" (Gray 2017). Stems say "more than a dozen".
- Whether the flagella claim was in the 1967 paper: Grokipedia is inconsistent; Gray 2017 says it was. Gray is followed.
- The 1927 meeting: Grokipedia (J Harlen Bretz) places it "in Seattle"; Wikipedia says the Geological Society of Washington, D.C. Not used in a stem.
- The ice sheet: LibreTexts says the Purcell Lobe of the Laurentide ice sheet; Grokipedia, Wikipedia and the NPS say the Cordilleran ice sheet. The stems say only "a dam of glacier ice".
- Ripple heights: 15-50 ft (NPS), up to 35 ft (PBS), 20-30 ft (USGS circular), "nearly 50 feet" (UChicago, citing Pardee). Stems say "up to about 15 metres" (50 feet), the figure Pardee's own paper is reported to give.
- Aerial photographs: a Colorado School of Mines guide says Pardee recognised the ripples on the first aerial photographs of Camas Prairie; a geology blog says he did it without them. Stems say only that the pattern shows best from the air (USGS, F29), never that Pardee used photographs.
- Bretz's age at the Penrose Medal: 96 (Wikipedia) or 97 (Grokipedia, UChicago). Not used.
- Shechtman's alloy: 86/14 (NIST, Grokipedia) against Al-25% Mn (an IUCr tribute). Not used.
- When Shechtman was asked to leave his group: "a few days later" (Wikipedia) against 1984 (Grokipedia). Not used.
- The 1989 cold-fusion announcement and neutrons: Grokipedia says in one place that it "reported no significant neutron emissions" and in another that the claims included neutrons; Wikipedia says small amounts of neutrons and tritium. Stems do not say what neutron claim they made.
- Lyell's "free the science from Moses" line could be traced only to secondary (mostly partisan) sources; not used.

Palmer's 1954 study was titled "Investigation of the gastric mucosa spirochetes of the human" (Gastroenterology 27:218-220); "Spirochetes are spiral bacteria discussed by Palmer and others"; he "examined 1,180 subjects", the study "showed no presence of bacteria in the gastric mucosa of the subjects", and it "was based on a method unsuitable for detecting spiral bacteria" (Radomski, Šešelja and Naumann 2021). Wikipedia: earlier sightings put down to contamination; no silver stain. So: he found none of the spiral bacteria.

F36. The order of the twinning story: on 8 April 1982 Shechtman "spent the whole afternoon of that day trying different TEM techniques to find the twins" ("I wanted to find the twins, record them and forget about it") (NIST); "every peak could be traced to one grain", and a beam on an area 20 nm across, "too small to harbor multiple twins", reproduced the whole pattern (Physics Today). Twinning was "the obvious explanation" among crystallographers (Physics Today). Pauling's twinning papers came after, in 1985 (Nature 317:512, "twinning of cubic crystals") and 1987 (F13), and he held to twinning until his death in 1994 (F12).
    https://www.nist.gov/node/1109896 ; https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/nobel-prize-in-chemistry-honors-the-discovery-of-quasicrystals ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicrystal
F37. Shape is not the test of a crystal: "a crystal is scientifically defined by its microscopic atomic arrangement, not its macroscopic shape"; "Metals crystallize rapidly and are almost always polycrystalline"; anhedral crystals lack obvious flat faces, "usually because the crystal is one grain in a polycrystalline solid" (Wikipedia, Crystal). Grokipedia (Crystal): flat faces are a characteristic, and "anhedral crystals display irregular shapes lacking well-defined faces".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Crystal
F38. Tight packing is not the test either: ordinary ice has "a hexagonal crystal structure", and "This structure results in ice Ih having a lower density than liquid water", "causing ice to float" (Grokipedia, Ice); "ionic crystals typically feature more open structures" (Grokipedia, Crystal).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Crystal
F39. How mitochondria multiply: "New mitochondria and plastids are formed only through binary fission, the form of cell division used by bacteria and archaea"; "If a cell's mitochondria or chloroplasts are removed, the cell does not have the means to create new ones" (Wikipedia, Symbiogenesis); Grokipedia: "Mitochondria and chloroplasts replicate through binary fission", "analogous to bacterial cell division". Bacteria: "Organisms in the domains of Archaea and Bacteria reproduce with binary fission"; "Binary fission means 'division into two'" (Wikipedia, Fission (biology)). Budding is a different way: "a new organism develops from an outgrowth, or bud", as in yeast, and it "contrasts with other asexual methods like binary fission by involving asymmetric division" (Grokipedia, Budding).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiogenesis ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Symbiogenesis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_(biology) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Budding
F40. A from-the-nucleus idea of the day: Peter R. Lothar Diers (J Cell Biol 28:527-543, 1966), on eggs of the liverwort Sphaerocarpus donnellii: "No indications have been observed that new mitochondria or plastids generate from the nuclear evaginations."

F41. Bacteria that live inside plants: in the root nodules of legumes, rhizobia "differentiate into nitrogen-fixing bacteroids enclosed in membrane-bound compartments", "called symbiosomes" (Grokipedia, Root nodule). Plastid DNA clusters with cyanobacteria (F22), not with these.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Root_nodule ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Symbiogenesis
F42. A flagellum's parts come from the cell body: "IFT functions as a protein carrier delivering hundreds of distinct proteins into growing cilia" (Lechtreck 2015, Trends Biochem Sci, PMID 26498262); ciliary proteins "are thought to come from the cell body through intraflagellar transport or diffusion" (Hao et al. 2021, Nat Commun 12:6971, PMID 34848703). Hao et al. report ribosome parts in mouse ependymal multicilia, of the cell's own kind ("18 S rRNA"; bacterial ribosomes carry 16S, F20); no bacterium-like ribosomes have been reported in flagella, and no genome (F23).

F43. Glaciers do shove gravel into ridges: moraines form by "pushing of material into ridges by advancing ice"; "advancing ice shoves preexisting sediment into push ridges"; washboard moraines are "regularly spaced, arcuate ridges transverse to glacier flow" (Grokipedia, Moraine). The Camas Prairie ridges are not these: "typical ripple marks in every respect except their great size", built by "the powerful currents" (USGS, F29; NOVA, F28).
