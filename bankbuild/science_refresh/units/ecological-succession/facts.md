## What succession is: primary and secondary

F1. Ecological succession is the process by which the make-up and structure of a community change over time (Grokipedia: "community composition and structure change over time"). Texas Gateway (TEKS 8.12B): students "describe how primary and secondary ecological succession affect populations and species diversity after ecosystems are disrupted by natural events or human activity."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_succession ; https://texasgateway.org/teks/s812b/alignments

F2. Primary succession starts on "newly exposed, barren substrates lacking preexisting soil": lava flows, glacial till, sand dunes; it is slow, decades to centuries (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: "colonization of an area with little to no biotic factors is referred to as primary succession", on new rock or sand, lava flows and glacial tills. A glacier "scours the landscape down to the bedrock" (LibreTexts).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_succession ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession ; https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Botany/Botany_(Ha_Morrow_and_Algiers)/05%3A_Ecology_and_Conservation/5.02%3A_Communities_and_Ecosystems/5.2.05%3A_Succession

F3. Secondary succession follows a disturbance that "removes vegetation but leaves the soil and some biological legacies": fire, logging, abandoned fields (Grokipedia). It is faster because of "pre-existing nutrient-rich soil and propagule sources" and a seed bank; it "progresses more rapidly due to the availability of nutrients, soil microbes, and seed banks", and "stumps and surviving roots enable vegetative resprouting" (Grokipedia, Secondary succession). Wikipedia: "soil development, seed banks, remaining organic matter" remain. The real difference between the two is the starting point (no soil, or soil left), not the cause or the speed (study-guide misconception note, M2).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_succession ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Secondary_succession ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession

## Pioneers and the making of soil

F4. Lichens are pioneer species, "initiating succession on bare rock or disturbed soils". A lichen "lacks true roots, stems, leaves, or flowers"; it takes in water "from rain, dew, fog, or humid air directly through their thallus surface", and "desiccation tolerance is a hallmark of lichens". Crustose lichens grow slowly, about 0.1 to 1 mm a year.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lichen

F5. Lichens weather rock: "By producing oxalic acid and other chelators, they break down silicate rocks", contributing "to soil formation by weathering rocks and accumulating organic matter" (Grokipedia, Lichen). Pioneers weather substrates by "secreting acids that weather rock substrates", and their growth and decay add organic matter that builds soil (Grokipedia, Ecological succession). LibreTexts: pioneers "help to further break down the mineral-rich rock into soil."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Lichen ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_succession ; https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Botany/Botany_(Ha_Morrow_and_Algiers)/05%3A_Ecology_and_Conservation/5.02%3A_Communities_and_Ecosystems/5.2.05%3A_Succession

F6. Mosses are pioneers too: they "lack true roots, stems, and leaves" (rhizoids anchor them), their green plant body is "photosynthetic and independent", and they colonize bare rock and soil.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Moss

F7. The stages of primary succession run from microorganisms and lichens to mosses, grasses, shrubs and trees (Wikipedia; LibreTexts). Each stage alters the habitat "to support the next" (Grokipedia); "As plants die and decay, their remains enrich the soil" (NPS, Indiana Dunes); "each community created the soil and microclimate conditions required for its successor community to thrive" (UChicago, of Cowles's dunes). Soil development "progressively improves substrate fertility and structure" (Grokipedia).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_succession ; https://www.nps.gov/indu/learn/nature/plant-succession.htm ; https://news.uchicago.edu/explainer/what-is-ecological-succession

F8. Early-succession species are "light-demanding" with "rapid growth rates, efficient seed dispersal, short lifespans"; late stages have "shade-tolerant species" that "regenerate in the understory" (Grokipedia). Wikipedia: early communities are "fast-growing, well-dispersed species", pioneer trees are "intolerant of shade", replaced by shade-tolerant species. Each stage casts more shade than the one before, not less.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_succession ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession

## Old fields: secondary succession on abandoned farmland

F9. North Carolina Piedmont old fields (Duke Forest): "1st year: Horseweed dominant"; "2nd year: Asters dominant"; "3rd to 18th year: Grass scrub community; broomsedge grass, pines coming in"; "19th to 30th year: Young pine forest"; "30th to 70th year: Mature pine forest; understory of young hardwoods"; "70th to 100th year: Pine to hardwood transition"; "100th year plus: Climax oak-hickory forest". Old fields "grow back in a century or two to become mixed hardwood forest." Catherine Keever's 1950 paper "Causes of Succession on Old Fields of the Piedmont, North Carolina" (Ecological Monographs) described the first years (horseweed, aster, broomsedge).
    https://dukeforest.duke.edu/forest-environment/forest-succession/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_Keever ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_succession

F10. Why pines first and hardwoods after (Duke Forest): "pines usually grow faster than the hardwoods under the high light" of an abandoned field, so they form the first forest. Once they dominate they cut the light reaching the ground and use much more soil water, making it "impossible for most of the pines to reproduce and grow in their own shade"; species that tolerate those conditions grow under them. "The normal life span of the southern pines in this area is 80 to 120 years."
    https://dukeforest.duke.edu/forest-environment/forest-succession/

## How we know: Cowles's dunes

F11. Henry Chandler Cowles, a professor at the University of Chicago, studied succession in the Indiana Dunes on Lake Michigan: PhD 1898, "Ecological Relations of the Vegetation on the Sand Dunes of Lake Michigan" (1899). He noticed that dunes farther from the lake had different plants from those near it: beach grass near the shore, then cottonwoods and pines, then oak forest. He reasoned that "the linear succession of these communities in space also represented a linear progression in time", the dunes farthest from the lake being the oldest, stable longest (UChicago). Wikipedia: he saw dunes of different ages as "different stages of a general trend" and "formulated the idea of primary succession". NPS: the dunes "hosted the first research on plant succession" in the 1890s.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Chandler_Cowles ; https://news.uchicago.edu/explainer/what-is-ecological-succession ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession ; https://www.nps.gov/indu/learn/education/history-of-science-plant-succession.htm

F12. The Indiana Dunes shoreline stood about a mile inland "only a few thousand years ago" (so the inland dunes formed first). Marram grass is "the first widespread plant away from the beach and most important of the dune-builders", thriving "on the rapid sand burial"; the oak woodland shows a dune "old enough that the soil has been enriched by previous plant communities." All the dunes are built of sand blown in from the lake beach.
    https://www.nps.gov/indu/learn/nature/plant-succession.htm

## How we know: Glacier Bay and Cooper's plots

F13. William S. Cooper, a University of Minnesota botanist, came to Glacier Bay, Alaska, in 1916 to study how plants colonize ground newly bared by the retreating glaciers, and "established a series of plots"; he "followed those same plots for 25 years". His student Donald Lawrence continued them from the 1940s to the late 1980s; the lost plots were found again in June 2016 (Buma and others, Ecology, 2017), about a century on, the longest-running study of their kind. IFLScience: six 1-square-metre quadrats (the 2017 paper speaks of eight plots; the count is not used). Cooper's advocacy helped bring about Glacier Bay National Monument (1925).
    https://www.nps.gov/articles/cooper-succession-plots.htm ; https://www.iflscience.com/plants-and-animals/worlds-longest-plant-succession-study-shows-how-ecosystems-evolve-and-change-over-time/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Glacier_Bay_National_Park_and_Preserve

F14. What the century of plots showed (NPS): "Many of the plots were invaded early (1920's) by willows (Salix species), and those willows are still present"; "later-successional species such as alder and spruce are rarer"; "a general increase in the number of species with time (increasing biodiversity)"; the older plots nearer the bay's mouth "have shown the greatest increase". The authors suggest that many plants have difficulty dispersing so far up the bay, and that early pioneers "have been able to almost completely monopolize resources and resist invasion by other species." Brian Buma: "There are a lot of random things that happen early in the plot history" that still shape the vegetation (IFLScience). (This is the inhibition pathway: early species hold the ground against later ones; Grokipedia lists inhibition beside facilitation and tolerance.)
    https://www.nps.gov/articles/cooper-succession-plots.htm ; https://www.iflscience.com/plants-and-animals/worlds-longest-plant-succession-study-shows-how-ecosystems-evolve-and-change-over-time/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_succession

F15. Glacier Bay's sequence (NPS): from "black crust" (algae) to moss, horsetail and fireweed, Dryas, willows, alder and spruce, ending in hemlock forest; a trip up the bay "rolls back plant succession, from the mature forest at Bartlett Cove to the naked Earth" near the ice. The rubble glaciers leave is "deficient in nitrogen"; alder and Dryas "improve the soil by adding nitrogen to it." Grokipedia: Sitka spruce by 100-150 years, western hemlock canopy around 200 years. Chapin and others (1994) found Dryas and alder helped spruce seedlings through added organic matter and nitrogen. (Grokipedia also calls fireweed nitrogen-fixing; it is not, and that claim is not used.)
    https://www.nps.gov/glba/learn/nature/change.htm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Glacier_Bay_National_Park_and_Preserve ; https://openpolar.no/Record/crwiley:10.2307%2F2937039

## How we know: Mount St. Helens after 1980

F16. Mount St. Helens erupted on 18 May 1980; the lateral blast "devastated approximately 230 square miles (600 km²) of forest north of the volcano" (Grokipedia). Survivors: "patches of late-lying snow shielded some plants from the blast"; some plants "survived in soils on the exposed rootwads of large blown-down trees" or resprouted "from perennial root stock"; hibernating frogs and toads and larval salamanders survived (USGS). Pocket gophers survived in their burrows and mixed old soil up through the ash (Grokipedia; UC Riverside). Fireweed sprouted from buried rootstocks by summer 1980 in less damaged areas.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mount_St._Helens ; https://grokipedia.com/page/1980_eruption_of_Mount_St._Helens ; https://webharvest.gov/peth04/20041021031853/http:/biology.usgs.gov/s+t/SNT/noframe/np105.htm ; https://cnas.ucr.edu/media/2024/11/07/how-gophers-revived-mount-st-helens-after-its-eruption

F17. In the pyroclastic-flow zone (the Pumice Plain) "no individuals survived" (USGS); "Vegetation establishment was also slowed by an absence of biological legacies" (Mount St. Helens Science and Learning Center). By 2016 vegetation had reclaimed most of the blast zone, but "the Pumice Plain and upper slopes" stayed largely barren (Grokipedia). The few survivors "will greatly accelerate the overall recovery process" (USGS). In the blowdown zone, where some life persisted, bird species reached about 70 percent of an undisturbed forest's by year 15, against 46 percent on the pyroclastic flows (USGS).
    https://webharvest.gov/peth04/20041021031853/http:/biology.usgs.gov/s+t/SNT/noframe/np105.htm ; https://www.mshslc.org/?p=822 ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mount_St._Helens

F18. "Two years after the eruption, scientists found the first plant on the Pumice Plain", a prairie lupine (Lupinus lepidus), which gets nitrogen "through an association with nitrogen-fixing bacteria on its roots"; its dead stems made "a net-like structure ... that helped trap windblown seeds" and a "favorable seedbed", and its yearly leaf drop added nutrients and organic matter to the pumice (MSHSLC). USGS: when lupine stands die they leave "a nutrient-rich substrate where other species can thrive." The pumice plain had very low nitrogen. So plants grew on the fresh pumice within two years: it did not stay too hot for plants for decades.
    https://www.mshslc.org/?p=822 ; https://webharvest.gov/peth04/20041021031853/http:/biology.usgs.gov/s+t/SNT/noframe/np105.htm ; https://grokipedia.com/page/1980_eruption_of_Mount_St._Helens

## How we know: Surtsey

F19. Surtsey's eruption began on the sea floor, and the island "reached the surface on 14 November 1963"; the eruption ended on 5 June 1967 (Wikipedia; Grokipedia). The island has been "a strict nature reserve" since 1965, open only to approved researchers (Grokipedia), so that succession can unfold "without outside interference" (Wikipedia). A tomato plant that grew from badly handled human waste was destroyed (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Surtsey ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surtsey

F20. Surtsey's first colonists: bacteria first (Grokipedia); sea rocket, the first vascular plant, in 1965 on the northern shore, carried by the sea (Wikipedia; Grokipedia), while the eruption still went on; mosses in 1967; lichens on the lava in 1970 (Wikipedia). "In the first 20 years, 20 species were observed, but only 10 became established" (Wikipedia). So bare lava does not stay too hot for plants for centuries.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surtsey ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Surtsey

F21. Gulls began nesting on Surtsey in the mid-1980s (Wikipedia: since 1984; Magnússon and others: "after 1985 ... the formation of a seagull colony"). The gulls "have had much more of an impact on plant colonisation" than other birds (Wikipedia). Magnússon and others (Biogeosciences, 2014): "seabirds, through their transfer of nutrients from sea to land, are major drivers of development of these ecosystems"; inside the gull colony plant cover, richness, diversity, biomass and soil carbon were "significantly higher", while low-impact areas "remained relatively barren". Gull droppings and pellets are rich in nitrogen and phosphorus. Birds also became the main carriers of seeds after 1985.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surtsey ; https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/11/5521/2014/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Surtsey

## The climax community, then and now

F22. Frederic Clements, Plant Succession (1916): one climax "determined primarily by the regional climate" (monoclimax), the climax as "the adult organism in the ecological sense" (Grokipedia, Climax community); "As an organism the formation arises, grows, matures, and dies" (Wikipedia, quoting Clements 1916). He held seres to be "highly predictable and deterministic", converging "on a climatically determined stable climax community" (Wikipedia). Henry Gleason (1926): "An association is not an organism, scarcely even a vegetational unit, but merely a coincidence" (Wikipedia).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Climax_community ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession

F23. The modern view: "the notion of a stable climax vegetation has been largely abandoned" (Wikipedia); succession is "neither entirely random nor entirely predictable" (Wikipedia). Disturbance is common enough that many systems never settle into a fixed climax; patch dynamics describes "frequent disturbances creating a mosaic of successional stages" (Grokipedia, Climax community). Climaxes need not be forest: fire-maintained chaparral (shrubland) in California is one (Grokipedia). Gibson (1996) wrote on "Textbook misconceptions: the climax concept of succession".
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Climax_community ; https://rd.springer.com/referenceworkentry/10.1007/978-90-481-9659-3_6

## Diversity along the way

F24. "The initial pioneer stage features low species diversity" (Grokipedia); "In the lichen stage, fauna is sparse" (Wikipedia). Plant diversity "often increases rapidly in early succession as pioneer species colonize" and "peaks during intermediate stages due to habitat heterogeneity and niche opportunities", and may "stabilize or decline toward climax communities" (Grokipedia); diversity "almost necessarily increases during early succession as new species arrive" and "may decline in later succession" (Wikipedia). Animal richness rises "as habitat complexity builds"; later stages bring "multi-layered canopies" and increases in "canopy height" (Grokipedia). At Glacier Bay the number of species rose with time (F14).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_succession ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession ; https://www.nps.gov/articles/cooper-succession-plots.htm

F25. Oxygen is 20.95 percent of dry air, and the main gases of the lower air are well mixed, "minimal horizontal spatial variations" (Grokipedia): the air over a moss field holds as much oxygen as the air in a forest.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Atmosphere_of_Earth

## Common misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. A climax community is fixed and never changes, one per climate (Clements's view, still in textbooks: Gibson 1996; a study guide's "climax community never changes" misconception).
    https://rd.springer.com/referenceworkentry/10.1007/978-90-481-9659-3_6 ; https://fiveable.me/ap-enviro/unit-2/ecological-succession/study-guide/9nK9VTaGMUhfWvoRySDr
M2. Primary and secondary differ only in speed, or in how violent the disturbance was; really they differ in the starting point (soil or no soil). A fire that kills every plant still leaves secondary succession.
    https://fiveable.me/ap-enviro/unit-2/ecological-succession/study-guide/9nK9VTaGMUhfWvoRySDr ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Secondary_succession
M3. Lichens and mosses are plants with roots (both lack true roots, F4, F6); mosses are not "real" plants that make food (they photosynthesize, F6).
M4. Forests make the oxygen that animals need there (the air is well mixed, F25).
M5. New lava must cool for centuries before life can start (sea rocket on Surtsey during the eruption, lichens three years after it, F20; lupine on Mount St. Helens's pumice in two years, F18).

## Added at revision (2026-11-26)

F26. Henry Gleason first challenged Clements in 1917, in "The Structure and Development of the Plant Association", describing an individualistic concept in which "the phenomena of vegetation depend completely upon the phenomena of the individual" species; in 1926 ("The Individualistic Concept of the Plant Association") he "expressed even stronger objections to Clements's theory" (Wikipedia, Henry A. Gleason). The "merely a coincidence" line is the 1926 paper's (F22).
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_A._Gleason ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession

F27. Arthur Tansley's polyclimax theory (1935) "proposes that within a single climatic region, multiple distinct climax communities can develop and persist as stable endpoints of succession", set by soil, topography, fire and grazing rather than climate alone (Grokipedia, Climax community). The same page: since the 1990s ecologists increasingly see the climax as "not an inevitable or enduring state", and "true climax endpoints are rare or nonexistent in real-world ecosystems". So a fixed climax for each soil is also not the modern view.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Climax_community

F28. Mount St. Helens, 1980 (Grokipedia, 1980 eruption): fireweed sprouted "from buried rootstocks and wind-dispersed seeds starting in summer 1980"; pocket gophers burrowed through the tephra, mixing ash with the soil beneath; pioneer plants emerged at "ash-covered sites where tephra was less than 8 inches thick"; the pyroclastic deposits run "3-12 meters on the pumice plain"; hot pumice "retained thermal energy for weeks post-eruption"; the lateral blast reached "several hundred degrees Celsius". USGS: in the blowdown zone wind-dispersed herbs "colonized the barren surfaces" in the first year and dominated many areas within 4 to 7 years, beside survivors under snow, on rootwads and resprouting from roots; in the pyroclastic-flow zone "no individuals survived". (So the first plants of the blast zone were not mostly survivors, and a key saying so is not used; the stem says only that the quicker ground had survivors and the slower had none.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/1980_eruption_of_Mount_St._Helens ; https://webharvest.gov/peth04/20041021031853/http:/biology.usgs.gov/s+t/SNT/noframe/np105.htm

F29. Cooper's plots a century on: Buma, Bisbing, Krapek and Wright (Ecology 98, 2017) found "almost a complete community composition replacement over the century and general species richness increase, but the effective number of species has declined significantly due to dominance of Salix species"; Buma and others (Ecology 100, 2019): "stochastic early community assembly and subsequent inhibition have dominated", and "no predictable species sequence or timeline was observed". NPS: the land is now about 130 years old; willows "almost completely monopolize resources ... at least for the first 130 years or so". (Why ccd53fe51e was dropped rather than re-keyed.)
    https://www.nps.gov/articles/cooper-succession-plots.htm ; https://scholarworks.alaska.edu/handle/11122/8176 ; https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/ecy.1848

F30. Diversity rises in succession because new species arrive: diversity "almost necessarily increases during early succession as new species arrive" (Wikipedia, as F24). New species form far more slowly: allopatric speciation is "often measured in thousands to millions of years", ecological speciation "can span decades to millennia" (Grokipedia, Speciation). So the forest's many kinds move in; they do not evolve on the spot in two centuries.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_succession ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Speciation
