# Electric Circuits: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-25. Grokipedia first; Wikipedia, school and university sources to corroborate and for the misconceptions.
Map line G5.P6 (TEKS 5.8B, TEKS 5.6A). Series and parallel circuits are G9 in the map, so they are not asked here.

## Facts

F1 An electric circuit is a closed loop that gives the current a path back to where it started; a complete circuit has no gaps. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_network ("An electrical circuit is a network consisting of a closed loop, giving a return path for the current."); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-primary-ks2/units/changing-circuits/lessons/the-position-of-switches-open-and-closed (Oak National Academy, Year 6, ages 10-11: "An electrical circuit is a closed loop or path that electricity can flow through to make a component work"; "A complete electrical circuit is made when all components are connected together correctly", with "no gaps in the circuit")

F2 The grade's standard. TEKS 5.8B: "demonstrate that electrical energy in complete circuits can be transformed into motion, light, sound, or thermal energy and identify the requirements for a functioning electrical circuit". The TEKS guide's glossary defines a complete circuit as "a closed path through which electricity can travel", and gives a fan (motion), an LED or bulb (light) and a speaker (sound) as examples; "A light bulb, resistor, or any device that is not 100% efficient will transform electrical energy into thermal energy." https://teksguide.org/teks/s58b-0

F3 Electric current is the flow of electric charge through a conductor; in metal wires the charges that move are electrons. https://grokipedia.com/page/Electric_current ("Electric current is the flow of electric charge through a conducting medium"; "in metallic conductors, the actual charge carriers are negatively charged electrons")

F4 A battery turns stored chemical energy into electrical energy, and it drives a current only when it is joined into a circuit. https://grokipedia.com/page/Electric_battery ("An electric battery is a device that consists of one or more electrochemical cells"; it converts stored chemical energy into electrical energy; its voltage "drives current when connected to an external circuit")

F5 A bulb lights only when the electricity can pass through it, in at one contact of its base and out at the other, and on round the loop back to the battery's other end. A single wire run from one end of a battery to the bulb's base, with nothing else touching, leaves the bulb dark; one wire can light it only if the bulb's base also touches the battery's other end (the wire to the side of the base, the tip of the base on the battery). NSTA's probe "Batteries, Bulbs, and Wires" (grade 4) asks whether a bulb can be lit with one wire, to find out whether students understand "the pathway of electricity through a complete circuit, including through the bulb". https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/batteries-bulbs-and-wires

F6 A switch opens or closes the path. When its metal contacts touch, current flows and the switch (and the circuit) is closed, or on; when they separate, a gap stops the current and the switch is open, or off. https://grokipedia.com/page/Switch ("a switch is an electrical component that can disconnect or connect the conducting path in an electrical circuit"; "When a pair of contacts is touching current can flow between them; when the contacts are separated no current can flow"; "a switch in the open state (off position) interrupts the circuit"); Oak National Academy, Year 6 (URL in F1): "Switches can turn a circuit off by making a gap so electricity cannot flow"; "Traditional switches work by making a pair of conductors touch each other or separate from each other"; exit quiz: when a traditional switch is closed, "a pair of conductors touch each other"

F7 The words open and closed confuse learners, because for a switch they mean the opposite of what they mean for a door: an open door lets people through, an open switch lets no current through. https://www.allaboutcircuits.com/worksheets/switches/4/ ("students new to electricity often exhibit confusion over these terms"; "in the context of doors they hold opposite meanings")

F8 In a single loop, a gap anywhere stops the current all the way round, so a switch works the same wherever it sits, before the bulb or after it. https://sites.google.com/bpsi.org.uk/members-area/subject-areas/science/electricity_6_concept ("A switch placed at A, B or C will have exactly the same effect: the circuit will be broken and neither bulb will light."); https://geniebook.com/exam-preparation/psle/article/psle-electric-circuits-misconceptions-guide (Primary 5-6, ages 10-12: "It doesn't matter where that gap is in a simple series circuit")

F9 Copper is the metal of house and appliance wiring: it conducts electricity better than any metal but silver, silver costs 50 to 100 times as much, and copper bends without cracking. The tall overhead power lines mostly use aluminium, which is lighter and cheaper. https://grokipedia.com/page/Copper_conductor ("Copper conductors are the predominant choice for building and appliance wiring"; copper "outperforms most metals except silver"; silver's "higher cost—often 50-100 times that of copper per unit mass"; "Copper's ductility allows easy installation via bending and pulling through conduits without cracking"; "overhead lines predominantly utilize aluminum conductors due to their lower density and cost"); https://www.thenational.academy/pupils/lessons/choosing-and-using-conductors-and-insulators-non-statutory/video (Oak, Year 6: "Copper is a good electrical conductor, which is why it is often used to make wires for electrical circuits.")

F10 How well metals conduct electricity at 20 C (millions of siemens per metre): silver 63.0, copper 59.6, gold 41.1, aluminium 35.5, iron 10.3. So gold conducts less well than copper, and iron only about a sixth as well. Silver is the best conductor of the elements. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistivity_and_conductivity (table); https://grokipedia.com/page/Electrical_resistivity_and_conductivity ("silver, the best conductor among elements"; silver 6.30 x 10^7, copper 5.96 x 10^7, aluminum 3.5 x 10^7 S/m); https://grokipedia.com/page/Gold (gold's conductivity is "surpassed only by copper and silver among pure metals"; gold is "electroplated onto connectors, circuit board edge fingers, and semiconductor bonding wires", not used for house wiring)

F11 An insulator does not let electricity pass easily: plastic, rubber, wood, glass, porcelain and air are insulators. Wires are coated in plastic or rubber to keep the current in the wire and keep people safe from shocks; PVC is the common plastic coat. https://grokipedia.com/page/Insulator_(electricity) (an insulator's charges are not free, "and thus resist the flow of electric current"; "Common examples of insulators include glass, rubber, porcelain, and air"; used "to coat wires or separate components"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/electrical-conductors-and-insulators (Oak, Year 4: "Examples of electrical insulators are plastic, rubber, wood, glass and air"); Oak Year 6 video (URL in F9): "To help keep us safe from electrical shocks, wires are covered by electrical insulators made from plastic or rubber."; https://grokipedia.com/page/Electrical_wiring ("Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) is widely used due to its flexibility, cost-effectiveness"; "Insulation materials are critical for preventing electrical leakage"); https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/science-primary-ks2/units/changing-circuits/lessons/choosing-and-using-conductors-and-insulators-non-statutory (Oak, Year 6: "Electrical insulators help keep us safe from the dangerous effects of electricity flowing through conductors."; a hair dryer's conductor is "the wire inside of the cable")

F12 An electric shock happens only when current flows through the body, which needs the body to complete a circuit. Touching a single live wire with no return path gives no significant shock; touching a live wire and the ground completes the circuit. Wet skin lets far more current flow. https://grokipedia.com/page/Electrical_injury ("Electric shock is caused by the flow of electric current through the body"; it "requires completion of a circuit, typically via entry and exit points on the skin"; a single live wire with no return path "results in no significant electric shock, as no appreciable current flows through the body"; a wire plus a path to ground "completes the circuit, allowing significant current to flow and potentially causing severe shock"; "Wet skin or breaches reduce contact resistance to below 1,000 Ω, increasing current flow")

F13 A bird with both feet on one power line is unharmed because its body is not part of a complete circuit: both feet are at the same voltage, and it touches nothing else. A bird that touches two lines, or a line and a grounded pole or tower, completes a circuit and can be electrocuted; electrocution kills many birds every year (one 2014 study estimated 0.9 to 11.6 million a year in the United States). The lines on poles and towers are bare metal, not insulated wire. https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/1341 ("you need a complete circuit to flow current through the bird to cause it damage"; "Birds are safe on electified conductors as long as they are only in contact with a single phase"; "The power lines on poles are NOT insulated wires, i.e., they do not have insulation wrapped around them."); https://engineering.mit.edu/ask-an-engineer/how-do-birds-sit-on-high-voltage-power-lines-without-getting-electrocuted (its "two feet are at the same electrical potential"; it "would also be dangerous for a bird to sit on the pole and touch a wire"); https://energized.edison.com/stories/why-can-birds-sit-safely-on-power-lines ("If a bird were to touch two wires at once, it would create a circuit."); https://www.iflscience.com/why-do-birds-sit-on-high-voltage-power-lines-and-how-come-they-dont-get-fried-75200 (the 2014 estimate); https://www.ipm.org/amomentofscience/bird-on-a-wire/ (the wires are "actually not insulated at all")

F14 The bare overhead lines are held away from their poles and towers by insulators of porcelain (ceramic), glass or, in newer designs, polymer, which keep the current out of the grounded structures. https://grokipedia.com/page/Insulator_(electricity) (insulators are used for "electrically isolating conductors from supporting structures"; "separating live conductors from grounded structures or each other"; pin types with "a threaded porcelain or glass body"; suspension types that "suspends vertically from the tower cross-arm"; "Composite long-rod suspension insulators feature a single elongated fiberglass core with polymer sheds"); Illinois Physics Van (URL in F13): "The insulation comes into play at the ceramic insulators that hold the wires away from the wooden or metal poles/towers."

F15 Perfectly pure water is an excellent insulator, but ordinary water (tap water, bath water) carries dissolved minerals, and these let it conduct electricity. That is why electricity and water are a dangerous mix. https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/conductivity-electrical-conductance-and-water (USGS: "pure water is actually an excellent insulator and does not conduct electricity"; "Water stops being an excellent insulator once it starts dissolving substances around it"; "Even a small amount of ions in a water solution makes it able to conduct electricity"; tap water contains "significant amounts of dissolved substances, minerals, and chemicals"; mixing water and electricity is very dangerous); https://grokipedia.com/page/Properties_of_water ("The electrical conductivity of ultrapure water is extremely low"; with electrolytes "conductivity rises dramatically"); Oak, Year 4 (URL in F11): "There are minerals that can conduct electricity when dissolved in water."

F16 Electrical-safety rules taught to children: "Never use anything with a cord or plug around water", "Never fly kites near power lines", "Stay away from broken or fallen power lines", "Never put fingers or other objects in an outlet" (BC Hydro); "Don't touch electrical objects with wet hands", "Don't use electrical objects in the bathroom" (a UK teachers' guide). https://www.bchydro.com/safety-outages/stay-safe/safety-at-home/safety-for-kids.html; https://www.highspeedtraining.co.uk/hub/electrical-safety-for-kids/

F17 A circuit's device changes electrical energy into another form: a motor into motion, a bulb into light (and heat), a buzzer into sound. https://grokipedia.com/page/Electric_motor ("An electric motor is an electromechanical device that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Buzzer ("an audio signaling device that produces a buzzing or beeping sound"); TEKS guide examples (F2)

F18 A conductor is a material that lets electricity pass through it easily (copper and other metals); a cable is a bundle of insulated wires in one outer jacket, only one part of a circuit. https://www.thenational.academy/teachers/lessons/electrical-conductors-and-insulators (Oak, Year 4: "An electrical conductor is a material that allows electricity to pass through it easily."); https://grokipedia.com/page/Electrical_cable ("An electrical cable is an assembly of one or more insulated electrical conductors under a common or integral jacket")

## Misconceptions a 5th grader may hold (for honest wrong choices)

M1 One wire from the battery to the bulb is enough (the "unipolar" model): younger students draw "a single wire going from the top of the battery (unipolar model) to the bulb", and some think energy simply flows along the one wire to the bulb. NSTA probe (F5).

M2 Both wires carry electricity to the bulb (the "clashing currents" or bipolar model), or one wire is "active" and the other a "safety wire". NSTA probe (F5).

M3 Current is used up by a bulb. https://geniebook.com/exam-preparation/psle/article/psle-electric-circuits-misconceptions-guide ("Current Gets "Used Up" by Bulbs"; the correction: "the flow of electric current is constant at every single point")

M4 A switch after a bulb cannot turn it off, because the electricity has already passed the bulb. Same geniebook guide ("A Switch's Position in a Series Circuit Matters"); BPSI's guide ties this kind of thinking to the idea that "electricity comes out of the battery" (F8).

M5 "Open" means on and "closed" means off, as for a door or a tap. allaboutcircuits (F7).

M6 Power lines are coated, and that is why birds are safe. Illinois Physics Van: the first answer's insulation explanation was corrected ("The power lines on poles are NOT insulated wires"); A Moment of Science: "You might think it's because the wires are insulated" (F13).

M7 Only metals conduct, and water is an insulator. Oak Year 4 and Year 6 list "Only metals are electrical conductors. All non-metals are electrical insulators." and teach water with dissolved minerals as the counterexample (F11, F15).

M8 Author's judgment, for lures with no survey found: that a switch can let "less" through, like a dimmer; that electricity is stored in a wire and keeps a bulb lit for a while after a break; that a plastic coat speeds electricity along or slows it to a safe speed, or stores it; that water soaks up and holds electricity, or pulls it in like a magnet; that a bird's body does not conduct (refuted by birds electrocuted on two lines, F13); that a power line's current is too weak to harm a bird (refuted by F12-F13); that steel does not conduct (refuted by F10 and the materials unit); that electricity moves too fast to turn down a tower; that a switch is needed to start a current flowing; that a single wire is too thin to carry enough electricity.

M9 Rubber underfoot protects from electricity (the source of the "rubber pads under the towers" lure, which no tower has). The National Weather Service lists the myth "The rubber soles of shoes or rubber tires on a car will protect your from being struck by lightning", with the fact "Rubber-soled shoes and rubber tires provide NO protection from lightning." https://weather.gov/fwd/ltgmythvfact

## Notes and cautions

- One wire CAN light a bulb if the bulb's base also touches the battery (F5): the one-wire question says "nothing else touches", and no choice says two wires are always needed.
- Pure water is an insulator (F15): the water question says tap water with minerals dissolved in it, so "blocks electricity, as rubber does" is wrong for that water.
- Power lines on poles and towers are bare (F13); low house drops, cable and telephone lines can be insulated. The bird and tower questions name the thick lines between tall steel towers.
- Overhead lines are mostly aluminium (F9): the copper question names a house's walls and a lamp's cord, and aluminium is not a choice. Gold is used in thin bonding wires inside chips (F10), not in house wiring.
- Mains current is alternating; no question here describes the direction of flow in a house circuit.
- A short circuit (a wire straight across a battery) was left out: a zinc-carbon cell may give too little current to heat a wire, so "the wire grows hot" is not always true at this grade's level.
- A pencil's graphite core was left out as a conductor lure or key: Scientific American notes it may make a flashlight bulb very dim or not light it at all.
- Overlaps with other units: telling-materials-apart owns "which material conducts" (its battery-and-bulb tester with a steel paper clip); here metal and plastic appear only inside a switch, a wire and a tower. energy-changes-form owns the energy changes (a bulb into light and heat, a fan's motor into motion, a phone's speaker into sound, a battery's chemical energy); here they appear only in contexts. caring-for-air-water-and-soil (T2) owns LED against incandescent bulbs.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-25, checked on the web now)

F19 Every electrical circuit needs a cell or a battery as well as wires and a device; the cell or battery gives the push that starts the electricity flowing round the circuit. https://www.thenational.academy/pupils/programmes/science-primary-year-6/units/changing-circuits/lessons/components-and-simple-circuits/video (Oak National Academy, Year 6, ages 10-11: "All electrical circuits need to have a cell or a battery, wires for the electricity to flow through"; "a device such as a bulb, buzzer, or motor"; "Remember, an electrical circuit must have a cell or a battery."; "Electricity passes around a circuit when a cell or battery gives a push to start the flow of electricity."); the unit's scope names "an energy source (a battery)" first among a working circuit's needs; TEKS 5.8B (F2) asks for "the requirements for a functioning electrical circuit".

F20 Silver's price against copper's moves with the market, and silver now costs well over a hundred times as much by weight, so the writer's "fifty to a hundred times" (F9, Grokipedia's historical range) is out of date and the context now says only "many times as much". Silver was $45.26 an ounce on 19 November 2026 (https://fortune.com/article/current-price-of-silver-11-19-2026/, about $1,455 a kilogram); LME three-month copper closed near $12,471 a tonne on 18 November 2026 (https://news.metal.com/newscontent/HPSJFB5W7OIYMM4YPU4ZEOJU7MTVWGRUBNTCQ2A-lme-copper-hits-four-month-low-as-ai-selloff-and-china-weakness-weigh, about $12.50 a kilogram).

F21 In a simple loop, a break anywhere puts the bulb out at once. https://www.allaboutcircuits.com/textbook/experiments/chpt-2/circuit-with-a-switch/ ("any break in the circuit at any location causes the lamp to immediately de-energize (darken)"); see also F8.

F22 A bird on one bare power line carries almost none of its current ("The current keeps going through the wire. Almost none of it detours through the bird."; electricity cannot flow through a bird "unless the bird is also touching another wire"; big birds can "stand with one foot on each of two different power lines", https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/1341). Birds are electrocuted "where conducting wires (conductors) are placed closer together than the wingspan of birds", and "If a bird's appendages bridge the gap between two energized parts or between an energized and a grounded metal part" (USGS, https://www.usgs.gov/publications/electrocution). MIT: a bird is in danger "If that bird stretches out a wing or a leg and touches a second wire", and "it would also be dangerous for a bird to sit on the pole and touch a wire" (https://engineering.mit.edu/ask-an-engineer/how-do-birds-sit-on-high-voltage-power-lines-without-getting-electrocuted). So a bird's feet carry electricity as its wings do, and the danger is a body joining two lines (or a line and a tower), not a store of electricity in the body.

F23 Water itself is not a source of electricity. At a hydroelectric dam, falling water turns turbines, and the turbines drive generators that make the electricity. https://grokipedia.com/page/Hydroelectricity ("electricity generated by converting the gravitational potential energy of water into mechanical energy", "which turbines extract to produce rotational mechanical power, subsequently driving synchronous generators"). A bath's water has no generator; it only carries electricity that reaches it (F15).

M10 Author's judgment (no survey found): that water makes electricity of its own, from "water power" at dams (refuted by F23); used as the lure "It makes electricity of its own" for bath water.

M11 Author's judgment: that a cord's soft plastic coat is what lets the metal wire bend (refuted by F9: copper bends without cracking on its own); used as the lure "It makes the metal wire soft enough to bend".

M12 Author's judgment: that a bird's feet do not carry electricity, so only its wings are in danger (refuted by F22: birds with one foot on each of two lines, or on a pole while touching a wire, are electrocuted); and that a body can store up electricity from two lines (refuted by F12: a shock is current flowing through the body, which needs a complete circuit).
