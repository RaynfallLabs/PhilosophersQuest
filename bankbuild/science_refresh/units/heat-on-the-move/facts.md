## Which way heat flows, and where it stops

F1. Heat flows on its own only from a warmer thing to a cooler one, never the other way round. The NSTA probe "Ice Cold Lemonade" (grades 6-12): ice in warm lemonade, the best answer "The heat from the lemonade moved into the ice", and "Thermal energy can move only from a warmer object or area to a cooler object or area, never the other way around."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Heat_transfer ; https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/ice-cold-lemonade-0

F2. Thermal equilibrium: things in thermal contact exchange heat until they reach the same temperature; then heat stops flowing between them (Grokipedia's example: two metal blocks at different temperatures conduct heat across their contact until both share one final temperature).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_equilibrium

F3. The shared final temperature lies between the two starting temperatures, because the heat the hot thing loses is the heat the cool thing gains. OpenStax's worked example: a 150 degree Celsius aluminium pan and 20 degree water end together at 59.1 degrees.
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity

F4. MISCONCEPTIONS (middle school): "Heat and cold are substances or entities, and different from one another" (MOSART, grades 5-8); that when a cold and a warm thing touch, "coldness" moves from the cold one; that heat keeps flowing after the two reach the same temperature (MOSART 9-12). NSTA: "many students think that cold moves", and middle school students may still struggle with the direction of flow after teaching.
    https://waps.cfa.harvard.edu/mosart/misconceptions.php ; https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/ice-cold-lemonade-0

## Conduction

F5. Conduction is heat transfer through matter by physical contact (OpenStax). Its mechanism: when two particles collide, energy passes from the one with more kinetic energy to the other, and the sum of these collisions is a flow of heat from hot to cold (OpenStax 14.5); Grokipedia: "successive collisions or vibrations of particles". In conduction the material stays in place; in convection the material itself moves, so a solid cannot carry heat by everyday convection (schoolphysics).
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-4-heat-transfer-methods ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_conduction ; https://www.schoolphysics.co.uk/age11-14/Heat%20energy/Transfer%20of%20heat%20energy/text/Convection_/index.html

F6. Metals are good conductors of heat (free electrons); wood, plastic and rubber are poor ones; gases such as air have very low conductivity. OpenStax table 14.3 (J/s m degree C): copper 390, steel 80, glass 0.84, wood 0.08-0.16, glass wool 0.042, down feathers 0.025, air 0.023. So solid glass conducts about 35 times better than air, and glass wool about 20 times worse than solid glass.
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_conduction

F7. The caloric theory, a respectable wrong theory of the past: heat as "an invisible, imponderable fluid known as caloric, which flows from hotter to colder bodies". The name was coined in the late 1780s in Antoine Lavoisier's reform of chemical names. Count Rumford's cannon-boring experiments (1798) and James Joule's work undermined it, and it gave way to the kinetic theory: heat is energy transferred, not a fluid.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Caloric_theory

## Convection

F8. Convection is heat carried by the bulk movement of a fluid (a liquid or a gas). Warmer fluid expands, becomes less dense and rises; cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place, and the loop is a convection current. OpenStax: hot water in a pot "expands, decreases in density, and rises", and a gravity furnace's air "expands and rises, forming a convective loop". (Caveat: over millions of years solid mantle rock and glacier ice convect too, so the bank never says convection is impossible in any solid; F5's schoolphysics line is about everyday solids.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Convection ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Heat_transfer ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-6-convection

F9. The classic demonstration: crystals of potassium permanganate (it colours water purple) are dropped so they fall near one side of a beaker of water, and the base is heated just under them. The colour rises up the heated side, crosses the top and falls down the other side (schoolphysics); in a glass loop tube heated at one corner the purple streaks travel round the tube (preproom).
    https://www.schoolphysics.co.uk/age11-14/Heat%20energy/Transfer%20of%20heat%20energy/text/Convection_/index.html ; https://www.preproom.org/practicals/pr.aspx?prID=1007

F10. Despite its name, a household hot-water radiator gives out most of its heat by convection: about 70-80 percent by convection and 20-30 percent by radiation (Grokipedia). The air it warms grows less dense and rises, setting up currents that carry the warmed air round the room. Wikipedia: such devices "tend to transfer heat mainly by convection and might logically be called convectors".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Radiator_(heating) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiator_(heating)

F11. MISCONCEPTION: "heat rises". Christopher Baird, a physics professor at West Texas A&M University: "Heat does not rise, hot air does (usually)"; heat by conduction and thermal radiation spreads in all directions, not just up. A warm thing in contact with a cooler one passes heat by conduction whichever way they touch (F5).
    https://wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/2014/07/10/what-makes-heat-rise/

## Radiation

F12. Thermal radiation is energy carried by electromagnetic waves, given off by all matter above absolute zero, mostly infrared at everyday temperatures; it needs no medium and crosses a vacuum; hotter things radiate more. OpenStax: "no medium is required for electromagnetic waves to propagate"; the space between Earth and the Sun is "largely empty, without any possibility of heat transfer by convection or conduction", and Earth gets almost all its energy as radiation from the Sun; the warming of Earth by the Sun is "an obvious example" of radiation.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_radiation ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-7-radiation ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-4-heat-transfer-methods

F13. A sunlit object is heated by radiation (sunlight absorbed); heat then passes into anything touching it by conduction (contact). The curriculum line's own example is a metal railing hot in the sun. Metals absorb and pass on heat this way; a dark surface absorbs better than a light one (OpenStax: black asphalt hotter than grey sidewalk).
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-4-heat-transfer-methods ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-7-radiation

F14. Beside a campfire, nearly all the heat a person feels comes as thermal radiation; the fire's hot air (convection) "shoots straight up into the sky and never reaches you", and the side of the body facing away from the fire is in shadow and gets none of the radiation (Baird). OpenStax: in a fireplace "Radiation is responsible for most of the heat transferred into the room."
    https://wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/2015/02/26/when-i-sit-by-a-campfire-how-does-its-hot-air-heat-me/ ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-4-heat-transfer-methods

F15. Shiny metal surfaces are poor absorbers and poor radiators: they reflect most of the radiation that falls on them (Grokipedia: "metals reflect most incident radiation", polished metals emissivity about 0.02-0.1 in the mid-infrared; OpenStax: a white or silver object is a poor absorber and a poor radiator).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_radiation ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-7-radiation

F16. A space blanket is a thin plastic film (often PET) with a vapour-deposited layer of aluminium, first developed by NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center in 1964. Its reflective surface sends back up to 97 percent of the heat a body radiates (the advertised 90 percent refers to radiant heat only); the airtight sheet also cuts convection (wind) and evaporation. Space blankets are often given to marathon runners at the end of races.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Space_blanket ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_blanket

## Insulators

F17. Insulators slow the flow of heat. Air is a poor conductor and so a good insulator, but it carries heat readily by convection; materials such as fur, fibre and fibreglass insulate by "trapping it in spaces too small to support convection" (OpenStax). Glass wool (fibreglass) traps "small pockets of air" and its "small, stationary air voids" cut conduction and convection (Grokipedia). Down traps air pockets too (Grokipedia, Thermal insulation); a double-paned window's gap of about 1 cm "prevents convection" (OpenStax).
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-6-convection ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Glass_wool ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Thermal_insulation

## The vacuum flask

F18. James Dewar, a Scottish chemist and physicist, invented the vacuum flask in 1892 at the Royal Institution in London, to hold liquefied gases at extremely low temperatures. Cooling gases until they turned liquid was costly, so he needed them to stay liquid long enough to study (Royal Institution). He liquefied hydrogen in 1898. The flask was first shown at the Royal Institution on Christmas Day 1892.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Vacuum_flask ; https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Dewar ; https://www.rigb.org/explore-science/explore/collection/james-dewars-vacuum-flask

F19. How the flask works: two walls, one inside the other, with a partial vacuum between them. The vacuum "significantly reduces conduction and convection", which need matter; radiation still crosses a vacuum, so the walls are silvered to reflect radiant heat.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Vacuum_flask ; https://grokipedia.com/page/James_Dewar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_flask

F20. The flask slows heat both ways: it keeps cold things cold as well as hot things hot (Grokipedia: "effective for both preserving heat in hot liquids and cold in chilled ones"). Dewar never patented it; in 1904 a company founded by two German glassblowers (Reinhold Burger and Albert Aschenbrenner) made it for everyday use under the name Thermos, from the Greek word for heat.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Vacuum_flask ; https://www.rigb.org/explore-science/explore/collection/james-dewars-vacuum-flask

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F21. Heat by conduction passes from a warmer thing to a cooler one it touches in any direction, down as well as up; it is warm air, not heat, that rises. Christopher Baird (West Texas A&M), re-checked: "Heat does not rise, hot air does (usually)"; "Heat traveling by conduction can travel in all directions as well"; with one end of a sloping metal rod in a flame, "the heat from the flame will have no problem traveling down the rod to the bottom end via conduction." (Used by the re-aimed ice-pack question: a pack under the ankle draws heat down out of it.)
    https://wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/2014/07/10/what-makes-heat-rise/

F22. The radiator's split is for a typical one: Grokipedia, "In typical heating radiators, convection accounts for approximately 70-80% of the total heat output, while radiation contributes the remaining 20-30%"; the split shifts with surface area, temperature and orientation (a finless panel at low water temperature can be far more radiant, Plumbing & Mechanical).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Radiator_(heating) ; https://www.pmmag.com/articles/104489-john-siegenthaler-how-radiant-are-your-radiators

F23. OpenStax table 14.3, re-checked: aluminium 220, copper 390, glass (average) 0.84, glass wool 0.042, air 0.023 J/s m degree C; "good conductors of electricity (metals like copper, aluminum, gold, and silver) are also good heat conductors." So a space blanket's aluminium is a good conductor, not a poor one, and solid glass conducts about 20 times better than glass wool.
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction

F24. A space blanket, re-checked on Grokipedia: a plastic film with "a vapor-deposited layer of aluminum to create a highly reflective surface", "reflecting up to 97% of radiant body heat back toward the user"; its metallized surface has low emissivity, "typically around 0.05 to 0.1" (so it absorbs and gives off little radiation); the sheet is "a barrier to wind and air movement" and limits evaporative heat loss.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Space_blanket

On F18: the Royal Institution calls the 1892 vessel "Dewar's first-ever vacuum flask" (his first), but earlier vacuum-walled vessels are recorded (Dewar's own goblet with Tait, 1872; Weinhold's double-walled bottle, 1881), so "the first vacuum flask" is not claimed anywhere. (The Dewar question was dropped.)
