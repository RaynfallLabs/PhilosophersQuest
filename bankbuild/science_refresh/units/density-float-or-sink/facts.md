## What density is

F1. Density is the mass of a substance per unit volume: how much mass is packed into a given volume.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Density ("quantifies the mass of a substance per unit volume") ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle

F2. Density is an intensive property: it stays the same for a given substance whatever the size of the sample, so a small piece has the same density as a large piece of the same material.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Density ("As an intensive property, density remains constant for a given substance regardless of the sample size") ; https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/floating-logs (Page Keeley, Uncovering Student Ideas in Science vol. 2: "Density is an intensive property of matter, which means that it is independent of the amount of material")

F3. Quartz has a density (specific gravity) of 2.65 grams per cubic centimetre; it is made of silicon dioxide. Water's density is 1.000 grams per cubic centimetre.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Quartz ("Its specific gravity is 2.65 g/cm3") ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle ("water has a density of 1.000 g/cm3")

## Floating and sinking in any fluid

F4. An object floats in a fluid when its average density is less than the fluid's, and sinks when it is denser than the fluid. The rule holds for any fluid, liquid or gas.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Buoyancy ("if less than 1, the object floats; if greater than 1, it sinks", of specific gravity against the fluid) ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle ("If its average density is less than that of the surrounding fluid, it will float"; "an object denser than the fluid will sink"; "The average density of an object is what ultimately determines whether it floats")

F5. Archimedes' principle: the upward buoyant force on an object in a fluid equals the weight of the fluid it pushes aside (displaces). (Background for the contexts; no question asks it.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Buoyancy ("is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the object")

## Liquids in layers

F6. Liquids that do not mix settle into layers by density: the densest sinks to the bottom and the less dense stays above it. Motor oil floats on top of water in a puddle.
    https://www.teachengineering.org/activities/view/cub_flow_activity2 (TeachEngineering, "Density Rainbow and the Great Viscosity Race": "the densest fluid sinks to the bottom and the less dense fluid stays above it"; "motor oil floats on top of water in a puddle")

F7. Thickness (viscosity, how slowly a liquid flows) is a different property from density. Oil is far thicker than water (olive oil about 56 mPa s against water's 1.0) yet floats on it.
    https://www.teachengineering.org/activities/view/cub_flow_activity2 ("Absolute viscosity is independent of density"; the two are often confused because dense fluids are usually more viscous) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity (table: water 1.0016 mPa s at 20 C, olive oil 56.2 mPa s at 26 C)

F8. Spilled crude oil, typically 0.8 to 0.95 grams per cubic centimetre, spreads across the sea's surface as a thin slick because it is less dense than water; booms are floating barriers that corral it. (Some heavy oils are denser than water and sink, so no line says every oil floats.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Oil_spill ("spreads rapidly across water surfaces due to its lower density compared to water, forming thin slicks"; "oil density (typically 0.8-0.95 g/cm3 for crude)"; booms are "floating barriers deployed to encircle and corral oil"; Group 5 oils have "densities greater than water, leading to submersion")

## A metal that floats on a metal

F9. Mercury is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature; its density is about 13.55 grams per cubic centimetre. Iron's density is 7.874 grams per cubic centimetre. Gold's is about 19.3.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mercury_(element) ("the only metallic element that remains liquid at standard room temperature and pressure"; "a density of 13.55 g/cm3") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Density (element table: iron 7.874 g/cm3; mercury 13.6 g/cm3 at 20 C) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gold (infobox "19.32 g/cm3"; body "19.3-19.4 g/cm3"), so gold, denser than mercury, sinks in it (F4)

F10. Objects denser than water but less dense than mercury float on mercury, held up by buoyancy: a round pound coin (about 7.6 grams per cubic centimetre) "floats on mercury due to the buoyancy force upon it". Iron does not dissolve in mercury (no amalgam), and iron flasks have traditionally carried it. Mercury is diamagnetic: a magnet does not pull it.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_(element) ("A round pound coin (density ~7.6 g/cm3) floats on mercury due to the buoyancy force upon it"; "Iron is an exception, and iron flasks have traditionally been used to transport the material"; infobox: magnetic ordering diamagnetic) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mercury_(element) (mercury supports "objects denser than water but less dense than itself, such as a British pound coin")

## Ice and the frozen lake

F11. Ice is less dense than liquid water: about 0.917 grams per cubic centimetre against about 1 at 0 C. Water expands by about 9 percent in volume as it freezes, because hydrogen bonds lock its molecules into an open, cage-like (hexagonal) lattice that takes up more room than the liquid.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice ("approximately 0.917 g/cm3 compared to 1 g/cm3"; "Upon freezing, water expands by about 9% in volume"; an open lattice with voids) ; https://wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/2013/12/05/why-does-ice-form-on-the-top-of-a-lake/ (Christopher Baird, West Texas A&M: "Ice is less dense than water because of the way it forms a hexagonal crystalline structure")

F12. Because ice floats, a lake freezes over at its surface and liquid water stays beneath the ice; the ice lid slows the water's loss of heat to the cold air, and a deep enough pond keeps liquid water at the bottom all winter.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice (liquid water persists beneath surface ice; lake ice spreads from the edges) ; https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/surprises-ice (University of Alaska Geophysical Institute, Alaska Science Forum: the surface ice lid will "seal in the water below and slow its rate of heat loss to the frigid air"; deep ponds keep water at about 4 C at the bottom)

F13. Most substances are denser as solids than as liquids. If water behaved like them, its ice would sink and a pond "would solidify from the bottom up".
    https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/surprises-ice (if water behaved like most substances "it would be heavier in its solid phase than in its liquid state"; such a pond "would solidify from the bottom up") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Density (water's density anomaly "allows ice to float while denser water sinks")

## Salt water holds things up better

F14. Seawater averages about 35 grams of dissolved salts per kilogram (about 3.5 percent) and has a typical surface density of about 1.025 grams per cubic centimetre, against 1.000 for fresh water. Dissolved salt adds mass without adding as much volume, so salt water is denser and holds things up better.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Seawater ("density = 1.025 kg/L"; "averaging 35 grams of dissolved salts per kilogram of seawater") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Buoyancy ("higher salt concentrations increase density by adding mass without proportionally increasing volume") ; https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/12884 (University of Illinois physics: "Almost all salts ... form watery solutions that are denser than pure water")

F15. The denser the liquid, the easier it is to float on it: a floating body pushes aside less of a denser liquid to be held up, so it rides higher (ships' load lines carry a separate fresh-water mark for this). In a very salty lake such as the Dead Sea, about 1.24 grams per cubic centimetre and roughly ten times saltier than the ocean (about 340 grams of salt per litre), people float effortlessly on their backs.
    https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/12884 ("The denser the liquid, the easier it is to float on it, since anything less dense than the liquid floats") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Waterline (load lines set by water density; "the freshwater load line (F)"; a floating object displaces its own weight of fluid) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Dead_Sea ("a density of approximately 1.24 g/cm3"; dissolved salts about 340-348 g per litre; "its high density allows humans to float effortlessly on the surface")

F16. A person floats when the body's average density is less than the water's; body density is one indicator of a person's share of body fat. (Background for the swimmer's context.)
    https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle ("Her density is less than the fluid density. We expect this because she floats"; "Body density is one indicator of a person's percent body fat")

## Steel ships

F17. Steel's density is 7.75 to 8.05 grams per cubic centimetre, about 7.85 on average (nearly eight times water's), so a solid block of steel sinks. A steel ship floats because its large hull encloses a great volume of air, which brings the average density of the whole ship, steel and air together, below water's. A lump of clay sinks, but the same clay shaped into a boat floats, and "the same is true of steel ships".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Steel ("between 7.75 and 8.05 g/cm3, averaging 7.85 g/cm3") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Buoyancy (steel ships "achieve an effective specific gravity below 1 through their large enclosed volume of air") ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle ("The same is true of steel ships")

F18. Submarines change their average density with ballast tanks: flooded with water to dive, blown out with compressed air to rise. (Background; no question keys it.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Buoyancy (tanks "can be flooded with water to increase the submarine's weight") ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle ("Submarines have adjustable density (ballast tanks) so that they may float or sink as desired")

## Floating in air

F19. Air is a fluid too, about 1.2 kilograms per cubic metre at sea level. Helium is about 0.178 kilograms per cubic metre, far less dense than air, so a helium balloon rises. A hot-air balloon rises because heating its air lowers that air's density to about 0.9 kilograms per cubic metre, below the cooler air around it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Buoyancy ("Helium-filled balloons, in contrast, rely on helium's inherent low density of 0.178 kg/m3 at standard conditions"; heated air about 0.9 kg/m3 against about 1.2 outside) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Density (air about 1.225 kg/m3 at sea level; helium 0.1785 g/L at STP) ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle ("Helium-filled balloons tug upward on their strings, demonstrating air's buoyant effect")

F20. Helium has mass and weight: a balloon filled with helium has more mass than the same balloon empty. It rises because it is less dense than the air around it, not because it is weightless.
    https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/floating-balloon (Page Keeley, "Floating Balloon", Uncovering Student Ideas in Science vol. 3: helium balloons "rise because they are less dense" than the surrounding air, not because they have less mass)

F21. Not every gas rises: carbon dioxide gas, about 1.977 kilograms per cubic metre, is denser than air and flows along the ground into low places.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Carbon_dioxide ("The density of gaseous CO2 at STP is 1.977 kg/m3"; "This greater density causes CO2 to flow along low layers and accumulate in basins or low-lying areas")

## A rock that floats

F22. Pumice forms when frothy, gas-rich lava cools and hardens fast in an explosive eruption, trapping its gas bubbles (vesicles); the pore space can make up as much as about 90 percent of the stone. Its density, typically 0.25 to 0.75 grams per cubic centimetre, lets many pieces float on water.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pumice ("formed by the rapid cooling and solidification of frothy, gas-rich lava during explosive volcanic eruptions"; "typically ranges from 0.25 to 0.75 g/cm3"; floats "owing to the trapped gases within the vesicles"; pores up to about 90% of the volume) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumice (vesicles are "void spaces left behind by bubbles that developed in the material while it was still molten")

F23. Undersea eruptions make pumice rafts that drift on the ocean for weeks to months, even years (1 to 3 years on average; Krakatoa's drifted through the Indian Ocean for months, by one account up to 20 years). A raft sinks when its pores fill with water, its density then rising above 1; sea life growing on it adds weight too.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pumice_raft (rafts "primarily form during submarine volcanic eruptions"; "persist for weeks to months, or even years"; "Sinking ultimately occurs when pores become fully saturated with water"; biofouling adds weight) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumice ("floats on water, possibly for years, until it eventually becomes waterlogged and sinks")

F24. The solid stuff of pumice is volcanic glass, denser than water: "Highly vitreous pumice is considered a volcanic glass", obsidian (solid volcanic glass with no bubbles) has a density of about 2.35 to 2.6 grams per cubic centimetre, and waterlogged pumice sinks.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumice ("Highly vitreous pumice is considered a volcanic glass") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Obsidian (density 2.35-2.60 g/cm3; the page also gives 2.4-2.8 elsewhere) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Pumice_raft (saturated pumice's bulk density rises above 1 g/cm3)

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. The size or the weight of an object decides whether it floats: "small objects float and large objects sink"; "light objects float and heavy objects sink". Also: "Objects that float have air inside them" and "Floating objects must sit wholly above the surface of the liquid".
    https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/3181-building-science-concepts-floating-and-sinking (Science Learning Hub, "Alternative conceptions")

M2. "More A, more B": some students reason that more material means a higher density or more sinking, so a bigger piece of the same stuff must be denser.
    https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/floating-logs (Keeley, citing Stavy and Tirosh 2000)

M3. Students link a liquid's thickness (viscosity) to its density: earlier studies of children and students found the naive idea that "the density or stickiness of a substance explains its viscous behavior".
    https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01741 (Feser and Krumphals, "Viscous behavior of fluids in the eyes of adults: A global survey", 2023) ; https://www.teachengineering.org/activities/view/cub_flow_activity2 (the two are often confused)

M4. Students believe "gases are weightless materials", and children aged 9 to 13 "tend to predict that gases have negative weight", the helium balloon being the gas they meet most.
    https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/floating-balloon (Keeley, "Floating Balloon")

M5. Author's judgment, from the facts above: students mix up a helium balloon with a hot-air balloon (helium is not warmer than the air; heated air is a different case, F19); think a hollow ship floats because ship steel is a lighter kind (F17); think a surface "skin" holds up a floating solid (surface tension can hold a carefully laid paper clip on water, but buoyancy holds up a bolt on mercury, F10, or a ship); and think denser ice would still float (F13).

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F25. Liquids that do not mix settle by density whatever order they are poured in, and a solid dropped in comes to rest by its own density: one that is denser than the upper liquid but less dense than the lower one sinks through the first and stops on top of the second. Scientific American's "Stacking Liquids" activity: the layers are the same whatever the pouring order; the objects "settled in the density column depending on their own densities", and the wax "should have rested on top of the water layer", below the oil. A real bead can do this with motor oil and water: motor oil's density "typically ranges from 860 to 940 kg/m3" (common grades about 880-900), and high-density polyethylene's is 0.941 to 0.967 g/cm3, so an HDPE bead sinks in motor oil and floats in water.
    https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/stacking-liquids/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Motor_oil ; https://grokipedia.com/page/High-density_polyethylene

F26. Why a pumice raft finally sinks: the gas in the stones' pores slowly escapes and water takes its place until the stone as a whole is denser than water. Grokipedia: "Sinking ultimately occurs when pores become fully saturated with water", "often after trapped gases diffuse outward over time, increasing bulk density beyond 1 g/cm3"; rafts last "1-3 years on average before sinking". Berkeley Lab (Fauria, Manga and Wei, 2017): pumice floats "because of pockets of gas in its pores", and the work "explains the eventual loss of these gases that causes the stones to sink". Kept out of the choices because they are partly true: wave abrasion wears and breaks the stones, and smaller pieces soak through and sink sooner (Grokipedia: "abrasion-induced size reduction can trigger sinking in 50-80% of fragments"); sea life growing on them adds weight; and daytime heat makes trapped gas swell so that some stones bob up for a while (Berkeley Lab). So no wrong choice says waves wear or break the stones, names sea life, or names heat.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Pumice_raft ; https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/news/solving-mystery-floating-rocks ; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/05/170523144110.htm

M6. For the pumice question's wrong choices: "Soft objects are more likely to float than hard objects" and "small objects float and large objects sink" are documented alternative conceptions (Science Learning Hub), so a stone that "hardens" or stones that "cling together into big clumps" are honest lures; neither changes a stone's average density (a clump of floating stones floats). The calm-sea lure is the author's judgment, the same belief as the swimmer's "its waves lift her up": buoyancy holds a floater up, not the waves.
    https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/3181-building-science-concepts-floating-and-sinking
