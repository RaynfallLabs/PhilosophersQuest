## Trophic levels

F1. A trophic level is the position a living thing holds in a food chain or food web. Producers (plants, algae, phytoplankton) are the first level; primary consumers, the herbivores that eat producers, are the second; secondary consumers, which eat herbivores, are the third; tertiary consumers, which eat secondary consumers, are the fourth. Top predators, also called apex predators, eat other consumers and "have no natural enemies except humans." A fifth level, an animal eating tertiary consumers, is called a quaternary consumer.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_level ("the position that an organism occupies within a food chain or food web"; primary consumers "typically herbivores"; "tertiary consumers or apex predators") ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ ("Primary consumers are herbivores"; "They are at the second trophic level"; "Secondary consumers eat herbivores. They are at the third trophic level"; "Tertiary consumers eat the secondary consumers. They are at the fourth trophic level"; apex predators "have no natural enemies except humans") ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/46-1-ecology-of-ecosystems ("The organisms that consume the primary producers are herbivores: the primary consumers"; "Tertiary consumers are carnivores that eat other carnivores"; "the apex consumers") ; https://biologydictionary.net/?p=8791 ("A quaternary consumer is simply a consumer which preys upon a tertiary consumer"; the "fifth trophic level")

F2. A desert example of a chain: a mouse eats seeds (primary consumer), "a secondary consumer may be a snake that eats a mouse," and "an owl or eagle may prey on a snake" (tertiary consumer). Wild mice live mainly on "seeds and grains"; they are prey for owls and snakes.
    https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mouse ("seeds and grains such as wheat, oats, and wild grasses"; "prey for numerous predators, including owls, snakes, foxes, and cats")

F3. Omnivores feed at more than one trophic level: omnivory is where "species feed across more than one trophic level," which blurs strict level assignments. People are omnivores. Someone eating plant food is feeding as a primary consumer; eating a herbivore (such as a rabbit, a strict herbivore) he is feeding as a secondary consumer.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_level ("species feed across more than one trophic level") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Omnivore (humans eat plant and animal matter) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Rabbit ("Rabbits are strict herbivores")

## The energy pyramid and why it shrinks

F4. An ecological pyramid shows the amount of living things, biomass or energy at each trophic level. A pyramid of energy shrinks at every level up and is always upright: "Pyramids of energy are invariably upright" (Grokipedia); "Pyramids of energy are always upright, and an ecosystem without sufficient primary productivity cannot be supported" (OpenStax). Every level's energy comes from the level below it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_pyramid ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/46-2-energy-flow-through-ecosystems

F5. A pyramid of numbers can stand upside down. Grokipedia's example: one oak tree "supports approximately 2,000 insects as primary consumers," which feed about 90 birds, which support one hawk. OpenStax: "Pyramids of numbers can be either upright or inverted, depending on the ecosystem." Charles Elton introduced the pyramid of numbers in his 1927 book Animal Ecology.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_pyramid ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/46-2-energy-flow-through-ecosystems

F6. Why so little energy passes up: most of the energy at each level is used by the living things there (cellular respiration, for moving, growing, keeping warm) and given off as heat; some food is never eaten, and some is not digested and leaves as waste, which goes to the decomposers. National Geographic: "between 80% and 90% of an organism's energy, or biomass, is lost as heat or waste." HHMI: some energy is "left behind in uneaten food or waste, which goes to decomposers," and a large share is "released as heat during cellular respiration."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_level ("losses from respiration, heat, and incomplete consumption") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Energy_flow_(ecology) (the remainder goes to "respiration, excretion, and undigested material," much of it as heat) ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ ; https://media.hhmi.org/biointeractive/click/energyflow/foodchain-03.html ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/46-2-energy-flow-through-ecosystems (metabolic heat lost; "Incomplete ingestion")

F7. Warm-blooded animals build very little of the energy they absorb into their bodies: net production efficiency "drops below 10% in endothermic vertebrates due to high metabolic demands" (Grokipedia); OpenStax gives about 1.6 percent for a squirrel against 18 percent for a caterpillar, and says ectotherms are about ten times as efficient as endotherms. So most of the energy a warm-blooded plant-eater such as a rabbit absorbs is spent on living and ends as heat.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Energy_flow_(ecology) ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/46-2-energy-flow-through-ecosystems

F8. Plants use much of their own energy too. At Silver Springs, Florida, the producers caught 20,810 kcal per square metre per year, and "13,187 of the 20,810 kcal/m2/yr were used for respiration or were lost as heat, leaving 7,633" for the primary consumers. (Context only.)
    https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/46-2-energy-flow-through-ecosystems

## The rule of thumb

F9. The ten percent rule: about a tenth of the energy at one level passes to the next. It is a rule of thumb, not a law: Grokipedia calls it "an approximation"; HHMI says "only about 10% of the energy in one trophic level is passed on to the next" and "This percentage may vary from 5%–20% depending on the types of organisms and ecosystem"; Grokipedia gives a typical range of 10 to 20 percent (aquatic studies 5 to 25). Raymond Lindeman's 1942 paper, The Trophic-Dynamic Aspect of Ecology, from which the rule is traced, cited efficiencies "ranging from 0.1% to 37.5%."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_level ("The 10% rule, an approximation originating from Raymond Lindeman's seminal trophic-dynamic analysis") ; https://media.hhmi.org/biointeractive/click/energyflow/foodchain-03.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_efficiency ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Lindeman (Cedar Bog Lake, Minnesota; died 1942 aged 26)

F10. By the rule, the shares multiply from step to step: a tenth reaches the second level and a tenth of that, about a hundredth, reaches the third. (Arithmetic on F9; National Geographic and Wikipedia's Ecological efficiency both work energy down a chain this way.)
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_efficiency ; https://media.hhmi.org/biointeractive/click/energyflow/foodchain-03.html

## Why top predators are few

F11. Because energy, and so biomass, shrinks at each level up, a healthy food web holds many producers, many herbivores and relatively few carnivores; the pyramid explains "why ecosystems support fewer top predators." An animal feeding a level nearer the producers has more energy available to it than one feeding a level higher.
    https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ ("Because biomass decreases with each trophic level, there are always more autotrophs than herbivores in a healthy food web"; "A healthy food web has an abundance of autotrophs, many herbivores, and relatively few carnivores and omnivores") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_level ("emphasizing why ecosystems support fewer top predators")

F12. On land, a pyramid of biomass (the total weight of living matter at each level) usually narrows upward, "as higher consumers require more prey"; it can be inverted in some waters, as in the English Channel, where "phytoplankton biomass is less than that of zooplankton" at a given moment. So in a grassland the top predators weigh least, all together, of any level.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_level ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_pyramid ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ ("biomass decreases with each trophic level")

## Why food chains are short

F13. Most food chains have only four or five levels: inefficient transfer "limits food chain lengths to typically four or five levels" (Grokipedia Energy flow); the 10% rule constrains chains "to about 4-5 levels in most ecosystems" (Grokipedia Trophic level); "after four to six energy transfers, there is not enough energy left to support another trophic level" (OpenStax). Caveat: chain length also varies with productivity and setting (Grokipedia Food chain: chains are longer in stable, productive habitats, 2-3 levels on small islands against 4-5 on mainlands), so energy is the textbook reason, not the whole story.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Energy_flow_(ecology) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_level ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/46-2-energy-flow-through-ecosystems ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_chain

## Matter cycles; energy flows through and out

F14. Energy flows one way through an ecosystem: it enters as sunlight and leaves as heat, and "energy cannot be recycled within the ecosystem and must be continually input from external sources." Matter cycles: nutrients and atoms pass from producers to consumers and decomposers and back to the soil, water and air for producers to use again ("conservation and reuse without net loss"). NGSS MS-LS2-3: "The atoms that make up the organisms in an ecosystem are cycled repeatedly between the living and nonliving parts of the ecosystem."
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Energy_flow_(ecology) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Nutrient_cycle ("energy flow is unidirectional"; "energy cannot be recycled within the system once converted to unusable forms") ; https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/ms-ls2-3-ecosystems-interactions-energy-and-dynamics

F15. Energy is never destroyed: "energy can neither be created nor destroyed but only transformed between different forms." The energy "lost" at each level is not gone; it has become heat, which living things cannot use to make food or to live on.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Conservation_of_energy ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_pyramid (losses "primarily as heat," per the second law of thermodynamics)

F16. A sealed glass ecosphere of water, algae, tiny shrimp and bacteria can live for years: it exchanges no matter with the outside but needs light, energy "entering as light and exiting as heat," while its matter recycles. (Context only.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Closed_ecological_system ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_ecological_system ("constant external energy input, such as sunlight")

## Decomposers in the energy flow

F17. Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down dead matter from every level and are "generally not assigned a fixed trophic level"; they are often drawn outside the main chain. They take in a great deal of energy: on land "less than 15% of terrestrial plant production is consumed by herbivores," so most plant growth dies and goes to the decomposers; at Silver Springs the decomposers took in 5,060 kcal per square metre per year, more than the primary consumers' 3,368. Fungi have no chlorophyll and cannot make their own food.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Trophic_level ("generally not assigned a fixed trophic level") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Energy_flow_(ecology) ("Decomposers break down organic matter at all levels") ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Food_chain ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_efficiency ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/46-2-energy-flow-through-ecosystems ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Fungus ("Unlike plants, fungi lack chlorophyll and cannot perform photosynthesis")

## Misconceptions (for honest wrong choices)

M1. Energy cycles like matter: "Decomposers release some energy that is cycled back to plants" is a listed middle-school misconception; plants get their energy only from sunlight.
    https://nsdl.library.cornell.edu/websites/wiki/index.php/MSP_MiddleSchoolPortal/Misconceptions_at_the_Middle.html

M2. Energy builds up the chain: students think "Carnivores have more energy or power than herbivores do," and that a top predator, eating many animals, collects the energy of the whole chain. In fact very little is left for the top.
    https://nsdl.library.cornell.edu/websites/wiki/index.php/MSP_MiddleSchoolPortal/Misconceptions_at_the_Middle.html ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/

M3. "Lost" energy is destroyed or used up and gone (against F15), and energy can be turned into matter as a body grows.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Conservation_of_energy

M4. Counting levels from the first consumer, or counting the producer as a consumer, so that the owl in a four-level chain is called the quaternary consumer; and treating every meat-eater as the same level.
    https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/food-web/ (levels counted from the producers)

M5. Judging a level by the size of one animal: tiny insects seem to weigh least, though there are so many that together they outweigh the few birds that eat them.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Ecological_pyramid (2,000 insects feed about 90 birds and one hawk)
