# Mixtures and Solutions: fact sheet (T1, 5th grade)

Researched 2026-11-25. Grokipedia first; curriculum documents, the Royal Society of Chemistry (RSC), NSIDC, USGS and others to corroborate.
Curriculum line G5.P3 (TEKS 5.6B, 5.6C; NGSS 5-PS1-2, 5-PS1-4).

## Facts

F1 A mixture is two or more substances mixed together that each keep their own chemical identity and properties; no chemical reaction joins them. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mixture ("two or more substances that retain their individual chemical identities and properties")

F2 A mixture can be separated back into its parts by physical methods: filtering, evaporating, sieving, magnets and others. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mixture ("can be separated back into their original components using physical methods such as filtration, distillation, or evaporation"; magnetism and sieving in its separation section); National Curriculum in England, Year 5 (ages 9-10): "decide how mixtures might be separated, including through filtering, sieving and evaporating", https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-science-programmes-of-study

F3 Iron filings mixed into sand stay magnetic: a magnet swept over the mixture pulls the iron filings out and leaves the sand behind. This is the 5th-grade standard's own example (TEKS 5.6B: "some mixtures maintain physical properties of their substances such as iron filings and sand"). https://grokipedia.com/page/Mixture ("a classic example removes iron filings from sand by sweeping a magnet over the mixture"); https://keslerscience.com/teks-5.6b-properties-of-mixtures ("Run a magnet over the pile and the iron leaps out, leaving the sand behind"; "The iron is still magnetic")

F4 Most sand is made of grains of the mineral quartz, and quartz is not pulled by a magnet (it is diamagnetic, unsuitable for magnetic separation). Some dark "black sand" is rich in magnetite, a magnetic mineral, so the question names pale sand. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sand ("Sand grains predominantly consist of quartz (SiO2)"; "black sand (magnetite-rich or mafic)"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnetic_separation ("quartz, feldspar, calcite, and most non-metallic silicates are diamagnetic")

F5 Magnets separate mixtures outside the classroom too: recycling plants pull steel and iron out of other waste, and mines separate iron minerals such as magnetite from waste rock. https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnetic_separation ("distinguishing ferrous metals like steel and iron from non-metallic debris"; "separates iron minerals like magnetite and hematite from gangue materials")

F6 A sieve sorts a mixture by the size of its pieces: pieces smaller than its holes fall through, and larger ones are held back. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sieve ("allows materials to be sorted by size"; "retaining larger particles on the mesh while permitting smaller ones to pass through the apertures")

F7 Geologists sort sediment with a stack of sieves, the largest openings at the top; the stack is shaken, and each sieve holds back the grains too big for its holes. The sorting is by grain size; each sieve's catch is then weighed to report the result. https://grokipedia.com/page/Sieve_analysis ("Agitation of the assembled stack follows to separate particles by size"; "the sieves act as barriers that retain larger particles while allowing smaller ones to pass through"; used in "geology for sediment analysis"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sieve ("stacking calibrated meshes to determine grain size distribution")

F8 Filtering separates solid bits from a liquid: the liquid passes through the filter (the filtrate), and solid particles too big for its pores are held back. https://grokipedia.com/page/Filtration ("isolating solids from a liquid or gas using a filter medium"; "permitting the fluid, known as the filtrate, to flow through")

F9 Sand and salt stirred into water and poured through filter paper: the sand stays in the filter paper, and the salt passes through, dissolved in the water (the filtrate is a salt solution). Heating the filtrate drives off the water and gives the salt back. https://edu.rsc.org/experiments/separating-sand-and-salt-by-filtering-and-evaporation/386.article (RSC class practical, listed for ages 9-11 among others: "the damp sand in the filter paper"; "Pour the filtrate into an evaporating basin. Heat the salt solution gently"); FOSS Mixtures and Solutions (a grade-5 module), Investigation 1, as described on its publisher's page: students "separate the mixtures with screens and filters" and find that salt water is a solution "which cannot be separated with a filter, but only through evaporation", https://fossnextgeneration.com/?p=29537 (these FOSS words come from the publisher page's search excerpt; the page itself redirected and could not be fetched, so the RSC practical is the primary source here)

F10 A solution is a mixture that is the same all the way through: one substance is spread evenly through another in bits far too small to see. Salt water is a solution. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mixture (homogeneous mixtures "exhibit a uniform composition and appearance throughout", "components dispersed at the molecular or ionic level, making them indistinguishable by the naked eye"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Solution_(chemistry) ("completely dispersed and uniformly distributed throughout the mixture"; salt water: "sodium chloride (NaCl) ... acts as the solute and water serves as the solvent")

F11 The dissolved bits in a solution do not settle out, however long it stands, and they pass through filters. In a suspension such as sand or mud stirred in water, the bits are big enough to settle to the bottom. https://grokipedia.com/page/Suspension_(chemistry) (solutions are "homogeneous mixtures" whose solute particles "do not settle and pass through filters"; suspensions "settle out of the mixture upon standing due to gravity"; "sand stirred in water, where the solid grains settle to the bottom"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Mixture ("In suspensions, these particles are sufficiently large to settle out over time due to gravity")

F12 In a solution, the substance that dissolves is the solute and the liquid it dissolves in is the solvent: in salt water, salt is the solute and water the solvent. https://grokipedia.com/page/Solution_(chemistry) (quoted in F10)

F13 Copper sulfate (copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate) forms bright blue crystals and dissolves well in water, giving a blue solution. It is poisonous if swallowed, so it is a lab chemical, not a food. https://grokipedia.com/page/Copper(II)_sulfate ("bright blue triclinic crystals or granules"; "a blue solution of copper(II) sulfate"; solubility about 32 g per 100 mL; "toxic if ingested")

F14 Water can dissolve only so much of a solid. A saturated solution holds the most solute it can at that temperature; any more of the solid added stays undissolved. FOSS's grade-5 module has students add salt to water until no more will dissolve. https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility ("a saturated solution where the solution is in dynamic equilibrium with any undissolved solute"; "For most solid solutes in liquid solvents, solubility increases with rising temperature"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Solution_(chemistry) ("A saturated solution holds exactly the maximum amount of solute dictated by its solubility at that temperature"); FOSS Mixtures and Solutions, Investigation 4 (students make a saturated solution by adding salt until no more will dissolve), https://fossnextgeneration.com/?p=29537

F15 About 36 grams of table salt dissolve in 100 grams of water at room temperature (25 C). Dissolving salt in water is slightly endothermic: it takes in a little heat rather than giving heat off. https://grokipedia.com/page/Solubility (36 g per 100 g of water at 25 C); https://grokipedia.com/page/Sodium_chloride (enthalpy of solution "slightly positive at approximately +3.9 kJ/mol", "making the process endothermic")

F16 A solution's properties differ from those of its parts. Dissolving a substance in water lowers the temperature at which the water freezes: pure water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius, seawater only at about -1.8 degrees Celsius. https://grokipedia.com/page/Freezing-point_depression (a solute "lowers the freezing point of the solvent compared to its pure state"; seawater "freezes at around -1.8 C due to dissolved salts"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Seawater ("Seawater is the saline aqueous solution that fills Earth's oceans and seas"; freezing point "around -1.8 C"); https://nsidc.org/learn/parts-cryosphere/sea-ice/science-sea-ice ("the freezing temperature of salt water is lower than freshwater"; about -1.8 C against 0 C)

F17 Road crews spread salt on icy roads because the salt dissolves into the water on the ice, and salt water freezes at a lower temperature than pure water, so the ice turns to slush. Rock salt stops working well in hard cold, below about -9 degrees Celsius. Salt does not melt ice by giving off heat (F15) or by changing it into a new substance: the slush is salt water. https://grokipedia.com/page/Freezing-point_depression (salts de-ice roads "by lowering the freezing point of water below 0 C"; effectiveness "diminishes below -9 C"); https://www.usgs.gov/centers/new-england-water-science-center/news/deicing-new-englands-roads-parking-areas-and-walkways (deicers lower water's freezing point by dissolving into the water); https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/salt-doesnt-melt-ice-heres-how-it-makes-winter-streets-safer/ ("The salt impedes the ability of the water molecules to form solid ice crystals"; "the salt must be in a solution with liquid water")

F18 The total mass of matter stays the same through heating, cooling, mixing and dissolving, even when a substance seems to vanish (the 5th-grade standard). In a closed container, mass stays the same through any physical or chemical change. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ps1-2-matter-and-its-interactions (5-PS1-2: "regardless of the type of change that occurs when heating, cooling, or mixing substances, the total weight of matter is conserved"; clarification: "phase changes, dissolving, and mixing that form new substances"; PS1.A: "The amount (weight) of matter is conserved when it changes form, even in transitions in which it seems to vanish"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Conservation_of_mass (in a closed system total mass "remains constant before and after any physical or chemical change")

F19 When a solid dissolves, the solution weighs exactly as much as the water and the solid did before: 150 g of water and 10 g of salt make 160 g of salt water. https://edu.rsc.org/resources/mass-and-dissolving/1084.article (worked answers: 150 g water + 10 g salt = 160 g; 200 g water + 10 g sugar = 210 g); NSTA grade-5 probe "Sugar Water", https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/sugar-water (the mass of the sugar remains though it cannot be seen)

F20 When water freezes, its mass stays the same but its volume grows by about 9 percent, which is why ice is less dense than water and floats. https://grokipedia.com/page/Ice ("Upon freezing, water expands by about 9% in volume"; ice about 0.917 g/cm3 against 1 g/cm3 for water; ice floats); https://grokipedia.com/page/Conservation_of_mass ("the mass of solid ice precisely equals the mass of resulting liquid water"); Inquiry Project grade 5 (TERC), https://inquiryproject.terc.edu/curriculum/teacher/child_and_scientist/carol5_3.cfm.html (water's weight "remains the same" when it freezes, even though its volume increases)

F21 When two or more substances are mixed, sometimes a new substance with different properties forms (the first look at a chemical change). Such changes are not usually reversible; rusting and burning are the standard examples. https://www.nextgenscience.org/pe/5-ps1-4-matter-and-its-interactions (PS1.B: "When two or more different substances are mixed, a new substance with different properties may be formed"); National Curriculum in England, Year 5: "some changes result in the formation of new materials, and that this kind of change is not usually reversible"; guidance: "burning, rusting and other reactions" (URL in F2)

F22 Rust is a new substance, not iron with a new colour: iron reacts with oxygen and water to make iron oxide, a reddish-brown, flaky, crumbly coat that is softer and weaker than the metal. https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction (reactants "transformed into one or more different substances"; "the rusting of iron, in which iron atoms react with oxygen and water molecules to yield iron(III) oxide (rust)"); https://grokipedia.com/page/Rust ("reddish-brown oxide coating, primarily hydrated iron(III) oxide"; "flaky, powdery, or scaly"; "softer and more friable than the base metal")

F23 Dissolving, mixing and melting make no new substance: they are physical changes, and they can be reversed. https://grokipedia.com/page/Chemical_reaction (melting ice and "dissolving sugar in water is a physical change"); National Curriculum in England, Year 5: "demonstrate that dissolving, mixing and changes of state are reversible changes" (URL in F2)

## Misconceptions (students of this grade)

M1 A dissolved solid has vanished, or weighs nothing, so the solution weighs less than its parts. RSC: "Younger learners may be satisfied with the idea that solutes 'disappear' when they dissolve" and "may not expect the mass of the solute to register" (https://edu.rsc.org/resources/mass-and-dissolving/1084.article); NSTA's grade-5 Sugar Water probe targets the idea that the solute "disappears" or "melts away" (https://www.nsta.org/lesson-plan/sugar-water).

M2 Freezing changes the weight of water. Grade-5 children "think that when something freezes or melts, its weight should change"; many expect ice to be heavier because "harder things are heavier" (TERC Inquiry Project, URL in F20). Others reason the other way, that water is "heavier than ice" (NZCER ARBS "Freezing water", https://arbs.nzcer.org.nz/resources/freezing-water); ice floating feeds the "lighter" version.

M3 Mixing changes the substances, so a mixed-in substance loses its own properties. This is exactly what TEKS 5.6B is written to correct (F3); the lure that mixing "changed the iron" is that belief.

M4 Salt melts ice by heat. Scientific American's headline answers it: "Salt Doesn't Melt Ice" (URL in F17); dissolving salt in fact takes in a little heat (F15).

M5 Rust is just iron that has changed colour, or dirt stuck to the iron. Author's judgment (no survey found); the standards name rusting as the change that makes a new material (F21, F22).

M6 A sieve sorts by weight or by what things are made of, and a filter can catch anything mixed into water, dissolved salt included. Author's judgment for the sieve; FOSS's grade-5 investigation is built round the filter surprise (F9).

## Notes and cautions

- Iron filings and sand: name the sand pale or white (quartz). Black sand can hold magnetite, which a magnet does pull (F4).
- Rust is hydrated iron oxide, so do not say "rust contains no water"; say it is not a mixture of iron and water from which the iron could be got back by drying.
- Salt's solubility hardly changes with temperature (about 36 g at 25 C), so do not teach "hot water dissolves much more salt" with salt as the example.
- Filter paper: very fine silt can slip through school filter papers (RSC teaching notes), so ask about sand, not clay or silt.
- Road salt: the ice melts because salt water's freezing point is below the air temperature; do not say salt "heats" the ice. Keep the air temperature in the question above about -9 C.
- Copper sulfate is a lab chemical (poisonous to swallow); keep it in a chemist's hands, not a kitchen.
- Neighbouring units already ask that salt dissolves in water while sand does not, that dissolved salt comes back when seawater dries, and that a magnet pulls iron but not other metals. This unit does not ask those facts again.

## Added in the revision (2026-11-25)

F24 A solution is the same in every part: any sample taken from it has the same make-up as any other, so a drop from the top of a flask of a solution matches a drop from the bottom. https://grokipedia.com/page/Mixture ("any sample taken from the mixture has the same properties and proportions of components"; homogeneous mixtures are "characterized by a uniform composition throughout its volume")

F25 The more salt water holds, the lower the temperature at which it freezes: salt water of about 3 percent salt (seawater strength) freezes at about -1.8 C, of 8 percent at about -5.1 C, and the saltiest (about 23 percent) at about -21 C. Rock salt, the road crews' de-icer, is halite, the natural mineral form of sodium chloride. https://grokipedia.com/page/Freezing-point_depression ("the extent of the depression is directly proportional to the concentration of the solute"; seawater "freezes at around -1.8 C"; saturation "at approximately 23% by mass, where the solution freezes at -20.5 C"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saline_water (sodium chloride table: 3 wt% -1.79 C; 8 wt% -5.08 C; eutectic 23.3 wt% -21.1 C); https://grokipedia.com/page/Halite ("Halite, commonly known as rock salt, is the naturally occurring mineral form of sodium chloride"; "halite in the form of rock salt is widely applied for de-icing roads and highways")

M7 Undissolved salt at the bottom of a saturated solution is blamed on a caked lump, on salt water grown too thick, or on a different kind of salt. Author's judgment (the judge's suggested lures; no survey found). The real limit is how much salt the water can hold (F14); more water takes in more salt.
