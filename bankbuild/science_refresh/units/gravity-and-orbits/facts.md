## Every mass pulls every other

F1. Newton's law of universal gravitation: "every particle of matter in the universe attracts every other particle"; the attraction grows with the two masses (their product) and weakens with the square of the distance between their centres. Newton published it in the Principia (Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica), first printed in Latin in London on 5 July 1687; Edmond Halley paid for the printing.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Philosophiæ_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica

F2. Gravity "possesses an infinite range": its pull weakens with distance but never cuts off at any edge. It is "the weakest of the fundamental forces by many orders of magnitude", and it rules at the scale of planets and stars because it adds up over vast amounts of matter. So the pull between everyday objects is far too weak to notice.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gravity ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation

F3. Henry Cavendish measured the gravitational pull between lead balls in 1797-1798 with a torsion balance designed by John Michell: two small balls of about 0.73 kg hung near two large ones of about 158 kg. The pull between a small and a large ball was only about 1.5 x 10^-7 newtons. The balance sat in a closed wooden case against air currents and changes of temperature, and Cavendish read it through a telescope from outside. (The T5 Kepler unit owns the experiment; here it is only a mention in a context.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Cavendish_experiment ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation

F4. Gravity cannot be blocked or screened as electric forces can (a Faraday cage stops those): gravitational shielding is "considered impossible" in mainstream physics, and "no reproducible evidence for gravitational shielding has emerged" from the experiments that claimed it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gravitational_shielding

## Newton, the apple and the Moon

F5. The apple story: Isaac Newton told William Stukeley at dinner on 15 April 1726, the year before he died, that the notion of gravity came to him as he watched an apple fall in the garden at Woolsthorpe Manor, his family home in Lincolnshire, during the plague years of 1665-1666, when Cambridge was closed. He asked why the apple fell "straight downward toward the Earth", and then whether the same pull reaches the Moon and governs its orbit. Stukeley's memoir appeared in 1752; John Conduitt also recorded the story. Newton left no written account of it, and the apple hitting his head is a later embellishment, absent from the early versions.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Isaac_Newton's_apple_tree ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gravity

F6. In the Principia Newton argued that the Moon is held in its orbit by a pull toward Earth ("nor could the moon without some such force, be retained in its orbit"), and in Book III he compared how far the Moon falls toward Earth with how far a heavy body falls near the ground, concluding that the force holding the Moon is the same gravity that makes bodies fall on Earth.
    https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Principles_of_Natural_Philosophy_(1846)/Definitions ; https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Principles_of_Natural_Philosophy_(1846)/BookIII-Prop4 (the page served holds Props. 36-38; Prop. 37, Cor. 7 sets the Moon's fall beside a heavy body's) ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Philosophiæ_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica

F7. Older, respectable wrong ideas. Aristotle held that the heavenly bodies, made of a fifth element (aether), move "eternally in perfect circles", unlike earthly bodies with their downward tendency, so the heavens had laws of their own. Rene Descartes, in his Principia Philosophiae (1644), had the Sun "enveloped by a primary vortex of ether-like matter, carrying the planets" round it, a whirlpool of unseen matter; Newton's Principia showed the vortices did not fit the observations, and they were given up.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Gravity ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mechanical_explanations_of_gravitation ; https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/rene-descartes/

## An orbit is a fall that never ends

F8. Newton's cannonball, in the Principia (1687), Definition V: a lead ball fired by gunpowder from the top of a mountain, level with the horizon, curves down to the ground; with the air's resistance taken away, a faster ball flies farther, until with enough speed it "might go quite round the whole earth before it falls", or at last never falls at all and goes on into space.
    https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Principles_of_Natural_Philosophy_(1846)/Definitions

F9. The famous diagram is in Newton's A Treatise of the System of the World (De mundi systemate), written about 1685 and published in 1728, after his death: a cannon on a mountain so high that it rises above the air fires level; slow balls fall nearby (path VD), and at one speed the ball falls all the way round Earth and comes back "to the mountain from which it was projected" (path VF). Newton's text: arcs "of 1, 2, 5, 10, 100, 1000 miles before it arrived at the Earth".
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_cannonball ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_cannonball

F10. An orbit is a continual fall: the ball (or a station) falls toward Earth all the time, but it moves forward so fast that Earth's round surface curves away beneath it as fast as it falls. NASA: the space station "moves at a speed that matches the way Earth's surface curves." Just above Earth's surface (radius about 6,371 km) the speed for a circular orbit is about 7.9 km/s.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_cannonball ; https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-5-8/

F11. Orbital decay: a satellite in a low orbit is brushed by thin traces of air, which slow it so that it spirals inward and at last re-enters the atmosphere. The International Space Station loses on average about 2 km of height a month and is pushed back up by reboosts, engine burns of docked spacecraft such as the Russian Progress; decay is faster when high solar activity thickens the upper air.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Orbital_decay ; https://grokipedia.com/page/International_Space_Station

F12. Skylab, "the first space station launched and operated by the United States", was launched on 14 May 1973; three crews lived aboard, and the last left on 8 February 1974. Atmospheric drag, sped up by heightened solar activity, lowered its orbit. NASA had hoped to use the Space Shuttle to boost it, but the Shuttle was not ready in time. Skylab re-entered on 11 July 1979, and its debris fell across the Indian Ocean and Western Australia.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Skylab

## Why astronauts float

F13. The International Space Station orbits about 400 km up (average about 408 km; roughly 370-460 km) at about 28,000 km/h (NASA: 17,500 miles per hour), going round Earth once in about 90-93 minutes, some 15.5 times a day.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/International_Space_Station ; https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-5-8/

F14. At the station's height, NASA says, "Earth's gravity is about 90 percent of what it is on the planet's surface." The station is only about 400 km farther out than the ground, against Earth's radius of about 6,371 km, so the pull is only a little weaker (an NSTA calculation gives a weakening "of only 12%").
    https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-5-8/ ; https://www.nsta.org/science-teacher/science-teacher-mayjune-2023/whats-gravity ; https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html

F15. Astronauts float because they are falling: "The spacecraft, its crew and any objects aboard are all falling toward but around Earth. ... Since they are all falling together, the crew and objects appear to float when compared with the spacecraft." NASA: microgravity "is sometimes called 'zero gravity,' but this is misleading." NSTA: "The reason astronauts float is not due to a weakening in the force of gravity"; the crew and station are in "a constant state of free fall".
    https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-5-8/ ; https://www.nsta.org/science-teacher/science-teacher-mayjune-2023/whats-gravity

## Mass, weight and the Moon

F16. The Moon has 0.0123 of Earth's mass (Earth has about 81 times the Moon's mass) and 0.27 of its radius; its surface gravity is 1.62 m/s², 0.165 of Earth's (about one-sixth). It has essentially no atmosphere. It turns once on its axis in about 27.3 days (655.7 hours), against Earth's 23.9 hours. Its mean distance from Earth is about 384,400 km.
    https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html ; https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass_versus_weight

F17. The Moon "currently lacks a global dipole magnetic field"; only weak, local patches of magnetised crust remain from an ancient core dynamo that has stopped. (Used only as a true-but-irrelevant premise: magnetism does not make gravity.)
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Magnetic_field_of_the_Moon

F18. Mass is "the amount of matter in an object" and "remains constant regardless of location"; weight is "the force exerted on an object by gravity", so it changes with place, about one-sixth on the Moon. A balance measures mass by comparing an object with known masses, so it gives the same reading on the Moon; a spring scale measures the pull of gravity, so it reads less there. Mass is measured in kilograms, weight (a force) in newtons; everyday speech mixes the two words up.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass_versus_weight ; https://keslerscience.com/gravity-motion-space-activity-teks-7.9b

F19. Where the pulls balance (derived from F1 and F16, no new measurement): on the line from Earth to the Moon, the spacecraft feels Earth's pull and the Moon's pull equally where distance from Earth = d / (1 + square root of (Moon's mass / Earth's mass)) = 384,400 / 1.111, about 346,000 km from Earth's centre and about 38,000 km from the Moon's, roughly nine-tenths of the way. Halfway, Earth's pull is still about 81 times the Moon's (equal distances, so the pulls are in the ratio of the masses). A textbook problem (Giancoli) gives 3.46 x 10^8 m from Earth. CAUTION: popular Apollo accounts quote other figures (such as 43,495 miles from the Moon), which are a navigators' switch-over point, not the equal-pull point; no Apollo figure is used.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation ; https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html ; https://d21r7v8xqgaihj.cloudfront.net/giancoli-physics-7th-edition-solutions/chapter-5/problem-30 (search excerpt)

F20. The Sun "accounts for 99.86% of the system's total mass", which is why its gravity holds the whole solar system in order (context only; T1 owns "the Sun's gravity holds the planets").
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_System

## The grade

F21. Seventh-grade standards teach this unit. Texas TEKS 7.9(B), a grade-7 readiness standard: "describe how gravity governs motion within Earth's solar system" (TEA moved this concept from grade 6 to grade 7). A TEKS 7.9B classroom unit teaches that "mass is the amount of matter" while weight is gravity's force on it, weight on other worlds, that "more mass means stronger gravity", orbits as "the tug-of-war between gravity and inertia", and the station's "continuous freefall". NGSS MS-ESS1-2 (the role of gravity in the motions of the solar system) and MS-PS2-4 (gravitational interactions are attractive and depend on the masses) are the middle-school lines.

## Misconceptions (honest wrong choices)

M1. That there is no gravity in space, so astronauts float because gravity is gone. NASA: "Some people think that there is no gravity in space." NSTA: some students "claim that they experience 0% gravity since they are in space." (A search excerpt of Montana State thesis work cites a study in which 81% of 13-year-olds and 75% of 14-year-olds did not think there is gravity in space; not read in full, not used.)
    https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-5-8/ ; https://www.nsta.org/science-teacher/science-teacher-mayjune-2023/whats-gravity

M2. That astronauts float because gravity is very weak that far from Earth (students predict a weakening near 100%; the true figure is about 10%).
    https://www.nsta.org/science-teacher/science-teacher-mayjune-2023/whats-gravity

M3. That gravity needs air, so there is no gravity on the Moon or in space; and that gravity exists only on Earth or ends at its atmosphere (the "boundary" model). The Science Learning Hub lists "gravity exists due to the presence of air or the Earth's atmosphere" and "Gravity only exists on the Earth or its surface"; Montana State work reports students saying Earth's gravity stops at the top of the atmosphere.
    https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/294-alternative-conceptions-about-gravity ; https://scholarworks.montana.edu/handle/1/10047 (search excerpt)

M4. That gravity is the same thing as, or comes from, magnetism, a planet's spin or air pressure: Williamson and Willoughby (2012, Astronomy Education Review) found college students often confound gravity with magnetism, rotation and atmospheric pressure.
    https://istardb.org/aerpublications/student-understanding-of-gravity-in-introductory-college-astronomy/ ; https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/294-alternative-conceptions-about-gravity

M5. That only big bodies (planets, stars) have gravity, and that a mass in between (Earth, a station's walls) blocks gravity (F1, F4).
    https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/resources/294-alternative-conceptions-about-gravity ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gravitational_shielding

M6. That a fast-moving object escapes gravity's pull, or that an orbiting thing has stopped falling (F10, F15).
    https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-5-8/

M7. That mass and weight are the same thing, so both change on the Moon, or neither does (F18).
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Mass_versus_weight

## Added in the revision (2026-11-26)

F22. Newton's own picture of the cannon: in A Treatise of the System of the World a stone is projected "from the top of a high mountain", and Newton asks us to suppose "that there is no air about the earth, or at least that it is endowed with little or no power of resisting" (as quoted by Wikipedia). The mountain whose top rises above the atmosphere (Grokipedia's wording, and many popular accounts) is a later paraphrase, not Newton's. On the apple: Stukeley's memoir names no place for it (the family-home garden is John Conduitt's account), per the fact-check; no question now uses the apple.
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_cannonball ; https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/OTHE00001

F23. The Sun's mass is 1,988,400 x 10^24 kg, about 332,900 times Earth's; Jupiter's is 317.8 times Earth's, so the Sun has about 1,048 times Jupiter's mass ("more than a thousand times"). Grokipedia: "The Sun accounts for 99.86% of the system's total mass", and of the planets' small share Jupiter contributes "over two-thirds".
    https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/sunfact.html ; https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/planet_table_ratio.html ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_System

F24. NASA: "dozens of moons swarm around" Jupiter (counts change as more are found; Grokipedia's Solar System page gives 115 known). A planet's gravity holds its moons, as the Sun's holds the planets.
    https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/moons/ ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Solar_System

F25. Two masses pull on each other with forces "equal in magnitude but opposite in direction" (Newton's third law), so a light ball pulls a heavy one exactly as hard as the heavy one pulls it. The pulls of several bodies add: the total field at a point is "the vector sum of the fields from each source mass" (superposition), so a stronger pull from the Sun adds to Earth's pull and does not block it.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Gravitational_shielding

F26. The theory of impetus (Philoponus in the 6th century, Jean Buridan in the 14th) held that a thrower or launcher gives a projectile an impressed force, an impetus, that carries it on until resistance and gravity use it up; Grokipedia calls Buridan's impetus a "proto-concept of inertia". Newton's first law replaced it: "Every body perseveres in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a right line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed thereon"; uniform motion "persists when the net external force on the object is zero", so no force from the blast is needed to keep a ball moving.
    https://grokipedia.com/page/Theory_of_impetus ; https://grokipedia.com/page/Newton's_laws_of_motion
