**T1.** Joan of Arc rode at the very front of her army's charges, where the fighting was thickest — and yet she swore at her trial that she had never killed a single person in battle. - A trumpet to signal the charge
- A shield blessed by her saints
*src: Trial of Condemnation (1431); Jeanne-darc.info, 'Banner.' Her words: 'I loved my banner forty times better than my sword. **T2.** Joan of Arc's most famous sword was not given to her by the king and not bought in any town. She claimed her heavenly voices told her exactly where an old blade lay hidden, marked with five crosses, and she sent an armorer from Tours to dig for it. Where did she say it would be found?

- Sunk in the river where a knight had drowned
- Walled up in the ruins of an old abbey
*src: Trial of Condemnation, Fourth Session (1431); Jeanne-darc.info, 'Sword.' Behind the altar of the church of Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois. It came up heavily rusted with five crosses on it, and the rust reportedly fell away easily when cleaned. She never claimed the sword itself was magic.*

**T2.** Among the 'proofs of witchcraft' the judges hunted at Joan of Arc's trial was a simple ring her parents had given her, which she looked at for comfort before battle 'in honor of my father and mother.' - 'Jhesus Maria'  **✓**

*src: Trial of Condemnation, March 17, 1431, via famous-trials.com. The ring bore 'Jhesus Maria' with three crosses. Joan said she had it on her finger when she touched Saint Catherine, and the court treated even this keepsake as an instrument of sorcery.*

**T2.** On 30 May 1431, Joan of Arc was burned at the stake in the marketplace of Rouen. In the moments before the fire was lit, one of the English soldiers guarding her was so moved by pity that he quickly made something with his own hands and pushed it to her. **T3.** The day before the assault on the fortress of the Augustinians at Orléans in May 1429, Joan of Arc warned the captains of something that would happen to her in the fighting the next day — naming where on her own body it would strike. **T3.** A prisoner in the high tower of Beaurevoir, Joan of Arc heard a rumor that the people of the besieged town of Compiègne — down to children of seven — were to be put to fire and sword, and that she herself was about to be handed to the English. **T3.** When the teenage Joan of Arc was begging the captain Robert de Baudricourt at Vaucouleurs to send her to the French prince, he did not believe her — until she told him something on 17 February 1429 that no rider could yet have brought him: that a great French defeat had just occurred outside Orléans that very day. She named it before any messenger could have carried the news; when official word confirmed it days later, Baudricourt relented and sent her to the Dauphin.*

**T4.** Before she was ever given an army, Joan of Arc had to face a panel of learned churchmen at Poitiers who tested whether her mission was genuine. One theologian pushed her with a trap dressed as piety: if God truly meant to save France, then soldiers were pointless — God could do it alone. Joan's reply became one of her most famous lines. What did she say?
- 'The men-at-arms will fight, and God will give the victory'  **✓**
- 'God helps only those who first help themselves'
- 'Then let God strike me down if I am false'
- 'A sword in a coward's hand still does God's work'
*src: Testimony of Jean Seguin, professor of theology on the Poitiers panel, given at the Nullification Trial of 1456. Her full reply: 'In God's name, the men-at-arms will fight and God will give the victory.' The exchange survives only because Seguin recalled it under oath decades later.*

**T4.** Joan of Arc owned more than one sword. Her celebrated blade from the church at Fierbois was treated as a holy symbol, but she had a second, ordinary war-sword she had taken from a captured enemy commander, Franquet d'Arras, at Lagny. Asked about it at trial, she gave a blunt, soldierly verdict on why she valued that captured weapon. What did she say it was good for?
- Laying on stout thumps and blows  **✓**
- Cutting through any armor in France
- Reminding her enemies of their defeat
- Being light enough for a girl to swing all day
*src: Trial of Condemnation, Feb 27, 1431; Jeanne-darc.info, 'Sword.' She prized the captured Franquet d'Arras blade as 'good to lay on stout thumps and blows with' — a plain fighting weapon, in pointed contrast to the symbolic Fierbois sword she carried for its meaning.*

**T5.** Joan of Arc's judges built her a perfect theological trap. They asked whether she knew herself to be in God's grace. By Church doctrine, claiming certainty of one's own salvation was the sin of presumption — yet denying it would mean confessing her heavenly voices were lies. A notary recorded that her reply left the examiners stunned. What did the illiterate teenager answer?
- 'If I am not, may God put me there; and if I am, may God so keep me'  **✓**
- 'Only my confessor may ask me that, and you are not he'
- 'I am as much in God's grace as any judge in this room'
- 'No living soul may know that — not even the Pope'
*src: Trial of Condemnation (1431); EWTN, '15 quotes from her trial.' Her fuller answer added, 'I should be the saddest in all the world if I knew that I were not in the grace of God.' A notary testified the examiners were stupefied that a peasant girl had slipped a trap built by university theologians.*

**T5.** Most of Joan of Arc's surviving letters were dictated by her, since she could not read or write. But one — a 1430 letter threatening a crusade against the Hussite heretics of Bohemia — is the item scholars most doubt she truly authored. Three clues point away from her: it is in Latin, which she never used; it does not sound like her other letters; and the signature on it is not hers. Whose name does it bear instead?
- Her chaplain, Jean Pasquerel  **✓**
- Her squire, Jean d'Aulon
- Her judge, Bishop Cauchon
- The Duke of Bedford
*src: archive.joan-of-arc.org, 'Letter to the Hussites' (23 March 1430); jeanne-darc.info, 'Letters.' Because it is Latin, unlike her voice, and signed by her chaplain Pasquerel, scholars think he composed it with at most Joan's cursory approval — a reminder to weigh each source rather than accept every document attributed to a famous figure.*
