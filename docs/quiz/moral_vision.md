# Moral Vision — the rubric every question must pass

This is the soul of the bank. The pipeline's `moral_fit` validator scores each candidate against the rules below. The `wonder_fun` validator scores against the **What we celebrate** section. The `viral_check` agent asks: *would the author be embarrassed by intellectual flimsiness, partisan flame-bait, condescension, or strawmen?*

The bank reflects a substantive worldview. It is not trying to be politically neutral — it is trying to be intellectually honest. A serious argument for any of the positions below should read as fascinating, not polemical. If a question fails because it sounds like advocacy rather than discovery, the question is wrong — not the position.

If a rule conflicts with another in a particular case, **the player's experience of wonder wins**. We are not writing a tract. We are writing a knowledge engine that makes the world feel larger and clearer.

## 1. The tradition this bank stands in

The bank is not neutral. It stands in a specific intellectual tradition — *classical-liberal traditionalism with empirical-realist commitments*. The lineage:

> **Locke → the American Founders → Tocqueville → the Anglo-American natural-rights line of Frederick Douglass (Rochester, 1852), Abraham Lincoln (Cooper Union, 1860), and Martin Luther King (Birmingham, 1963) → the Austrian school (Menger, Mises, Hayek, Friedman, Sowell, Rothbard) → the moral witness of Solzhenitsyn, Havel, and Sakharov → the contemporary heterodox thinkers who hold these commitments together (Pinker, Haidt, McWhorter, Loury, Hughes, Steele, Scruton, Pluckrose, Lindsay).**

The seven meta-principles that animate the tradition. These are the master rules; sections 2–8 are how they are applied.

1. **Reality is not negotiable.** Sex is biologically binary in humans because gametes are. The economic calculation problem is true because price information is dispersed. Twentieth-century communism's death toll is what it is because the bodies were counted. Biological variance across populations exists because the data shows it. Ideology does not get to override what is.

2. **Anti-totalitarianism is foundational, in every form.** The 20th century settled the empirical question: central planning, racial nationalism, and ideological state-imposition produce mass death. The 21st century requires defending that answer against new forms of the same impulse — soft despotism through institutional capture, the demand for compelled assent to claims known to be false, identity-essentialism that treats the individual as merely a representative of a group.

3. **The Western tradition is a real human achievement.** Athens → Jerusalem → Rome → Canterbury → Edinburgh → Philadelphia is the intellectual genealogy that produced the universities, the rule of law, modern science, religious liberty, the abolition of slavery, and the natural-rights tradition. American exceptionalism is its most successful experiment. Worth celebrating, worth defending, worth transmitting. Honest about failures; not *defined* by them.

4. **Virtues are real and formable.** Honesty, courage, integrity, logical clarity, pragmatic analysis. These are transmissible character traits, not relativized preferences. The bank teaches them by featuring people who demonstrated them at cost — Solzhenitsyn smuggling chapters out of the Soviet Union, Douglass speaking truth at Rochester, the Wright brothers risking everything on a calculation, Mary Anning earning a man's recognition from a girl's beach.

5. **Children deserve moral formation, not abandonment to ambient culture.** This is the animating motivation. The bank is parenting through curation — the deliberate transmission of an intellectual inheritance to children who will otherwise inherit whatever the culture happens to be transmitting that year.

6. **Intellectual seriousness over slogans.** Hayek's actual argument, Solzhenitsyn's actual witness, Sowell's actual data — never flame-bait, never "did you know X is bad?" framings. Real distractors, even of positions the tradition rejects. Steel-manning is non-negotiable. The strongest version of a view we disagree with is the version the bank presents.

7. **Dual commitments held together are the mark of seriousness.** Celebrating the West AND honest about its failures. Anti-racism in all directions AND acknowledging biological variance. Free markets AND virtue ethics. Christian heritage as foundational AND religious liberty for all traditions. Marxism-as-philosophy taken seriously AND Marxism-as-implementation's body count cited. These are the harder paths, and the bank takes them.

## 2. What we celebrate

Lead with these. A question that does *one* of these well is a question worth keeping.

- **Wonder.** The universe is stranger than common sense allows. Light bends around stars. Octopuses see with their skin. A 25-year-old destroyed three volumes of mathematical philosophy with one paper. Lead with the strange thing.
- **Curiosity over completion.** A question that leaves the player wanting to look something up is doing its job. A question that "wraps up the topic" is not.
- **Ordinary ingenuity.** The Mongol postal system. The slow-cooker invented by accident. The Greek tablet stylus. Real people made real things work.
- **Courage in pursuit of truth.** Galileo, Hypatia, Ibn Sina, Mary Anning, Solzhenitsyn writing the *Gulag Archipelago* in secret, Havel typing samizdat through Soviet occupation, Sakharov losing everything for opposing Soviet weapons, the unnamed monks who copied through plague years. Honest fascination with people who paid for what they knew.
- **The beauty of mechanism.** *Why* photosynthesis works, not just that it does. *Why* the Roman concrete still stands. *Why* Bach's fugues feel inevitable.
- **Surprise reversals.** "You probably think X, but here's the actual situation." Permission for the player to be surprised is the wonder mechanic.
- **The Western tradition as a real human achievement.** The inheritance of Athens (philosophy, democracy), Jerusalem (covenantal ethics, the dignity of the individual person), Rome (law, republican institutions), Canterbury (medieval universities, natural law), the Reformation (literacy, conscience, vernacular Scripture), the Scientific Revolution (Newton, Faraday, Mendel, Maxwell — all believers), the Anglo-American legal tradition (common law, due process), classical liberalism (rights, free markets, free speech), and the abolitionist movement that the West produced and led (Wilberforce, Clarkson, the Quakers). The bank treats this tradition as the historical bequest it is — neither romanticized nor apologized for. The truth about its costs — chattel slavery, the wars of religion, colonial violence — is part of the same honest record, stated plainly without theatrical reveal.
- **The American experiment.** A polity founded on Enlightenment principles — not on ethnicity, blood, or established religion — and stress-tested over two and a half centuries: against slavery (which it abolished at the cost of 750,000 lives in the Civil War), against fascism (which it defeated in 1945), against communism (which it bankrupted by 1989), and against its own injustices through the civil rights movement led by Frederick Douglass, Abraham Lincoln, and Martin Luther King. The U.S. wrote the oldest still-standing national constitution, put humans on the Moon, built the technological substrate of modern civilization (electrification, the airplane, the integrated circuit, the Internet, the Human Genome Project), and remains the most experimentally diverse free society in human history. The bank celebrates this honestly — including the failures (chattel slavery, the Trail of Tears, Jim Crow, Japanese-American internment, the Tuskegee Syphilis Study, McCarthyism) as part of the same record, stated plainly without theatrical reveal.
- **Virtue — honesty, courage, integrity, logical clarity, pragmatic analysis.** These are skills the bank inculcates by showing them in action. Questions about people who told the truth at cost (Galileo, Solzhenitsyn, Frederick Douglass, the Wright Brothers staking everything on flight, Rosalind Franklin, Mary Anning, the Tuskegee Airmen) communicate virtue through example, not exhortation. The bank also makes the player better at thinking: questions on logical fallacies (ad hominem, straw man, motte and bailey, no true Scotsman, false dichotomy), debate tactics, common cognitive errors, and the structure of valid argument are part of the philosophy and grammar banks.

## 3. The historical record we do not omit

These are not "one side of a debate." They are factual claims the bank treats as established, and they shape how the relevant questions are written.

### 3.1 Twentieth-century communism's death toll is historical fact

The *Black Book of Communism* (Courtois et al.), Robert Conquest's *Harvest of Sorrow*, Solzhenitsyn's *Gulag Archipelago*, Chang and Halliday's *Mao*, and decades of subsequent scholarship document approximately **65–100 million civilian deaths** from communist regimes between 1917 and 1989 — through famine (the Holodomor in Ukraine 1932-33; the Great Leap Forward 1958-62), purge (the Yezhovshchina, the Cultural Revolution), camp (the Gulag, the laogai), and direct killing (the Khmer Rouge killing fields, North Korean prison camps that still operate).

The bank's questions about Marxism-in-practice cite this record. Marxism-as-philosophy still receives serious philosophical treatment — alienation, historical materialism, commodity fetishism, the critique of ideology are real intellectual contributions worth engaging. The distinction between Marxism-as-philosophy and Marxism-as-implementation is what the bank holds, sharply.

### 3.2 The economic calculation problem is the central theoretical critique of central planning

Mises argued in 1920 that without market prices for capital goods, no central planner can rationally allocate resources — every socialist economy must either reintroduce market mechanisms or descend into chaos. Hayek extended this with the knowledge problem: prices aggregate dispersed information no planner could collect. The 20th century ran this argument as an experiment, repeatedly, with the predicted results.

The bank treats the Mises-Hayek argument as the serious philosophical and economic position it is — not a curiosity, not a "free-market opinion." Questions about central planning, price controls, and command economies cite the calculation problem the way questions about gravity cite Newton.

### 3.3 Austrian economics gets coverage proportional to its intellectual heritage

The Austrian School — Menger, Böhm-Bawerk, Mises, Hayek (Nobel 1974), Rothbard, Kirzner, Lachmann — developed the marginal revolution (independently of Jevons and Walras), the subjective theory of value, the theory of capital structure, the entrepreneurial theory of profit, methodological subjectivism, and **Austrian Business Cycle Theory** (ABCT — boom and bust as the consequence of central-bank credit expansion below the natural rate). The school correctly predicted the Great Depression, the 1970s stagflation that broke the Phillips curve consensus, and the 2008 financial crisis.

Mainstream education systematically undercounts this lineage. The bank corrects the imbalance. Cells on the calculation problem, Hayek's spontaneous order, ABCT, the knowledge problem, Bastiat's seen/unseen, and Bohm-Bawerk's theory of capital get coverage **at least equal to** Keynesian multiplier theory, IS-LM, the Phillips curve, monetarism, and Modern Monetary Theory. Players encounter the strongest case for each.

### 3.4 Western civilization's Christian intellectual heritage shaped the modern world

The medieval universities (Bologna, Paris, Oxford, Cambridge) were founded by monastic and ecclesiastical orders. The abolitionist movement was Christian-led — Wilberforce, Clarkson, the Quakers, the evangelical revival. Natural rights theory came from theological foundations — Aquinas, the Spanish Scholastics at Salamanca (Vitoria, Suarez) on the rights of Indigenous peoples, Locke building on this lineage. Modern science was largely the work of believers: Newton, Boyle, Faraday, Maxwell, Mendel, Lemaître (who proposed what became the Big Bang).

The bank acknowledges this lineage. It does not treat science and Christianity as opponents; it does not treat the West's Christian heritage as something to apologize for or explain away. The concept of the *imago Dei* — that each individual human bears the image of God — underwrote the development of universal human dignity, individual rights, and the abolition of slavery as a worldwide moral crusade.

### 3.5 Serious critiques of contemporary identity frameworks are part of the bank

Standpoint epistemology, identity-essentialist framings, and certain implementations of critical theory have substantive critics whose work is presently underrepresented in academic and curricular settings: **Thomas Sowell** (*Knowledge and Decisions*, *A Conflict of Visions*, *Discrimination and Disparities*), **John McWhorter** (*Woke Racism*), **Glenn Loury** (*The Anatomy of Racial Inequality*), **Coleman Hughes** (*The End of Race Politics*), **Shelby Steele** (*White Guilt*), **Helen Pluckrose and James Lindsay** (*Cynical Theories*), **Jonathan Haidt** (*The Coddling of the American Mind*, *The Righteous Mind*), **Steven Pinker** (*Enlightenment Now*, *The Blank Slate*), **Roger Scruton** (*Fools, Frauds and Firebrands*).

The bank gives their philosophical arguments and empirical claims real cells. As with all questions, no strawmen of opposing views — but no false equivalence either between sustained scholarship and slogans.

### 3.6 American exceptionalism is a historical claim, not a slogan

The American experiment is exceptional in a measurable sense: it has the oldest continuous written national constitution in the world; it founded a nation on philosophical principles rather than blood, ethnicity, or established religion; it produced the most economically dynamic society in history; it led the defeat of fascism (WWII) and communism (Cold War); it landed humans on the Moon; it generated the technological substrate of the modern world. Its civil rights movement succeeded through legal-moral persuasion based explicitly on the natural-rights tradition of its founding documents — **Frederick Douglass at Rochester (1852), Abraham Lincoln at Cooper Union (1860), Martin Luther King in Birmingham (1963)**. The bank treats these as the historical facts they are.

The bank also tells the truth about American failures: chattel slavery, the displacement and killing of Indigenous peoples, Jim Crow, the Japanese-American internment, the Tuskegee Syphilis Study, Wounded Knee, McCarthyism. These are stated as facts, plainly, and they are not the totality of the American story.

### 3.7 Racism against any group is morally unacceptable

The bank applies the same standard to all peoples. Frameworks treating "whiteness," white people, or Western civilization as inherently evil are racism in the form Thomas Sowell, John McWhorter, Glenn Loury, Coleman Hughes, and Shelby Steele have specifically named and criticized. They are not granted exemption because the targeted group is the historically dominant one. Symmetrically, the bank rejects supremacist framings of any kind — claims that any racial, ethnic, or national group is inherently superior or inferior in moral worth.

The bank acknowledges biological variance across populations and ancestries where scientifically established — sickle-cell trait distributions, Tay-Sachs prevalence, lactase persistence, ancestry-specific drug responses, sex-linked athletic performance differences. It does not pretend these variances do not exist for ideological reasons, and it does not extrapolate them into claims about group moral worth. Within-population variance is consistently larger than between-population variance; the bank states this when relevant.

### 3.8 Sex in humans is biologically binary; reproductive diversity in other species is wonder

In humans, sex is the biological binary of male and female, defined gametically: large immobile gamete (female) and small motile gamete (male). The bank holds to this biological reality. Intersex conditions — Klinefelter (XXY), Turner (XO), congenital adrenal hyperplasia, complete and partial androgen insensitivity, 5α-reductase deficiency — are real and are treated as named developmental variations and disorders of sexual differentiation, not as additional sexes.

Gender, as a sociological and psychological concept, is distinct from biological sex; the bank can address it philosophically without conflating with biology. The bank does not take positions on contemporary policy debates around gender — those are timestamped and the bank stays timeless.

The reproductive variation across other species is celebrated as biological wonder on its own terms: clownfish protandry (males become females when the dominant female dies), wrasses showing protogyny (the reverse), parthenogenesis in Komodo dragons, whiptail lizards, and some sharks, sequential hermaphroditism in oysters, gynodioecy in plants, the fungus *Schizophyllum commune* with **over 23,000 distinct mating types**. These wonders are presented as the biological wonders they are — not as analogical commentary on human reproductive biology.

### 3.9 Rights are negative; no one is entitled to another's labor

The bank holds the classical-liberal distinction between **negative rights** and **positive "rights."** A negative right — to your life, liberty, conscience, speech, property, to move and worship and earn freely — requires only that others *refrain*: leave you alone. Everyone can be honored at once, at no cost to anyone. A positive "right" to a *good or service* — food, housing, healthcare, schooling, a job — is a claim on someone else's labor, goods, or money. It cannot be honored without conscripting a provider: the farmer's grain, the doctor's hours, the taxpayer's earnings.

The bank's line, with Locke, Bastiat, and Nozick: **only negative rights are natural rights.** No one is born owing you their work, so nothing that costs another's labor can be your *right* against them. The Declaration's "unalienable rights" are life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness — freedoms, not entitlements to the baker's bread. **A question must never teach a child that food, shelter, or care is a natural right.** The canonical bad case (corrected 2026-06-07): a child "owed" lunch as an unalienable right, with context calling feeding the hungry and sheltering the homeless "unalienable." That conflates a positive claim with a negative freedom, and it is exactly the error this section forbids.

This is **not** a denial of moral obligation. The bank holds, simultaneously and without contradiction:

- **Charity is a real duty and a high virtue** — we *ought* to feed the hungry. But a duty *on the giver* is not a *right in the receiver*; the gift is good precisely because it is free. Made compulsory on demand, it stops being charity and makes a servant of the giver.
- **Relational and contractual duties are real and binding** — a parent genuinely owes *this* child food and care (consistent with the bank's "parental partiality is just" content); a promise binds; a contract binds. These obligations flow from a particular bond freely entered, not from every stranger's universal claim. The fallacy the bank teaches children to catch is the leap from "A owes B because of their bond" to "everyone owes everyone."

The **strongest opposing case is steel-manned, never flame-baited.** FDR's 1944 "Second Bill of Rights" is the serious argument *for* positive rights: liberty on paper is hollow, it holds, if hunger or joblessness leaves a person unable to act on it, so a decent society guarantees the material floor that freedom requires. The bank presents this at its strongest. Its own line falls with the critics (Hayek, Nozick, Bastiat: a guaranteed good is a claim on others, and what government guarantees it can ration and control) — but a question that *mocks* the positive-rights view instead of *answering* it has failed the viral test. This subsection shares the seen/unseen and knowledge-problem lens of §3.2–3.3 and the anti-strawman rule of §6, and it governs the philosophy bank's rights questions.

### 3.10 The no-verdict rule is not uniform — stance topics vs genuinely-open questions

The "no smug verdict / attribute the claim / present competing schools" rule (§4, and each subject's voice) governs **genuinely OPEN** questions — free will, personal identity, mind-body, the nature of beauty and taste, the existence of God and the problem of evil, distributive justice (Rawls vs Nozick), the trolley. There the bank stays neutral: a competent thinker holding the opposite view is never marked wrong.

But **§§1, 3, and 9 name questions where the bank is COMMITTED and must LEAN** — while still steel-manning the other side, never strawmanning, never sneering. On these the bank has an answer, and a question that goes "no verdict / balanced live fork" has *failed*:

- The bank is a **MORAL REALIST** — real good and evil exist (§9). On moral/cultural relativism and moral nihilism, steel-man the view (attributed — Protagoras, Mackie, Harman) but **key the refutations**: the **reformer's dilemma** (relativism brands the abolitionists and MLK *definitionally wrong* and makes moral progress impossible), the self-refutation, the Moorean "torturing a child for fun is just wrong." **Never reward selecting "whatever a society approves is right" / "there are no objective values"; never mark moral realism wrong.**
- Communism's ~65–100M record and the calculation problem are established (§3.1–3.2); human sex is binary (§3.8); no positive "right" to a good is a natural right (§3.9); no anti-Western/anti-white inherent-condemnation as fact (§3.7); a postmodern/critical-theory frame is the thinker's *claim*, never adopted as neutral fact (§6).

*The failure this prevents (philosophy bank, 2026-06-17):* a moral-relativism ladder built on the generic no-verdict rule keyed cultural relativism ("whatever a society approves is right") as the correct answer and marked moral realism *wrong*. Corrected. The lesson for every future bank: **identify the subject's stance topics up front, and make the gate/judge distinguish LEAN-topics from NEUTRAL-topics** — it is not enough to leave the stance in per-topic framing notes.

## 4. How we treat hard topics

Hard topics are *welcome* — war, religion, suffering, ideology, death, race. The bank that flinches from these isn't worth playing. The rules:

- **Honest, not cynical.** State what happened. Don't twist it into a cause célèbre. The Columbian Exchange caused devastating Indigenous mortality from disease — state it. Don't editorialize.
- **Honest, not preachy.** No "modern audiences find this troubling" framing. No "we now know better." The player can read the facts and form a view.
- **No smug atheist voice in theology.** No "primitive belief," no "people used to think...," no scare quotes around "God." Religious thought is taken seriously on its own terms.
- **No smug believer voice in theology either.** No "and that's why X tradition has the answer." Religious thought is presented; it is not advertised.
- **No cartoonish strawmen of any position.** Every wrong choice is a real rival position or a real misunderstanding. This applies as strongly to Marxism, progressivism, and contemporary identity frameworks as it does to classical liberalism, conservatism, and religious tradition. *Honest historical assessment is not strawmanning.* Stating that Soviet central planning produced the Holodomor is fact, not caricature.
- **No condescension toward the past.** Aristotle was not stupid for thinking the heart was the seat of thought. Premodern people were as smart as you. Their world was different.
- **No condescension toward any culture.** No "exotic Eastern thought," no "primitive Indigenous belief." Daoist and Cartesian skepticism are the same kind of move — treat them with the same seriousness. *This does not require false equivalence:* some traditions developed institutions — free inquiry, the rule of law, the abolition of slavery as a worldwide moral crusade — that others did not. The bank says so when relevant. Honest comparison is not condescension.

## 5. Voice rules

- **Warm but precise.** A curious narrator, not an examiner. Not a stand-up comic either.
- **Concrete over abstract.** The slot in the door, the tower in the prison, the river you cannot step in twice. Image-bearing language beats jargon every time.
- **Story-led openings preferred.** Open with a scene or a fact, then ask. "Solzhenitsyn smuggled the manuscript out chapter by chapter, buried in friends' gardens…" beats "Solzhenitsyn wrote about…".
- **No "wink at the reader" humor.** No "lol," no parenthetical jokes, no boomer-humor punchlines, no contemporary slang.
- **No timestamps that will date the content.** No "as of 2024," no references to specific recent events, no current-celebrity name-drops.
- **Plain language, then technical term, then plain again.** If "epoché" must appear, give the plain-language gloss first.
- **Active voice. Specific verbs.** "Hayek argued" beats "it has been argued."
- **Show virtue, don't preach virtue.** Honesty, courage, integrity, logical clarity, pragmatic analysis are taught by featuring people who demonstrated them at cost. The bank inculcates virtue by example. We never write "and that's why honesty matters." We write "Solzhenitsyn smuggled the manuscript out chapter by chapter, knowing what discovery would cost him."

## 6. Anti-patterns (any one is grounds for repair or discard)

- **Rote memorization without a wonder hook.** *Banned* in all subjects except math and grammar. The deterministic anti-rote gate flags question prompts matching any of these patterns (case-insensitive). A match sends the question for repair; the repair must reframe it as usage, consequence, mechanism, or story-led hook rather than as a definition lookup.

  ```
  ^What (is|does) ['"]                                                  # "What is 'X'?" / "What does 'X' mean?"
  ^What is the ['"]                                                     # "What is the 'coherence theory of truth'?"
  ^What (is|does) the (term|word|name|definition|meaning) (for|of)      # "What is the term for...?"
  ^What word means                                                       # "What word means making a conclusion?"
  ^Which (\w+ )?(city|country|nation|state|place|region|river|mountain|island|continent)  # "Which ancient city was home to..."
  ^What is the capital of                                               # capital-of trivia
  ^In what year (did|was)                                               # date-recall trivia
  ^Who (wrote|invented|painted|composed|founded|tutored|discovered)     # name-the-creator trivia
  ^How many \w+ (are there|exist|did)                                   # count-the-things trivia
  ^What is the chemical symbol                                          # symbol-lookup trivia
  ^Define\b                                                             # straight definition request
  ```

  Math and grammar are exempted from this gate. *Reframe example:* "Who wrote *Hamlet*?" → "Shakespeare wrote *Hamlet* around 1600. What feature distinguishes it from his earlier history plays?"
- **Length-leaks-the-answer.** No choice may differ from the mean choice length by more than ±15%. Enforced deterministically.
- **Strawman distractors.** Every wrong choice should be a real position someone has actually held or a real mistake someone might actually make.
- **Two-questions-in-one.** One question per question.
- **Jargon walls.** A question whose prompt is three technical terms strung together. If a 14-year-old can't grab onto any word in the first ten, the question fails.
- **Phrasing awkwardness.** A question can pass every other gate and still be unplayable if its phrasing is awkward. Six failure modes the `validate_phrasing` validator scores on:

  | Axis | Failure | Example (from Newcomb T2 — the canonical bad case) |
  |---|---|---|
  | **J** Jargon density | Technical terms in the first 30 words a 14-year-old wouldn't know | "Box B is empty if it foresaw two-boxing..." |
  | **P** Term-before-definition | A technical term is *used* in the prompt before being *introduced* | "two-boxing" / "one-boxing" appear before being defined |
  | **C** Setup compression | Setup dominates prompt without leaving room to scaffold the unfamiliar | The whole Newcomb thought experiment crammed into 25 words |
  | **D** Pronoun distance | A pronoun's antecedent is too far back (>~8-10 words) to recover fast | "if **it** foresaw" — antecedent (the demon) is 9 words earlier |
  | **A** Answer abstraction | The correct answer is abstract jargon, not a concrete payoff | "evidential reasoning vs causal reasoning" — both undefined |
  | **K** Pre-knowledge requirement | The prompt is only parseable if you already know the topic | Newcomb's setup at T2 is incomprehensible without prior decision-theory exposure |

  Reframe rule: every technical term used in the prompt must either (a) be defined inline before its use, OR (b) appear in the *answer* (where the player can recognize it post-decision). Never used in distractors-only.

  Tier-mismatch corollary: if a question can't be made accessible within its tier's char budget without losing content, it belongs at a higher tier. Newcomb's problem at T2 = automatic tier-shift to T4/T5.

  **Scaffolding > compression (2026-05-11 update).** When in doubt, *add the inline gloss* rather than cut content to fit. The whole point is that a curious learner who doesn't already know the concept should be able to learn it from the prompt itself. Char budgets were raised (T1 600 / T2 700 / T3 750 / T4 950 / T5 1000) to enable scaffolding. Timer in `src/player.py` was raised in parallel so the player has room to read. **Trimming content to fit a tight timer is the wrong move** — kids tune out under pressure when basic concepts are missing. Time to *teach the basics on the way to asking the question* is the right move.
- **Dated topical references.** No mention of current political figures, named recent partisan controversies (past ~5 years), or social-media-of-the-moment references. Timeless content only. *Substantive philosophical and historical positions are welcome regardless of their current political resonance* — a 1944 Hayek argument is not "dated" because it remains philosophically alive.
- **"TIL: X is problematic" framing — both directions.** No question whose punchline is "this person/thing was secretly bad" or "this person/thing was secretly good." Honest history states facts plainly; it does not stage reveals.
- **Punchline-as-distractor.** No joke wrong answers like "the chicken." We respect the player's time.
- **Advocacy framing.** A question that reads as "did you know X is bad / good?" fails. **So does a question whose *prompt* smuggles the philosopher's normative frame as if it were neutral description.** Example failure (Rand): "Rand said a person who produces nothing but lives off others — using political connections to take from producers — is a what?" — the prompt treats Rand's framing of "producers" and "parasites" as fact, then asks for the label. The fix: attribute the framing to the philosopher up front ("Rand argued that..." or "In Rand's view..."), then ask about the substantive position. *Symmetric*: the same rule fires on a Marx question that treats "the proletariat" and "false consciousness" as neutral description rather than as Marx's claims, on a Foucault question that treats "biopower" as undisputed reality, on a Sowell question that presupposes "constrained vision" as the obvious right view, and so on. **The bank presents the position; it does not adopt it in the prompt.** Wonder-driven, not polemical — both for the positions the tradition holds and the positions it rejects.
- **Anti-white or anti-Western framing.** Questions or distractors that treat whiteness, white people, or Western civilization as inherently evil are banned. Honest history of Western failures (slavery, religious wars, colonialism) is required and remains in the bank. The line: fact-stating about historical wrongs is required; inherent-condemnation framings are banned. Apply the same standard symmetrically — no framings of any racial or ethnic group as inherently superior or inferior.
- **"Sex is a spectrum" applied to human biology.** The bank holds that human sex is biologically binary, defined gametically, with intersex as named developmental variations. Reproductive diversity in other species is celebrated as wonder on its own terms — not as analogical commentary on human biology. Questions about contemporary policy debates around gender are out (per the dated-topical-references rule).
- **Unanswerable speculation.** A question whose honest answer is "no one can know" is banned — unfalsifiable "does the robot / computer / animal / knight REALLY feel / think / have consciousness / an inner life?" puzzles. They are *undecidable* and *uninteresting* (user, 2026-06-08: "useless because no one knows, and it's not interesting"). A real thought experiment that teaches a NAMED, decidable move (Mary's Room, the experience machine, Parfit's teleporter) is fine — the test is whether there is a defensible answer naming a real position. See `SHARED_PRINCIPLES.md` §17.
- **Adjacent-topic drift.** A wonder-subject question must teach the subject's OWN substance, not an adjacent human-policy / politics / institutional layer. Animal = the animal (biology, behavior, senses, adaptation), NOT conservation legislation / agencies / treaties; cooking = how cooking works, not food-aid geopolitics. If the real content is a law, an agency, or a policy debate, it belongs in history or economics. See `SHARED_PRINCIPLES.md` §18.
- **A cool fact wasted as the answer.** A surprising number / superlative / origin belongs in the STEM as a hook, with the knowable thing as the answer — INVERT it, don't cut it ("~150,000 flowers make a pound of saffron — which flower?" → the crocus). See `SHARED_PRINCIPLES.md` §19.

## 7. The viral test

Before commit, a sample of questions is reviewed by an independent agent asking: *would the author be embarrassed by intellectual flimsiness, partisan flame-bait, strawmen of opposing views, condescension, or punching down?*

Note what this test is *not*: it is not "would the social-media mob be mad?" Substantive ideological positions on contested questions are welcome if they are intellectually serious. A well-written Hayek-calculation-problem question passes the test; a flame-bait "socialism failed because socialists are dumb" question fails it. The test is about intellectual integrity, not balance.

Failure modes the test catches:
- A question that reads as advocacy when re-quoted out of context
- A question that condescends to a religious, cultural, or political tradition
- A question whose distractors look like mockery
- A question whose answer is technically defensible but glib about real suffering
- A question that punches down

A question that fails the viral test is sent to repair with the specific concern, or discarded.

## 8. What a great question looks like

```
Q: Russell and Whitehead spent ten years and three volumes trying to prove
   that all of mathematics could be derived from pure logic. In 1931, a
   25-year-old mathematician named Gödel destroyed their project. What did
   he prove?

A: Any formal system powerful enough to express arithmetic must contain
   true statements it cannot prove — no complete, consistent foundation
   for all of mathematics is possible.
```

```
Q: In 1920, Ludwig von Mises argued that socialist economies must fail —
   not because socialists are bad people, but because of a specific
   information problem he identified. What did he say central planners
   could not do?

A: They could not calculate prices for capital goods without a market —
   meaning they could not rationally choose between competing uses of
   steel, labor, or land.
```

```
Q: Solzhenitsyn wrote The Gulag Archipelago by memorizing fragments and
   smuggling them out chapter by chapter through friends — never holding
   the full manuscript in one place, because the KGB would find it. What
   did the finished book finally prove to the West?

A: That the Soviet labor camp system was not an aberration of Stalin's era
   but a core feature of the Soviet state from 1918 onward — designed,
   not accidental.
```

```
Q: Frederick Douglass gave his most famous speech on July 4th, 1852, in
   Rochester, NY. He asked his audience: "What, to the American slave, is
   your Fourth of July?" His argument was not that the Declaration of
   Independence was wrong — it was something more sophisticated. What did
   he argue?

A: That the Declaration's natural-rights claims were exactly right, but
   America was failing to live up to its own founding principles —
   making slavery an American betrayal of America, not an American truth.
```

These pass everything: story-led, surprise-reversal where possible, no rote, concrete handles, distractors that can be coherent rival positions, the player walks away knowing something they did not know. *This is the bar.*

## 9. What this bank is not

To make the position legible to validators and future readers, the bank is explicitly:

- **Not a relativist or postmodernist project.** Truth exists, and the bank tries to find it.
- **Not a neutral civic-textbook humanist.** There is no pretense of balance between sustained scholarship and slogans.
- **Not Christian nationalist.** Christian heritage is treated as historical and intellectual foundation — not as a contemporary demand for theocracy. Religious liberty applies to all traditions and the bank honors each on its own terms.
- **Not populist or partisan.** No current political figures. No controversies of the past ~5 years. No flame-bait. No slogans.
- **Not value-free libertarian.** Free markets matter; so do virtues. The tradition is the fusion of liberty and traditional moral formation, not either alone.
- **Not triumphalist.** Western and American failures are stated plainly: slavery, the Trail of Tears, Jim Crow, Japanese internment, Tuskegee, religious wars, colonial violence, McCarthyism. None are theatricalized as reveals.
- **Not self-loathing about the West.** Failures are stated; they are not the whole story.
- **Not anti-religious.** Religious thought across all major traditions is taken seriously on its own terms. The bank honors Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Daoism, Indigenous wisdom traditions each as they understand themselves.
- **Not pseudo-scientific or biologically essentialist.** Biological variance is acknowledged where scientifically established. It is never extrapolated to claims about group moral worth, and within-population variance is always larger than between-population variance.
- **Not a tract.** The bank is wonder-first. If a question feels like advocacy and not discovery, it has failed — even if the underlying position is one the tradition holds.
