## Systematic patterns

### Working extraordinarily well
1. **Defensive recognition** consistently concrete, kid-actionable, world-grounded
2. **Named figures with arcs** (Hassabis, Lee Sedol, Hinton, Haugen, SBF, Sewell Setzer) work brilliantly
3. **Real-world hooks** kids actually encounter (TikTok, Siri, school AI surveillance, Duolingo)
4. **Recognition Pattern hierarchy** well-distributed
5. **Wonder anchors** (Move 37, AlphaFold 200M proteins, voice clone scams) deliver
6. **Counter-alarmism** working — kids learn mechanism + skill, not panic

### Watch list
1. A handful of T1 questions remain rote (years of founding) — could add narrative
2. T4–T5 occasionally uses adult/corporate language ("structural recognition", "vendor lock-in")
3. "X et al" citations at T4 (Zou et al CMU) — could trim to first author + year
4. A few T5 questions assume researcher-name knowledge without context (Gebru's DAIR)
5. T5 election-integrity + compute-concentration are notably abstract relative to peers

### Nothing I'd call broken
All 50 samples were genuinely teaching something. No fabricated facts spotted. No anthropomorphizing. No false-balance framings detected.

## My provisional verdict
**Ship-quality.** Better than my expectation going in. The Recognition Pattern is doing the work it should. Two systematic improvements would lift the bank from "good" to "great":
1. Rewrite ~15-20 T1 pure-recall items as named-event stories (Hassabis template)
2. Lighten the ~10-15 most abstractly-framed T4–T5 power questions with a concrete hook in the first sentence

Even without those, the bank delivers on the user's brief: kids learn what AI IS, how it works, where the power plays are, and how to defend against the scams. Wonder is present at every tier. Mystery (open problems, unsolved tradeoffs) is honored, not pretended-away.
